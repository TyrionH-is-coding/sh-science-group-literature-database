# Thrombin generation test as a useful tool for improving disease severity stratification in antiphospholipid syndrome

## Abstract

Background: Patients with antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) display a wide range of clinical manifestations with similar immunological profile with the presence of lupus anticoagulant in around 70% of them. Although antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL) profile influences the risk of thrombosis in APS, APS risk stratification remains to be improved.

Objectives: Our study aimed to evaluate thrombin generation test (TGT) interest to improve disease severity stratification in APS patients.

Patients/methods: In this monocentric, transversal study, we included unselected primary thrombotic APS patients, fulfilling APS classification criteria and treated by vitamin K antagonist (VKA). 28 non-APS patients under VKA followed in our hospital were included as controls. TGT was performed on plasma samples with the calibrated automated thrombogram in our core laboratory.

Results: Between January 2020 and March 2023, 114 thrombotic APS patients (without systemic lupus erythematosus) were included (female 71 (62.3%), mean age 50±14 years old, median duration since the last APS event of 51 months (IQR 23–129 months)). APS patients had prolonged lag time (LT) and time to peak (TTP) compared with anticoagulated controls. History of relapse, catastrophic APS (CAPS), all aPL positivities and elevated Bb fragments were strongly associated with a prolonged LT and TTP.

Conclusions: Our findings suggest that APS patients under VKA had significantly prolonged TGT times compared with anticoagulated controls, with the greatest prolongations in patients with history of relapse or CAPS. Thus, TGT might be a useful tool to improve APS disease severity stratification without temporary cessation of VKA.

## Introduction

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a vascular autoimmune disease characterised by the persistence of antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL), including anticardiolipin antibodies (aCL), anti-β2-glycoprotein I (aβ 2 GPI) and lupus anticoagulant (LA). APS is a highly heterogeneous disease characterised by the occurrence of thrombosis that might affect all types of vascular beds. Its severity also varies from isolated deep vein thrombosis to life-threatening diffuse activation of the coagulation with multiple organ failures called catastrophic APS (CAPS). It has been widely reported that the aPL profile greatly influences the risk of thrombosis and adverse pregnancy outcomes (APO), with the highest risk for LA and triple aPL positivity. 1 4 However, not all patients tested positive for aPL will develop clinical manifestations. Current risk-stratification tools, such as the Adjusted Global Anti-Phospholipid Syndrome Score (aGAPSS), are largely limited to the aPL profile and traditional thrombotic risk factors. 5 Novel advanced functional assays are needed to test the function of aPL and to improve risk stratification and prediction in APS. 6

Prothrombotic effect of aPL, via interference with natural anticoagulant proteins regulating coagulation and fibrinolysis (especially protein C and antithrombin) or platelet activation, was the first pathophysiological mechanism identified in APS that led to a large prescription of lifelong anticoagulant therapy in all thrombotic APS patients. 7 Despite progress in the understanding of APS pathogenesis, it remains difficult to identify patients at risk of APS severe manifestations (CAPS, recurrent APO or thromboses). Moreover, the laboratory tests for the detection of aPL show methodological challenges: LA detection is based on clotting-based assays with very heterogeneous reactivity for the detection of LA that might be modified by the anticoagulant therapy, and solid-phase immunoassays for aCL and aβ 2 GPI still lack standardisation. Thrombin generation test (TGT) measures the dynamic process of thrombin generated over time and offers a global assessment of the coagulation to evaluate the hypercoagulable state in patients with various thrombophilia. 8 The assay allows obtaining the thrombin generation curve (thrombogram)( figure 1 ). TG parameters of interest included the lag time (LT), thrombin peak height (PH), time to peak (TTP) and area under the curve or endogenous thrombin potential (ETP). Only a few studies investigated whether TGT could be used as a diagnostic tool for APS diagnosis or for thrombotic risk assessment, and they had significant methodological limitations. 9 Most studies used TGT to evaluate the resistance to activated protein C (aPC) in APS patients. Devreese et al demonstrated that LA influenced the TG profile directly, and that the TG profile might be used to discriminate LA-positive patients with or without a history of thrombosis. 10 11 A recent study shows that TGT can be performed in thrombotic patients under vitamin K antagonist (VKA) and is useful for APS diagnosis in combination with an artificial neural network. 12 However, those results were not independently confirmed, and there is still a lack of evidence whether TG parameters correlate with poor thrombotic outcomes in APS.

**Fig:** Figure 1 Thrombin generation test. This figure illustrates key parameters of the thrombin generation test, reflecting coagulation dynamics: Lag time (LT): delay before thrombin generation begins, indicating the initiation phase of coagulation cascade; time to peak (TTP): time to reach maximum thrombin concentration, marking the amplification phase; peak or peak height: maximum thrombin concentration generated, influenced by procoagulant and anticoagulant factors; endogenous thrombin potential (ETP): total thrombin generated over time, representing overall coagulation potential and regulation.

Beyond aPL profile, very few other prognostic biomarkers were identified in APS. Animal models demonstrated the critical role of complement activation to induce aPL-related pregnancy losses and thromboses, and it has been recently reported that increased circulating complement activation fragments were associated with severe features of the disease (CAPS, microvascular thrombosis, recurrent thromboses and triple aPL positivity). 13 15 The complement system plays a major role at the intersection of the immune and haemostatic systems, 16 and it would be of interest to study the correlation between biomarkers of complement activation and markers of coagulation activation to predict severe manifestations of APS.

Our study aimed to evaluate TGT to stratify APS disease severity, in an unselected population of thrombotic primary APS patients outside acute thrombosis. Second, we aimed to evaluate the correlation between TGT parameters and complement split products levels, identified as potential markers of severe disease.

## Patients and methods

### Study design

We conducted a monocentric, cross-sectional study based on our local ongoing APS cohort open since 2020 (BIO-APL). All unselected consecutive thrombotic APS patients who were referred at the outpatient clinic of our tertiary reference centre for rare autoimmune diseases (Lille University Hospital) are proposed to participate in BIO-APL with clinical data and blood sample collection after each routine care visit.

### Study population

In this study, we included all consecutive thrombotic APS patients who met the following inclusion criteria and had available samples in the repository for TGT: (1) thrombotic APS diagnosis according to international criteria, 17 (2) patient aged ≥18 years, (3) under long-term VKA, (4) no acute thrombotic event or new non-criteria APS manifestation for at least 3 months before inclusion and (5) no evolutive infection, pregnancy, neoplasia or hospitalisation for other cause at the time of sample collection. Patients with associated systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) were excluded (n=11 during the study recruitment period). The patients’ physicians made all treatment decisions.

As a reference group, we also included all consecutive patients with stabilised renal function, who were treated with VKA and referred to the outpatient clinic of our nephrology department for routine follow-up with citrate samples sent to the core lab, following the same pre-analytical procedure as APS patients’ samples. Recruitment was stopped once the targeted number of 28 controls was reached.

### Data and samples collection

Medical history and physical examination data were collected using a digital standardised case-report form. In addition to demographical data, we collected data on APS-related history including criteria (thrombosis, obstetrical event and CAPS) and non-criteria aPL-manifestations (livedo, thrombocytopaenia, aPL-nephropathy, valve disease and chorea/seizure), associated SLE, hypertension (defined by self-report and/or anti-hypertensive drug use), diabetes (defined by self-report and/or glucose lowering drug use), dyslipidaemia (defined by self-report and/or lipid lowering drug use), current smoking, height, weight, body mass index (BMI) and current medications (antithrombotic therapies, immunosuppressive drugs and lipid-lowering therapies). aGAPSS was calculated as previously published. 18

APS relapse was defined as any new macrovascular or microvascular thrombosis and/or CAPS and/or pregnancy morbidity such as described in the classification criteria that occurred after APS diagnosis.

Blood samples were taken by vein puncture in each patient at inclusion and were timely and locally stored in our APS repository for all biological analyses. Plasma was obtained by double centrifugation according to current LA guidelines and kept frozen at −80°C until tested. 19

### APL assays

LA, aCL and aβ 2 GPI assays were performed following the international guidelines for APS laboratory criteria in our core laboratory. 19 21

Briefly, LA detection was based on a three-step procedure including a screening step, a mixing step and a confirmation step as recommended using the diluted Russell viper venom time (dRVVT, HYPHEN BioMed group) and the silica clotting time (SCT; Instrumentation Laboratory group). The result of each test is expressed as a normalised ratio and interpreted according to the local cut-off values (dRVVT cut-off value=1.20, SCT cut-off value=1.20). aCL and aβ 2 GPI IgG/M assays were performed using commercial ELISA kits (Orgentec by Sebia, Mainz, Germany). We defined aCL and aβ 2 GPI IgG/M as ‘positive’ when the IgG/M titers measured were ≥40 units.

### Thrombin generation test

TGT was performed with Calibrated Automated Thrombogram (CAT: Stago, Asnieres-sur-Seine, France) in 1:1 mixtures of patient plasma and pool normal plasma (cryocheck, Precision Biologic, Canada) (PNP) to correct for reduced coagulation factor activity as a result of VKA treatment in the presence of 4 µM phospholipids and 5 pM tissue factor (PPP reagent, Stago), as recommended. 22 In these conditions, TG depends to a small degree on FVIII and FIX but not on FXI and is phospholipid-controlled. 23 Experiments were done in duplicate, and the results were considered valid if the coefficient of variation between duplicates was below 10%; otherwise, the TGT was redone. TG-parameters recorded included LT, ETP, thrombin PH and TTP. All results were normalised (n) using PNP as a reference, by division of the results for the 1:1 plasma mixtures and those measured for the PNP of the same run (respectively, nLT, nETP, nPH and nTTP).

### Complement split products assays

Plasma samples were used to detect complement activation products levels of sC5b-9, C3a, Bb fragment by using commercial ELISA assays (MicroVue EIA kits, Quidel, San Diego, California, USA). Normal range values were available from the manufacturer for sC5b-9 levels (≤219 ng/mL) and Bb fragment levels (≤1.42 µg/mL), but not C3a. The intra-assay coefficients of variation ranged from 2.4% to 6.8% and the interassay coefficients of variation from 4.4% to 14.7%.

### Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables are expressed as mean (±SD) or median (IQR), according to normality of distribution, and categorical variables as a number (percentage). Normality of distributions was assessed using histograms and the Shapiro-Wilk test. Comparison in TG parameters between APS patients and controls was done using Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test according to normality of distribution. The magnitude of between-group differences was assessed by calculating the standardised difference (calculated on rank-transformed data for TG parameters with non-Gaussian distribution); standardised differences were interpreted as small, medium or large effect sizes for absolute values of 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8, respectively. 23 In APS patients, we assessed the association of significant TG parameters (nLT and nTTP) with the clinical manifestation of APS (including the aGAPSS risk score) after adjustment for prespecified confounders (age, BMI and time from last APS event to inclusion) using partial Spearman’s rank correlation analysis for aGAPSS risk score or non-parametric analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) in otherwise. We also investigated the association of both TG parameters with complement activation products (analysed as quantitative or binary variables according to established cut-off values) after prespecified adjustment using partial Spearman’s rank correlation analysis for quantitative complement activation markers and using ANCOVA for binary complement activation markers. Effect sizes with their 95% CIs were reported as partial correlation coefficient (r) for Spearman’s rank correlation analysis (using a Fisher’s z transformation to estimate the 95% CIs) or adjusted standardised differences derived from ANCOVA models. Finally, among APS patients, we assessed the association of both TG parameters with severe manifestation of APS (defined as relapse and CAPS) using a logistic regression model before and after adjustment for prespecified confounders and the LA status. The log-linearity assumption was assessed using restricted cubic spline functions, and a threshold assumption was found after applying a log transformation. Adjusted OR per one 0.1 log-increase in TG parameters (thresholded at 1.1 for nLT and 0.7 for nTTP) was reported as effect size. Data were analysed using the SAS software V.9.4 (SAS Institute).

## Results

### Characteristics of the study population

Between January 2020 and March 2023, 114 APS patients were included (female 71 (62.3%), mean age 50.1±13.6 years old), with a median duration since the last APS event of 51 months (IQ, 23–124 months). Among them, 37 (32.5%) had a history of thrombotic relapse, and 10 (8.8%) patients had a history of CAPS ( table 1 ).

## Discussion

Our study confirmed, in a large cohort of thrombotic APS patients without SLE, outside acute thrombosis or pregnancy, that APS patients had a modified TGT profile compared with controls under VKA, characterised by a prolonged LT and TTP. We also interestingly demonstrated that those TGT modifications were associated with the most severe features of the disease: high-risk aPL profile (LA, triple positivity), CAPS and thrombotic relapse. We also originally reported an association between these TG profile changes and increased levels of Bb fragments, a marker of alternative complement pathway activation.

TGT is a validated global assessment of the thrombin generated that allows evaluation of procoagulant state in various conditions, including hereditary thrombophilia or neoplasia. After a first episode of unprovoked venous thromboembolism (aPL-positive patients excluded), a higher thrombin PH and ETP were significantly associated with a higher risk of thrombotic relapse after anticoagulation withdrawal. 24 25 But these studies lack standardisation. Few studies (n=14) reported data on TG parameters in APS patients, including less than 51 APS patients each, except one study which included 106 APS patients. 9 Most of them (10/14) used TGT to demonstrate the APC resistance induced by aPL compared with healthy or thrombotic controls. 26 31 Others reports demonstrated, consistently with our results, that APS patients exhibited a prolonged LT compared with non-APS controls. 10 32 They also reported a reduced ETP and thrombin PH, which we did not report here, maybe due to our controls' characteristics (non-APS thrombotic patients under VKA). Notably, those aPL effects on TGT parameters preclude the use of TGT to compare the procoagulant state between patients with and without aPL. The stronger association between those TGT parameters changes was found with LA positivity, as also observed in our study where the highest standardised difference was obtained in LA positive vs negative APS patients. 32 Devreese et al proposed to associate the most informative parameters in a PH/LT ratio that strongly correlated with normalised dilute prothrombin time, dRVVT and SCT (correlation coefficients 0.59–0.72) in n=48 non-anticoagulated, and n=12 anticoagulated APS. 10 We report here similar findings. They also reported, in a series of 59 LA-positive patients with or without history of thrombosis, that the strongest reduced PH/LT ratio was found in patients with history of thrombosis, suggesting that this ratio can be used for quantification of LA activity. 11 As PH was not modified by APS in our study compared with other patients under VKA, we evaluated the clinical association with nLT and nTTP only. Our findings added new evidence that TGT might be useful for disease severity stratification in APS, as it was strongly associated with history of poor APS outcomes: APS relapse, CAPS, high aGAPSS and high-risk aPL profile.

Moreover, nLT was also associated with thrombocytopaenia, a manifestation found in high-risk patients. 33 Although critical for patient risk stratification, the aPL profile itself did not allow a good prediction of poor outcomes in all patients. For example, triple aPL positivity is more frequently found in patients with a history of CAPS (around 60% of patients), but triple aPL positivity affected around 40% of global APS patients, whereas only 1%–2% of them will develop a CAPS. 34 36 Additional relevant biomarkers are needed to improve APS risk and severity stratification. Our study adds evidence of the interest of TGT parameters. It is important to note that our study did not allow us to evaluate its predictive value. We cannot conclude if the TGT parameter changes are due to pre-existing abnormalities or the consequences of endothelial injuries over time. Only a prospective follow-up of our patients might allow us to answer the question.

Anticoagulant therapy modifies the TGT profile and might interfere with TGT interpretation. However, previous studies demonstrated that the VKA effect might be antagonised by the addition of PNP to the patient’s plasma in a 1:1 ratio, as we did in our study. Indeed, Devreese et al demonstrated that the PH/LT ratio was highly sensitive to detect LA, irrespective of the patient’s treatment with VKA. 10 Efthymiou et al also demonstrated that, after mixing patients’ plasma with PNP, the ETP and PH changes due to VKA (inverse correlation with INR) disappeared in the non-APS patients (n=50), but not in APS patients (n=50). 32 Those results demonstrated that the VKA effect might be reversed by the adjunction of PNP without abolishing aPL effects on TG-parameters and argued into the interpretability of our results in our population of patients mainly under long-term VKA. It might be useful in clinical practice as it is not mandatory to discontinue anticoagulant therapy to perform TGT in APS patients, who are at high risk of relapse after anticoagulation withdrawal. Moreover, subject to following ISTH guidelines, TGT is a far more standardised assay than assays used for LA detection, with only one choice of laboratory reagent to perform the assay, and an automatised procedure which is not time consuming. However, TGT is more complex to perform and analyse than conventional coagulation tests and should not be used as an alternative screening method for APS diagnosis in routine clinical practice, but reserved for specialised teams. Since APS is a rare disease that requires the expertise of reference centres for rare diseases, these limitations are less of an issue in such settings.

In our study, we also reported a correlation between complement biomarkers and TG profile in APS. When complement biomarkers were expressed as normal or above the cut-off value, only elevated Bb remained significantly associated with both prolonged nLT and nTTP. We previously reported, in a cohort of 98 quiescent APS patients, that Bb fragment was the sole complement split product associated with marker of high-risk profile in APS (triple aPL positivity). 16 Our present findings provide additional data on the interest of Bb level determination, over other complement biomarkers, in stable APS patients to evaluate their risk profile. Bb is a split product of the alternative complement pathway activation. Our results raise the question of whether this imbalance in alternative complement pathway regulation is a consequence of endothelial dysfunction induced by aPL and previous thromboses or a pre-existing susceptibility, as suggested by Chaturvedi et al who identified a higher rate of mutation in complement regulation genes in n=10 CAPS patients, similarly to what was observed in aHUS patients. 37 Prospective studies are now needed to evaluate the predictive value of elevated Bb associated with TG parameters and if they allow appropriately selecting patients who might benefit the most from complement inhibitors (such as eculizumab).

Limitations of our study include the lack of serial samples to draw solid conclusions regarding the stability over time of TG parameter values. As with other aPL assays, TGT is sensitive to preanalytical and analytical conditions, such as sample preparation, the use of phospholipid-containing reagents, and anticoagulant therapies, all of which may influence TGT variability and interpretation. To enhance reproducibility and mitigate variability, we employed a core laboratory with standardised methods for plasma preparation and storage, an automated procedure for TGT assessment, the use of a mixture of patient’s plasma and control plasma pools to neutralise the effects of VKAs, the normalisation of TGT parameters using normal control plasma measured within the same run, and experiments were performed in duplicate, all aimed at further reducing intra-assay and interassay variability. We did not perform a formal sample size calculation, and with our convenience sample, we could not exclude that some associations were overlooked. In a posteriori power calculation, our sample size allows for an effect size (standardised difference) of 0.59 (interpreted as medium effect size) between APS patients and controls with an 80% power by a two-sided test at 0.05 significance level. In other hand, due to the multiple comparison issue, we could not also exclude false positive associations. Our cross-sectional design did not allow us to evaluate the predictive value of TG parameter changes for severe APS manifestations occurrence. We cannot conclude if the TGT parameter changes are due to pre-existing abnormalities or the consequences of endothelial injuries over time. Only a prospective follow-up of our patients might allow us to answer the question. All previous studies were also retrospective; thus, new studies are needed to address this question. The Sapporo criteria were applied to diagnose APS, as the new criteria had not yet been published at the time of patient recruitment. However, we considered the 40 IU threshold for IgG or IgM anti-β2GPI and aCL titers, which have been widely reported to better identify clinically significant aPL, rather than the 99th percentile. Lastly, the selection of a non-APS control group under VKA was challenging, as direct oral anticoagulants are the primary anticoagulants used today, but unsuitable for APS. This explains why we chose to include patients from the nephrology clinic, in whom VKAs are still widely used. Controls were comparable in terms of preanalytical sample preparation and anticoagulation therapy, the most important parameters that could influence comparisons between groups. However, controls were slightly older and had a higher number of cardiovascular disease risk factors than the APS patients. We do not believe these differences impacted our findings, as these parameters do not artificially influence TGT, and the differences favour the control group rather than the APS patients.

Our study has many strengths, including its large sample size compared with previous studies on TGT in APS, the use of a core laboratory for all biological testing following a standardised method and international guidelines, a full report on all TGT parameters, and a normalisation procedure for TGT parameters analysis that reduces interassays variability and allows comparability between studies. Our method for TGT analysis complied with the recent ISTH recommendations, unlike the majority of previous studies in the field, improving the quality of our results. We also provided a detailed description of our APS patients’ clinical and immunological characteristics that was consistent with epidemiological data from large APS registries. Of note, the proportion of CAPS patients in our study is higher than the expected prevalence of 1%–2%, as patients with the most severe manifestations of APS are more likely to be referred to expert centres compared with those with milder forms of the disease. This provides us with a unique opportunity to study a significant number of patients with these rare manifestations of a rare disease. This description was missing in the majority of previous studies. So, the strengths of our study might allow a reliable generalisation of our findings to the primary APS population. Moreover, to our knowledge, our study evaluated for the first time the relationship between markers of complement activation and TGT parameters in APS patients.

To conclude, our study confirmed that APS patients had a particular TGT profile, in the largest APS population published so far. We reported for the first time an association between those TGT parameter changes and the most severe manifestations of APS, such as CAPS or APS relapse. We also originally reported a correlation between markers of complement activation (Bb fragment) and TG parameter changes. Before implementing these tests in routine care, our findings need to be confirmed in an independent APS cohort. Prospective studies are needed to evaluate their predictive value for a risk of poor APS outcomes.
