# Efficacy and safety of direct oral anticoagulants compared with warfarin in antiphospholipid syndrome. Results of a multicenter retrospective cohort study

## Abstract

Background: Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have become firstline treatment for many thrombotic conditions, but their use in antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) patients remains controversial.

Objectives: This multicenter, retrospective cohort study conducted at Emory and McMaster University Medical Centers aimed to explore pattern of DOAC use among APS patients and compare the efficacy and safety of DOACs vs warfarin.

Methods: We included APS patients aged ≥18 years who experienced acute thrombosis between 2012 and 2018 and initiated DOACs or warfarin. Clinical endpoints were recurrent thrombosis, clinically relevant bleeding, and a composite of thrombosis and bleeding (net clinical benefit). Cox proportional hazards models estimated hazard ratios (HRs) for the clinical endpoints and defined DOAC or warfarin exposure as time-varying to account for anticoagulant switching.

Results: A total of 152 patients were identified: 77 initiated treatment with warfarin and 75 with DOACs. Sixty patients switched anticoagulation therapy at least once. Twenty-four patients had triple-positive antiphospholipid antibodies anticoagulation class (DOAC vs warfarin) did not influence risk of recurrent thrombosis (HR, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.46-1.79) or net clinical benefit (HR, 0.81; 95% CI, 0.46-1.43). Conversely, DOACs were associated with 57% reduction in risk of clinically relevant bleeding (HR, 0.43; 95% CI, 0.20-0.95). The risk of recurrent venous or arterial thrombosis was comparable between DOAC and warfarin cohorts.

Conclusion: This study demonstrated comparable efficacy and improved safety of DOACs over warfarin in a predominantly lower-risk APS population. Caution is advised, particularly in APS patients with triple-positive antiphospholipid antibodies or history of arterial thrombosis.

Keywords: antiphospholipid syndrome, anticoagulants, antithrombins, factor Xa inhibitors, thromboembolism, thrombosis, warfarin

• The use of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACS) in antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is controversial. • This multicenter, retrospective study explored efficacy and safety of DOACS vs warfarin in APS. • Twenty-four of 152 APS patients had triple positivity. • DOACS were noted to have similar efficacy and improved safety compared with warfarin.

## Introduction

### Background

Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are approved for the prophylaxis and treatment of venous thromboembolism (VTE). Compared with vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) such as warfarin, DOACs offer many advantages, including fixed doses, fewer drug and food interactions, lower bleeding risk, and the lack of need for routine international normalized ratio (INR) monitoring [ 1 , 2 ]. Since their approval, there has been a growing interest in the use of DOACs for long-term anticoagulation in patients with hereditary and acquired thrombophilia.

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a major acquired thrombophilia in which vascular thrombosis (venous or arterial) and/or pregnancy losses occur in the presence of antiphospholipid (aPL) antibodies [ 3 ]. Despite the use of VKAs, the annual risk of recurrent thrombosis among APS patients ranges between 2% and 5% [ [4] , [5] , [6] ]. Many of the thrombotic recurrences occurred when INR was below desired range (usually 2.0-3.0).

Few prospective clinical trials have compared the efficacy and safety of DOACs vs VKAs in patients with APS [ 7 ]. Two meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials have shown that the risks of VTE recurrence and bleeding events were similar among those treated with DOACs compared with VKAs. However, the meta-analyses also highlighted how APS patients appear to have an increased risk of arterial thrombosis when treated with DOACs compared with warfarin [ [8] , [9] , [10] ].

Notably, the trial of rivaroxaban in APS (TRAPS) trial, which intended to study high-risk APS patients, was prematurely terminated after an interim analysis showed an excess of arterial events among the rivaroxaban group compared with VKAs [ 11 ]. Despite its low patient accrual, the Apixaban for Secondary Prevention of Thromboembolism Among Patients With AntiphosPholipid Syndrome study also showed an excess risk of stroke among APS patients treated with apixaban [ 12 ]. In contemporary case series, the rates of thrombotic recurrence ranged between 0% and 75% with DOACs. More than 50% of these recurrences occurred among patients with triple aPL positivity. A higher number of APS clinical features and history of arterial thrombosis were also associated with a higher risk of recurrent thrombosis [ [13] , [14] , [15] , [16] ].

These findings suggest that DOACs may not be safe in APS patients with high-risk features. Whether certain low-risk APS patient subgroups may benefit from DOAC use is still unknown. We, therefore, believe that there is a pressing need to further evaluate and compare the efficacy and safety of DOACs vs warfarin in APS patients and patient subgroups.

### Objectives

#### Primary objectives

(1) To determine the pattern of DOAC use among APS patients. (2) To compare the efficacy and safety of DOACs vs warfarin throughout study period and in the first 6 months following the index thrombosis event.

#### Exploratory objectives

(1) To compare the risk of recurrent venous vs arterial thrombosis among DOAC and warfarin cohorts. (2) To compare the efficacy and safety of starting DOACs at the time of index thrombosis (ie, acute DOAC use) vs switching to DOACs at a later point during the study period (ie, nonacute DOAC use). (3) To compare the efficacy and safety of initiating rivaroxaban vs apixaban after the index thrombosis event.

## Methods

### Study design

This was a multicenter, retrospective cohort study conducted at Emory University Hospital in Atlanta, Georgia, and McMaster University Medical Center in Ontario, Canada. Data were collected via a retrospective chart review of each institution’s electronic medical record.

### Patient identification

Inclusion criteria were adult patients (≥18 years old), fulfilling the international census criteria for APS diagnosis, who experienced acute thrombosis between January 1, 2012, and December 31, 2018, and started anticoagulation therapy with DOACs (rivaroxaban, dabigatran, apixaban, or edoxaban) or warfarin [ 3 ]. APS diagnosis requires the combination of at least 1 clinical and 1 laboratory criterion. Clinical criteria could either be vascular thrombosis (arterial, venous, or small vessel thrombosis) in any tissue or organ or pregnancy comorbidity (1 or more unexplained deaths of a morphologically normal fetus at or beyond the 10th week of gestation, 1 or more premature births of a morphologically normal neonate before the 34th week of gestation because of eclampsia or placental insufficiency, or 3 or more unexplained consecutive spontaneous abortions before the 10th week of gestation). Small vessel (ie, microvascular) thrombosis refers to thrombotic occlusion of the microcirculation (ie, arterioles, capillaries, and venules) and is usually confirmed via histopathology. Laboratory criteria could either be positive lupus anticoagulant, positive anticardiolipin antibody of immunoglobulin (Ig) G and/or IgM isotype, or positive anti-β2-glycoprotein I antibody of IgG and/or IgM isotype on 2 or more occasions at least 12 weeks apart [ 3 ].

Exclusion criteria were (1) patients who presented with pregnancy complications only during the identification period, or (2) unable to confirm if patients started anticoagulation therapy and/or followed up after initiation of anticoagulation therapy. Patients were followed from the date of the first acute thrombotic event in the identification period (the index date) until the end of the study period (December 31, 2019).

### Clinical endpoints

The primary efficacy endpoint was recurrent thromboembolism, including fatal venous or arterial thromboembolism. The primary safety endpoint was clinically relevant bleeding (CRB) events, which were a composite of major bleeding and clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding events. We also used a primary “net clinical benefit” endpoint, which represented a composite of recurrent thromboembolism and CRB. A similar composite outcome was used in the TRAPS trial and was a good summary representation of clinically important benefits and harm [ 11 ]. Minor bleeding and other non–bleeding-related adverse events represented the secondary safety endpoints.

Major bleeding was defined as (1) fatal bleeding, (2) bleeding associated with a fall in hemoglobin level of ≥2.0 g/dL in 24 hours, (3) bleeding requiring transfusion of ≥2 units of packed red blood cells or leading to permanent disability, and (4) bleeding involving critical anatomic sites (eg, intracranial, retroperitoneal, intraocular, articular, intramuscular, or pericardial bleeding). Clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding was defined as overt bleeding not meeting the criteria for major bleeding but requiring the need for medical, surgical, or angiographic intervention, unscheduled physician contact, or temporary interruption of anticoagulation therapy [ 17 ].

### Data extraction

The following independent variables were collected: (1) demographics, including age at the index thrombosis event, sex (male or female), race, and body mass index (BMI); (2) history of APS diagnosis, including the clinical criteria (ie, arterial thrombosis, venous thrombosis, microvascular thrombosis, or pregnancy complications) and the number of positive aPLs (lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin antibody, or anti-β2-glycoprotein I antibody) at the time of APS diagnosis (ie, single positive, double positive, or triple positive); (3) medical comorbidities; (4) anticoagulation therapy and follow-up duration. If patients switched from one anticoagulant to another, we collected information on the new anticoagulant, including class, duration of therapy, and reasons for switching therapy. We collected information on up to 2 anticoagulation switches so that each patient had follow-up information on up to 3 different anticoagulants and durations of anticoagulation. (5) Clinical endpoints; and (6) concurrent medications.

Comorbidities were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th edition (ICD-10) codes, as documented in the problem list section of each patient’s electronic medical record. Thus, for conditions like chronic kidney disease (CKD), we assumed providers adhered to the standard definitions outlined in the clinical practice guidelines for CKD [ 18 , 19 ]. Bleeding disorders were defined as hematologic conditions that increase a patient’s bleeding risk, commonly due to factors like thrombocytopenia (eg, idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura) or coagulopathy (eg, hemophilia) [ 20 ]. These conditions were also identified using ICD-10 codes.

### Statistical analysis

We compared the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics between the DOAC and warfarin cohorts with either chi-squared, Fisher’s exact (for categorical variables), or analysis of variance (for continuous variables) tests. Incidence of the efficacy and safety endpoints among the DOAC and warfarin cohorts was compared using Kaplan–Meier (K-M) computational methods along with log-rank tests and 95% CIs [ 21 ].

Time-to-event was defined as the time from the index date to the date of the first efficacy or safety endpoint or the date of censorship. DOACs or warfarin exposure was time-varying to account for people switching from DOAC to warfarin and vice versa. Thus, a patient could have multiple observations. Clinical endpoints were anchored to when a patient was on DOAC or warfarin, with no washout period, because of the short half-life of these anticoagulants. For example, a patient who was on warfarin for the initial 4 months, then switched to DOAC for the remainder of the study and had an event at sixth month, was considered in the warfarin group from time 0 to 4 months and in the DOAC group from 4 months onward. The event at sixth month would be anchored to when the person was in the DOAC group.

To generate survival curves for our time-dependent exposure (DOAC vs warfarin), we used the method proposed by Simon and Makuch [ [22] , [23] , [24] ]. This method takes into account the change in an individual’s exposure status over time. The survival computations follow the same principles as the K-M method. However, unlike the K-M method, where the risk set is fixed at time 0, Simon and Makuch [ 24 ] method incorporates a time-dependent exposure that results in a dynamically changing risk set. This risk set is redefined at each unique time point based on the current value of the exposure. Consequently, the number of individuals included within the risk set may increase or decrease throughout the duration of the study [ 24 ].

We used Cox proportional hazards regression models to estimate adjusted hazard ratios (aHR) and 95% CIs and to adjust for potential confounding factors. These models accounted for recurring events and time-varying DOAC or warfarin exposure as well [ 25 ]. The multivariable models included the treatment variable (DOAC vs warfarin) and 1 to 3 additional variables at a time to avoid overfitting. Furthermore, the risk of CRB analyses was stratified according to presence or absence of comorbidities that pose a higher risk of bleeding, including previous history of bleeding, peptic ulcer disease, esophageal varices, inflammatory bowel disease, bleeding disorders such as immune thrombocytopenic purpura, and CKD.

Finally, we conducted a sensitivity analysis focusing exclusively on the initial anticoagulation therapy prescribed to each patient. Patients were followed until they experienced an event, switched therapies, or the study concluded. In this approach, the analysis was limited to a single observation per patient, unlike the primary analysis described above, which allowed for multiple observations per patient due to recurring events.

We used the Lifelines 0.27.7 package by Python and LIFEREG package by SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc) for survival analyses. All analyses and reporting were compliant with Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology recommendations ( Supplementary Table S1 ) [ 26 ].

## Results

### Patient characteristics and pattern of DOAC use

The most common location for index thrombosis was lower extremity deep venous thrombosis, followed by pulmonary embolism and stroke/transient ischemic attack.

Patients who started on DOACs were less likely to have cerebral venous ( P = .03) or microvascular thrombosis ( P = .05) as their index event. ### Primary outcomes

There was no statistically significant difference in the risk of recurrent thrombosis during the time periods when patients were treated with DOACs vs warfarin ( Figure 2 A). On the contrary, the risk of CRB events was significantly lower among patients treated with DOACs ( Figure 2 B). The net clinical benefit outcome was similar between the 2 cohorts ( Figure 2 C). When time-to-event analysis was restricted to the first 6 months after index thrombosis event, there were no significant differences in the risk of recurrent thrombosis ( P = .28), CRB ( P = .08), or net clinical benefit ( P = .17) between warfarin and DOACs ( Supplementary Figure S1 A–C).

(C) Survival curves of time until thrombosis or bleeding by anticoagulation class. DOAC, direct oral anticoagulant.

In the univariable Cox regression models, none of the following variables influenced the risk of recurrent thrombosis: anticoagulation category, age, BMI, aPL positivity (single vs double vs triple positivity), or location of the index thrombosis event ( Supplementary Table S4 ). In the univariable models predicting the risk of CRB events, DOAC use was associated with a significantly lower risk of bleeding (HR, 0.44; 95% CI, 0.21-0.96; Supplementary Table S5 ). The net clinical benefit outcome was not influenced by anticoagulation category, age, BMI, or location of the index thrombosis event ( Supplementary Table S6 ).

In the multivariable Cox regression model, including age, aPL positivity (single vs double vs triple positivity), and location of the index thrombosis event, anticoagulation class (DOAC vs warfarin) was not significantly associated with recurrent thrombosis or the net clinical benefit outcome. DOAC use was associated with > 50% reduction in the risk of CRB ( Table 4 ).

When CRB regression analysis was stratified according to absence or presence of bleeding comorbidities (eg, previous history of bleeding, peptic ulcer disease, bleeding varices, inflammatory bowel disease, and CKD), DOAC use maintained its protective effect over warfarin in those without bleeding comorbidities (aHR, 0.27; 95% CI, 0.09-0.82) and was as safe as warfarin in patients at higher risk of bleeding (aHR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.27-2.98).

### Secondary outcomes

Minor bleeding events occurred in 32 patients (23%), whereas 15 patients (11%) had other adverse events, such as abdominal pain, dizziness, lightheadedness, irritability, and headaches. Only 1 patient had an obstetric complication, which was an early miscarriage (<10 weeks of gestation). There were no differences in the distribution of minor bleeding or other adverse events among DOAC and warfarin cohorts ( Supplementary Table S7 ). Supplementary Table S8 provides a description of the locations of minor bleeding events.

### Exploratory analyses

There was a total of 35 events (17 events with warfarin and 18 events with DOACs, 10 arterial thrombosis events, and 25 venous thrombosis events) in the time-dependent Cox regression analysis of the risk of recurrent thrombosis. There were 29 events (19 events with warfarin and 10 events with DOACs) in the time-dependent Cox regression analysis of the risk of CRB.

There were no significant differences in the risk of recurrent venous ( P = .80) or arterial ( P = .29) thrombosis during the time periods when patients were treated with DOACs or warfarin ( Figure 3 A, B). Seventy-five patients started on DOAC at the time of the index thrombosis event (acute DOAC use) compared with 25 patients who switched to a DOAC after a mean of 27.40 ± 18.49 months (range, 1.80-69.83 months) from the index thrombosis event (nonacute DOAC use). There were no significant differences in the risks of recurrent thrombosis events ( P = .20), CRB events ( P = .26), or the net clinical benefit ( P = .06) between acute and nonacute DOAC use.

**Fig:** Figure 3 (A) Survival curves of time to recurrent venous thrombosis by anticoagulation class. (B) Survival curves of time to recurrent arterial thrombosis by anticoagulation class. DOAC, direct oral anticoagulant.

Of 75 patients who initiated anticoagulation therapy with DOAC, 50 patients were treated with rivaroxaban and 22 patients with apixaban. There were no significant differences in the risks of recurrent thrombosis events ( P = .10), CRB events ( P = .15), or the net clinical benefit ( P = .05) between patients treated with rivaroxaban or apixaban ( Supplementary Figure S2 A–C).

### Sensitivity analysis

In the sensitivity analysis restricted to each patient’s initial anticoagulation therapy, the overall direction of the associations remained consistent. However, some associations were no longer statistically significant, likely due to the reduced follow-up time and fewer observed events ( Supplementary Figure S3 A–C, Supplementary Table S9 ).

## Discussion

In this multicenter, retrospective study, we examined the pattern of anticoagulation use and compared the efficacy and safety of DOACs vs warfarin in a large cohort of APS patients. Our patient cohort was equally divided between DOACs and warfarin as initial anticoagulants, and switching to different anticoagulants during the course of the study period was not uncommon. We found no statistically significant difference in the risk of recurrent thrombosis during the time periods when patients were treated with DOACs vs warfarin. The risk of CRB events was significantly lower among patients treated with DOACs compared with those treated with warfarin.

DOACs have emerged as the preferred choice for anticoagulation due to their fixed dosing, lack of INR monitoring, and improved safety profiles compared with warfarin [ 27 ]. We found that approximately 40% of APS patients who initiated treatment with a DOAC or warfarin switched therapy at least once, and about 10% switched twice. Patient preference emerged as one of the most common reasons for therapy switching. In addition, patients who started warfarin were more likely to switch to DOAC than the other way around. Results from a public (Medicare free-for-service) and commercial (International Business Machines marketscan) health insurance database, including 298,609 patients initiating oral anticoagulants for VTE, showed an increase in the proportion of patients on DOACs vs warfarin from 0% in 2010 to 31.9% in 2014 and 89.2% in 2020 [ 28 ]. These findings indicate an increasing preferential usage of DOACs over warfarin and underscore the importance of involving APS patients in the decision-making process regarding the choice of anticoagulant.

Recent clinical trials have raised concerns about an increased risk of thrombotic events in certain subgroups of APS patients receiving DOACs. Consequently, international societies recommend warfarin over DOACs, particularly for high-risk APS patients with triple-positive aPL or a history of arterial thrombotic events [ 29 ]. The risk of VTE appears to be similar between APS patients treated with warfarin and those treated with DOACs [ 11 , 30 , 31 ]. However, there is a real concern regarding the risk of arterial events with DOACs. In our study, the occurrence of venous and arterial events was comparable among patients receiving DOACs and warfarin. It is important to note that APS encompasses a heterogeneous group of patients with varying thrombosis risks. High-risk APS patients with triple-positive aPL seem to be at a much higher risk of recurrent arterial thrombosis events compared with their counterparts. The European Union Drug Regulatory Authorities 2019 trial and TRAPS 2018 trial reported an excess of recurrent arterial thrombotic events among APS patients treated with rivaroxaban [ 11 , 31 ]. These trials primarily recruited high-risk APS patients who tested positive for all 3 types of aPL: 61.1% in the European Union Drug Regulatory Authorities 2019 trial and 100% in the TRAPS study. In contrast, only 15% of the patients in our cohort had triple-positive aPL, which might explain the discrepancy in the risk of arterial events among those treated with DOACs noted in our study.

Conversely, in the rivaroxaban in APS (RAPS) 2016 trial, none of APS patients receiving rivaroxaban experienced arterial thrombotic events. This study intentionally selected APS patients with a lower-risk profile to ensure a homogeneous study population. Only 12% of the patients in the rivaroxaban group and 20% in the warfarin group of RAPS trial had triple aPL positivity [ 30 ]. Similarly, a single-arm feasibility cohort study of rivaroxaban in APS recruited 96 patients and did not reveal a higher-than-expected risk of recurrent thromboembolic events compared with historical cohorts [ 32 ]. Finally, a systematic review and meta-analysis investigating the use of DOACs to prevent thromboembolism in patients with various hereditary and acquired thrombophilia found that none of the APS patients receiving DOACs in the EINSTEIN, Hokusai, or RAPS studies experienced arterial thrombotic events [ 8 , 10 ]. However, the proportions of patients with triple aPL positivity in the EINSTEIN and Hokusai trials were not publicly available, though it is likely to be very small.

## Conclusion

Our study, which included predominantly lower-risk APS patient population, demonstrated comparable efficacy and improved safety of DOACs vs warfarin. While DOACs offer significant advantages over warfarin and have become the firstline treatment for many thrombotic conditions, their use in APS patients warrants careful consideration, particularly for those with triple-positive aPL or a history of arterial thrombotic events. Discussions with patients are crucial in determining the optimal anticoagulation strategy.

## Limitations

This study presents a comprehensive 7-year analysis of real-world experiences in managing a rare yet challenging group of patients afflicted with APS. However, its retrospective nature and limited inclusion of high-risk APS cases, particularly those with triple aPL positivity, constitute notable limitations.

The retrospective nature of our study inherently restricts the depth of data available and relies on the accuracy of recorded information. Data extraction through ICD-10 codes, while standardized, may not capture all relevant clinical nuances, as codes may be inconsistently applied or lack specificity for certain comorbidities and disease subtypes. Additionally, some conditions may be underreported or coded differently by individual providers, which can introduce variability in the identification and classification of patient conditions. These limitations may affect comprehensiveness and precision of the data obtained for the analysis.

One limitation of this study is also the relatively small sample size of only 152 patients, which may impact generalizability of our findings. Although the multicenter nature of our cohort helps improve diversity and representation, a larger sample size would allow for more robust subgroup analyses and increase statistical power. The limited sample size may have reduced the ability to detect any significant differences in the efficacy comparison between warfarin and DOACs.

We acknowledge that time in therapeutic range (TTR) can be an important measure in assessing the effectiveness and safety of warfarin. However, given that our study is a real-world analysis, we did not collect data on TTR. Our study was designed to reflect typical clinical practices, where detailed TTR data are not always readily available or recorded. Thus, evaluating TTR was beyond the intended scope of our study.

Our analyses were further constrained by hidden confounders that could not be reliably measured, including patient adherence and additional risk factors at time of thrombotic recurrence events. Finally, it is important to note that the real-world utilization of warfarin is less effective compared with the controlled conditions of clinical trials, potentially accounting for our cohort’s comparable thrombotic risk with both warfarin and DOACs, contrary to findings in certain referenced studies like TRAPS.
