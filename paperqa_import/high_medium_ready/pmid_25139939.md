# PAPER

# A new trick for an ancient drug: quinine dissociates antiphospholipid immune complexes

E Bezati $^{1}$ , X-X Wu $^{2}$ , AS Quinn $^{3}$ , DJ Taatjes $^{3}$ and JH Rand $^{2}$

<sup>1</sup>Montefiore Medical Center, Albert Einstein College of Medicine, Bronx, NY, USA; <sup>2</sup>Department of Pathology and Laboratory Medicine, Weill Cornell Medical College, Cornell University, New York, NY, USA; and <sup>3</sup>Department of Pathology and Microscopy Imaging Center, College of Medicine, University of Vermont, Burlington, VT, USA

<!-- page 5 -->
Quinine, a quinoline derivative, is an ancient antipyretic drug with antimalarial properties that has been phased out by more effective synthetic candidates. In previous studies we discovered that hydroxychloroquine (HCQ), a synthetic antimalarial with structural similarities to quinine, reduced the binding of antiphospholipid (aPL) immune complexes to phospholipid bilayers. We performed ellipsometry and atomic force microscopy (AFM) studies to measure the effect of quinine on dissociation of anti- $\beta 2$ -glycoprotein I (anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI) immune complexes. We found that quinine desorbed pre-formed $\beta_{2}$ GPI-aPL immunoglobulin (Ig)G complexes from phospholipid bilayers at significantly lower molar concentrations than HCQ. Quinine also inhibited the formation of immune complexes with a higher efficacy than HCQ at equivalent drug concentrations of $0.2\mathrm{mg / ml}$ ( $0.192 \pm 0.025\mu \mathrm{g / cm}^2$ for quinine vs. $0.352 \pm 0.014\mu \mathrm{g / cm}^2$ for HCQ, $p < 0.001$ ). Furthermore, AFM imaging experiments revealed that addition of quinine disintegrated immune complexes bound to planar phospholipid layers. The desorptive and inhibitory effects of the old drug, quinine, toward $\beta_{2}$ GPI-aPL IgG complexes and $\beta_{2}$ GPI were significantly more pronounced compared to the synthetic antimalarial, HCQ. The results suggest that the quinoline core of the molecule is a critical domain for this activity and that side chains may further modulate this effect. The results also indicate that there may yet be room for considering new activities of very old drugs in devising clinical trials on potential non-anticoagulant treatments for antiphospholipid syndrome (APS). Lupus (2015) 24, 32-41.

Key words: Quinine; hydroxychloroquine; antiphospholipid syndrome; APS; immune complexes

# Introduction

The bark of the cinchona tree was used by indigenous people of the Andes for its therapeutic properties long before the colonial era. The therapeutic utility of this bark became realized in the 17th century when it was found by Europeans to possess antimalarial properties. The active antimalarial molecule, quinine (Figure 1(a)), found in cinchona bark, was isolated in 1820 and was extensively used until the mid-20th century, when evidence of quinine resistance by the malaria parasite began to appear. Quinine has also been reported to have additional properties such as bronchodilation and

APS is an acquired autoimmune disease that manifests as vascular thrombosis and/or pregnancy complications that result from placental insufficiency.[15,16] Although several thrombogenic mechanisms have been proposed for the antibodies, a common denominator is their recognition of phospholipid-binding proteins—the most important of which appears to be $\beta 2$ -glycoprotein I ( $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ ),[17] a member of the complement control/short-chain consensus repeat family.[18]

Although prospective controlled randomized studies have not yet been completed, observational studies indicated that HCQ treatment may be associated with reduced thrombosis. A cross-sectional study of antiphospholipid (aPL)-positive patients with thrombotic events and asymptomatic aPL-positive patients revealed that HCQ may protect against thrombosis in the asymptomatic group.[19] The drug has also been shown to protect against thrombosis in an animal model of APS,[20] and reverse platelet activation, induced by aPL antibodies.[21]

Recently, we showed that HCQ disrupts aPL $\beta_{2}$ GPI complexes on phospholipid bilayers, and also interferes with $\beta_{2}$ GPI binding. This effect was confirmed on cultured monocytes, endothelial cells, and trophoblasts.[22,23] The common quinoline ring present in these two drugs motivated us to investigate the effect of quinine on adsorption and desorption of $\beta_{2}$ GPI-aPL immunoglobulin (Ig)G antibody complexes as well as $\beta_{2}$ GPI alone on phospholipid bilayers using a thin-film ellipsometer and atomic force microscopy. We confirmed the effects of HCQ and found that quinine is a more effective

desorptive agent of $\beta_{2}$ GPI-aPL IgG complexes and $\beta_{2}$ GPI than HCQ. Furthermore, it also inhibits the formation of $\beta_{2}$ GPI-aPL IgG complexes on phospholipid bilayers more efficaciously than HCQ.

# Materials and methods

# Materials

Permission for use of excess plasmas, obtained from clinical assays of APS patients, was granted by the institutional review board of Montefiore Medical Center. Recombinant $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ was expressed in insects, purified, and had its purification tag removed. Wild-type $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ (wt $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ ) was purchased from GenWay Biotech. The cell line for aPL mAb IS4 was a kind gift from Dr Pojen P. Chen (University of California, Los Angeles). Hydroxychloroquine sulfate, quinine hydrochloride dehydrate, and buffer chemicals were purchased from standard commercial services; 1, 2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phospho-L-serine (PS) and 1, 2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (PC) were purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids (Alabaster, AL). Bovine serum albumin (BSA) was purchased from Sigma. Anti-BSA antibody was received from Reichert Technologies.

# Preparation of lipid vesicles

Phospholipid vesicles were prepared by mixing $30\%$ PS in chloroform with $70\%$ PC (\% mol) and drying for four hours under nitrogen. Buffer $(3\mathrm{mM}\mathrm{CaCl}_2, 0.01\mathrm{M}$ HEPES, $0.14\mathrm{MNaCl}, \mathrm{pH}7.5)$ was added to the powdered mixture, followed by sonication using a Sonic Dismembrator (Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) operating at 4 watts for 20 seconds four times, with the vial cooled in ice in-between sonifications.

<!-- page 3 -->
# Ellipsometry studies

Ellipsometry is an established technique for monitoring protein adsorption that has found considerable usage in antibody-antigen recognition,[24-26] quantification of blood coagulation factors,[27-29] and complement activation.[30,31] The ellipsometer was purchased from Rudolph & Sons (Fairfield, NJ) and upgraded.[32] All experiments were performed in triplicate at room temperature as previously described.[22] Briefly, cleaned hydrophobic and reflective silicon slides, used as the support for phospholipid bilayers, were placed in a clean quartz cuvette containing $1\mathrm{ml}$ of bilayer buffer $(1\mathrm{mM}\mathrm{CaCl}_2$ $0.01\mathrm{M}$ HEPES, $0.14\mathrm{MNaCl}$ $\mathsf{pH}$ 7.5) followed by the addition of phospholipid vesicles $(50\mu \mathrm{M})$ . Upon formation of the bilayer, the cuvette was flushed with $20\mathrm{ml}$ of stirring buffer $(1.25\mathrm{mMCaCl}_2$ $0.01\mathrm{M}$ HEPES, $0.14\mathrm{MNaCl})$

To compare the desorptive effect of the two drugs on pre-bound $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ and aPL IgG complexes $(0.6\mu \mathrm{g / ml}\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ and $0.1\mathrm{mg / ml}$ aPL IgG) or $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ alone $(7\mu \mathrm{g / ml})$ serial concentrations of the drugs were added from 0.001 to $0.5\mathrm{mg / ml}$ , allowing for the establishment of equilibrium after each addition, and the quantities of adsorbed proteins were continuously monitored and recorded. The experiments were stopped when further additions of the drugs did not result in additional desorption. The inhibitory effect of the drugs on complex or $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ binding to phospholipid bilayers was also tested by first adding $0.2\mathrm{mg / ml}$ of each drug followed by complexes $(0.6\mu \mathrm{g / ml}\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ and $0.1\mathrm{mg / ml}$ aPL IgG) or $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ $(7\mu \mathrm{g / ml})$

Control experiments were also performed to gauge whether the desorptive effect noted for quinine might be non-specific. BSA $(5\mathrm{mg / ml})$ was added to a cuvette containing Hepes-buffered saline (HBS). After equilibrium was established the cuvette was flushed three times with HBS to remove unbound BSA. Next, anti-BSA antibody was added to the cuvette at $50~\mu \mathrm{g / ml}$ followed by 0.5 and $1.0\mathrm{mg / ml}$ quinine.

The results were analyzed using SigmaPlot software (SPSS, Chicago, IL). The adsorptions were plotted as a function of drug concentration and a four-parameter logistic equation was used to obtain half maximal effective concentration (EC50) values for the two drugs.

# AFM

The effects of quinine on complex formation and $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ binding, as well as their inhibitory effect, were also studied with AFM as previously described,[22] with minor modifications. Planar

phospholipid layers, containing $30\%$ PS and $70\%$ PC at a concentration of $50\mu \mathrm{M}$ were formed on freshly cleaved mica disks (Ashville-Schoonmaker Mica, Newport News, VA). $\beta_{2}$ GPI (final concentration approximately $10\mu \mathrm{g / ml}$ ) was added to phospholipid-coated disks and incubated at $4^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ overnight followed by imaging with an Asylum Research (AR) MFP-3D BIO AFM (Santa Barbara, CA). Characteristic antigen patches raised above the lipid membrane surface were observed while employing AC mode dynamic imaging with the i-Drive cantilever holder and AR's proprietary modified $100\mu \mathrm{m}$ $\mathrm{Si}_3\mathrm{N}_4$ probe (AR-iDrive-N01). Addition of aPL mAb IS4 $(20\mu \mathrm{g / ml})$ resulted in formation of large circular aggregates of increased heights composed of aPL mAb IS4- $\beta_{2}$ GPI complexes. Finally, after addition of quinine to the imaging fluid meniscus, dissociation effects of the complex were observed. Quantitative analysis of the effects of quinine on the membrane-bound aPL mAb IS4- $\beta_{2}$ GPI was performed with off-line sectional analysis. Cross-sectional height measurements of approximate same locations before and after quinine treatment were performed. Height images were flattened with the "iterative" mask function with a number 1 flatten prior to analysis.

# Statistical analysis

Adsorption and desorption values performed by ellipsometry were compared using a paired two-tailed $t$ test (Instat program, GraphPad, San Diego, CA). $P$ values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

# Quinine has higher efficacy than HCQ

The EC50 value of quinine for aPL IgG- $\beta_{2}$ GPI complexes was $0.136\pm 0.003\mathrm{mg / ml}$ compared to $0.580\pm 0.006\mathrm{mg / ml}$ for HCQ. For $\beta_{2}$ GPI alone, the EC50 values for quinine and HCQ were $0.129\pm 0.004\mathrm{mg / ml}$ and $0.440\pm 0.040\mathrm{mg}/$ ml, respectively. The difference between the EC50 values between complexes and $\beta_{2}$ GPI for quinine

was not significant $(p = 0.07)$ , whereas for HCQ $(p < 0.001)$ the difference was significant (Figure 3(c)).

# Quinine prevents complex formation

The inhibitory effect of the drugs on the adsorption of the complexes was also tested. Each drug was added first at a concentration of $0.2\mathrm{mg / ml}$ , followed by the addition of $\beta_{2}$ GPI and aPL IgG (Figure 4). The effect of quinine on adsorbed immune complexes was visualized using AFM. Addition of aPL mAb IS4 and wt $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ resulted in the formation of discrete immune complex structures. # Discussion

HCQ, a synthetic antimalarial that is a structural analog of quinine, has been exploited for its autoimmune properties.33 We previously showed that HCQ reduced the binding of aPL immune complexes to phospholipid bilayers and monocytes via ellipsometric and AFM studies, and reduces the binding of aPL IgGs to cultured monocytes22 as well as to cultured endothelial cells and a trophoblast cell line23 through quantitative immunoassays.22 This effect was also demonstrated on primary cultures of syncytialized trophoblasts by confocal microscopy.34 The therapeutic effects of HCQ combined with the ubiquity of quinoline derivatives associated with treatment of malaria and inflammatory diseases35-37 motivated us to

investigate similar drugs and compare their efficiency at desorbing aPL IgG- $\beta_{2}$ GPI complexes and $\beta_{2}$ GPI. Quinine and HCQ are antimalarial drugs with similar structures (Figure 1) and comparable molecular weights (324.4 g/mol for quinine and $335.9\mathrm{g / mol}$ for HCQ). The quinoline ring structure, which the two molecules have in common, occurs in various natural products, especially alkaloids, and its derivatives display a range of pharmacological properties.[38,39]

The results of the current study show that quinine is more effective at desorbing aPL IgG- $\beta_{2}$ GPI complexes and $\beta_{2}$ GPI than HCQ at concentrations of up to $0.5\mathrm{mg / ml}$ . The concentration of quinine needed to reach half-maximal desorption was significantly lower than HCQ. Furthermore, total percentage desorption of $\beta_{2}$ GPI by quinine at maximal effective concentration was comparable to HCQ, but the EC50 value for quinine was significantly lower than HCQ. Moreover, addition of quinine to lipid bilayers before the addition of aPL IgG- $\beta_{2}$ GPI complexes inhibited complex formation more efficaciously than HCQ (Figure 4). In addition, the observed effect was clearly not a nonspecific effect of the drug on immune complexes, since quinine did not have any desorptive effect on anti-BSA-BSA immune complexes.

AFM imaging experiments confirmed our ellipsometry results. Addition of quinine resulted in marked erosion of immune complexes. Furthermore, AFM suggested that quinine is able to break apart these complexes by having a disruptive effect on the interaction between domain I of $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ and aPL IgG antibodies, as supported

The differences between the drugs are due to their differing functional groups. HCQ has two hydrogen bond donors and four hydrogen bond acceptors, whereas quinine has one hydrogen bond donor and four hydrogen bond acceptors. Although quinine has a quinoline ring in common with HCQ, the distribution and connectivity of functional groups is different. Furthermore, quinine is more hydrophobic than HCQ. These different physical properties of quinine may render it more effective at desorbing immune complexes at the concentrations used in this study, and preventing them from forming.

These experiments do not directly address the mechanism(s) by which the drugs dissociate immune complexes. Among the possible targets is the phospholipid bilayer and $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ (Figure 6). Insertion of the drugs in the bilayer and association with domain V (phospholipid-binding domain $^{40}$ ) of $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ are among the possible mechanisms of action for the drugs. Insertion of the drugs in the bilayer might affect the lateral mobility of the phospholipids and, therefore, the association of $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ with PS. It is possible that $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ is in coordination with more than one PS molecule and the addition of the drugs increases the separation between PS molecules, reducing the affinity of $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ with the lipid membrane. Furthermore, lateral interactions between the negatively charged head of PS and the polar groups in the drugs could also inhibit the association of $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ with PS.

The epitope in domain V of $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ , which is involved in the binding with PS in phospholipid

membranes, is composed of a number of positively charged amino acids and a hydrophobic loop, which is thought to be inserted in the phospholipid membrane. $^{17,18}$ Association of the drugs with the hydrophobic loop and/or with the positively charged residues could potentially prevent it from binding to the lipid membrane. Studies with various analogs of quinine and $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ mutants are currently being explored to determine more precisely the mode of action of the drugs and their targets.

Adsorption of $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ to PS is a critical step in the APS disease process. $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ binds to PS with low affinity, undergoing a conformational change in the process. This conformational change results in the exposure of antigenic epitopes, which serve as binding sites for aPL IgG antibodies.[41] The binding of aPL IgG antibodies to $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ pre-bound on phospholipid bilayers increases the affinity of the complex for the bilayer.[42] Thus, prevention of complex formation via inhibition and/or disruption of $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ binding is likely to contribute to reducing the APS disease process. Hence, quinine may be a potentially promising drug as a prophylactic; however, determination of the validity of this concept would require appropriately designed clinical trials.

The drug concentrations used in the present study are higher than the ones observed in blood samples of patients undergoing quinine or HCQ therapy. The blood concentration of quinine in patients with uncomplicated malaria is approximately $6 - 10\mu \mathrm{g} / \mathrm{ml}^{43}$ and approximately $1\mu \mathrm{g} / \mathrm{ml}$ for HCQ in patients with immune diseases.[44] However, the concentration of the drug in other tissues ranges from 200 to 20,000 times the

<!-- page 9 -->
concentration found in plasma depending on the tissue. $^{44,45}$

It is possible that the effect of these antimalarial drugs on the binding of $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ to anionic phospholipids could also have an impact on other biologic activities of this protein. For example, it has been reported that $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ displays an anticoagulant effect in the presence of anionic phospholipids such as in activated blood cells.[46] It therefore remains possible that the addition of quinine might conceivably inhibit the anticoagulant property of $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ in otherwise healthy patients but reduce a tendency for thrombosis in APS patients by inhibiting the formation of anti- $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ immune complexes on cell membranes. Comparative studies with in vivo models that are beyond the scope of the present study would be important for elucidating the therapeutic potential of these drugs.

In conclusion, quinine shows potent activity in desorbing aPL IgG- $\beta_{2}$ GPI complexes and $\beta_{2}$ GPI alone. The results suggest that the quinoline core shared by both molecules is a critical domain for this activity and that side chains may further modulate this effect. These results provide insightful information in designing clinical trials on existing agents and designing new drugs. The potential in vivo significance of these findings will need to be investigated in well-designed prospective clinical trials.
