# ABSTRACT

Background: Despite anticoagulant therapy, a antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) has a higher rate of recurrent events, which can lead to damage accrual and a negative impact on life quality. Objectives: To evaluate the risk factors and APS subsets associated with damage accrual.

Patients/methods: We conducted a retrospective single-center study. We reviewed the medical records of 282 APS patients, with a median age of 36 (IQR 30-46) years and a median of 195 (IQR 137-272) months. The primary endpoint was damage accrual during follow-up, defined as organ/tissue impairment present for at least six months or causing permanent loss. The secondary endpoints were early organ damage within six months of disease onset and death.

Results: Eighty $(28.4\%)$ patients presented damage accrual; $52.5\%$ developed damage within six months of APS onset, and $41.3\%$ had more than one organ involved. Neuropsychiatric involvement, affecting $38.8\%$ of the patients, was the most frequent, followed by peripheral vasculopathy and renal involvement, $35\%$ either. Death happened in 7 $(2.5\%)$ patients; damage accrual was associated with a 6-fold risk of death [OR 6.7 (95% CI 1.3-35.1), $p = 0.03$ ]. Microangiopathy and non-criteria manifestations were independent risk factors for damage accrual with 5-fold and 4-fold higher risk, respectively [(OR 4.9 (95% CI 2.1-11.7), $p < 0.0001$ and (OR 3.8 (95% CI 1.5-10.1), $p = 0.007$ ]. The cumulative incidence of damage accrual increased by 5.7-fold and 3.6-fold in patients with microangiopathy and non-criteria manifestations.

Conclusions: APS patients had a higher frequency of damage accrual. Microangiopathy and non-criteria manifestations were independent risk factors for damage accrual.

# 1. Introduction

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a rare immune-mediated acquired thrombophilia mainly affecting young adults. It is characterized by a heterogeneous clinical picture due to arterial and/or venous thrombosis and pregnancy morbidity, giving rise to potentially devastating and life-threatening manifestations [1-3]. Despite the standard of care based on long-term anticoagulant therapy, approximately $44.2\%$ and $20 - 30\%$ of patients, respectively, experience recurrent thrombosis or loss of pregnancy [4,5]. This recurring course of the disease leads to potential damage accrual, negatively impacting quality of life [5,6]. Even though the acute manifestations of the disease are well known, this is not the case for the long-term outcome and damage accrual. Limited

data derive from mixed cohorts of secondary APS (SAPS) and primary APS (PAPS). In the early 2000, in a small cohort of APS patients followed for up ten years, $38.9\%$ developed organ damage. One-fifth of those had functional problems, being unable to carry out daily activities [7]. Almost one decade later, damage accrual was described in approximately $29\%$ of APS patients in two small cohorts of APS populations [5, 8]. Neurologic damage determined the highest morbidity with an estimated rate of 18.71 events/1000 person-years (5). Damage accrual, evaluated by Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) International College of Rheumatology/American College of Rheumatology damage index (SDI) score as a surrogate marker of damage determined a 1.3-fold increased mortality rate for a 1-unit increase [5,9].

In the last decade, efforts have been made to quantify the damage in

<!-- page 2 -->
APS. In 2015, a group of experts from Mexico developed for the first time the disease Damage Index in APS (DIAPS) [10]. DIAPS showed a high correlation with SDI in a cross-sectional study of 50 patients with PAPS, 50 SLE patients, and 50 with SAPS [11]. However, the damage pattern differed between groups; PAPS patients presented a higher score early in the disease course. Recently, Gaspar et al. [12] showed that thrombotic APS patients already presented organ damage six months after disease onset, and a one-point increase in DIAPS in the first five years was associated with about a 5-fold mortality risk. Few studies [5,8,12-14] have addressed the risk factor associated with damage accrual in APS patients with contradictory results. Moreover, the major populations in these studies also consist of SAPS patients for the most SLE patients.

In this study, we aimed to identify risk factors predicting damage accrual in PAPS patients. We also assessed the damage accrual rate and its association with laboratory and clinical APS subsets.

# 2.2. Data collection, outcomes, and definitions

Two authors (A.H., N.P.) independently reviewed the clinical charts of the patients obtained from an electronic centralized database and paper files. The medical records for up to 545 months (median 195; IQR 137-272 months) were reviewed. Patient demographics, clinical manifestations, and laboratory data were retrieved and collected in an ad hoc electronic database. Patients were divided into two groups, with and without damage accrual.

Outcomes. The primary endpoint was the damage accrual developed during the follow-up. It was defined as organ/tissue impairment present for at least six months or causing permanent loss. Although we considered the main domains of the DIAPS score [10] to define the damage, we did not calculate the DIAPS score due to the study retrospective design, which did not enable us to collect information on all items of the DIAPS score, leading to potential bias. The secondary endpoints were damage burden at baseline, defined as organ damage within six months of disease onset, lasting at least six months from the index event, or causing permanent loss and death.

Definitions:

- Microangiopathy was considered as the thrombosis of the small vessels in any organ/tissue, confirmed by biopsy or as the presence of clinical manifestations such as acral fine livedo, livedo racemose, aPL-nephropathy, pulmonary hemorrhage, pre-eclampsia [15], and CAPS [16].   
- Non-criteria manifestations were defined as (a) heart valve disease, (b) livedo reticularis, (c) thrombocytopenia, (d) neurological manifestations, and e) aPL-nephropathy according to the Sidney updated clinical classification [1] presented at disease onset and/or developed during follow up. APL-nephropathy, due to the high risk of performing a biopsy in APS patients, was defined as the presence of renal failure (increase in serum creatine $\geq 0.3\mathrm{mg / dL}$ within two days or glomerular filtration rate $< 60~\mathrm{ml} / \mathrm{min} / 1.73\mathrm{m}^2$ ) and/or of urinary abnormalities (proteinuria $0.5\mathrm{g}$ in a 24-h urine specimen with or without microscopic glomerular hematuria on two separate occasions, in the absence of urinary infection) or by renal biopsy consistent with histologic and immunohistologic features of APS whenever possible [17-19].   
- Congenital thrombophilia was considered the protein C deficiency, protein S deficiency, antithrombin deficiency, factor V Leiden, and prothrombin G20210A mutations.

Laboratory definition. Antiphospholipid antibodies were performed and confirmed by a second test repeated at least 12 weeks after. Immunoglobulin (Ig) G/IgM anticardiolipin (aCL) and IgG/IgM anti- $\beta 2$ glycoprotein I (anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ ) antibodies were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay following SSC recommendations for aPL testing [20]. The cut-off values for medium/high titers for both aCL and anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ antibodies were set by the 99th percentile calculated in 120 age-matched healthy donors. Lupus anticoagulant (LAC) was detected following internationally accepted recommendations [21,22] using dilute Russell viper venom and dilute activated partial thromboplastin times as screening tests. As previously described, the antiphospholipid antibody profile was defined as single, double, and triple positive [23].

# 3. Results

# 3.1. Characteristics of the study cohort

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the study cohort at baseline, according to damage burden, are illustrated in Table 1. Arterial thrombosis was the most frequent thrombotic manifestation, found in almost half of the population. However, the type of the first thrombotic event did not discriminate patients with organ damage from those without. Furthermore, one-third of the patients presented non-criteria manifestations at disease onset. They were characterized by livedo reticularis in 20 (47.6%), aPL-nephropathy in 12 (28.5%), valvulopathy in 8 (19.0%), thrombocytopenia in 5 (11.95), and epilepsy in one (2.4%) patient; 7 (16.7%) patients presented more than one non-criteria manifestation. Also, nearly half of the patients with organ impairment were found to have microangiopathy, thrombosis of the small vessels in any organ/tissue (15%), acral fine livedo (5%), livedo racemose (5%), aPL-nephropathy (40%), pre-eclampsia (20%), and CAPS (15%).

Forty-two $(14.9\%)$ patients presented damage burden at baseline. Table 2 illustrates the association between damage burden at baseline and clinical and laboratory APS subsets. Thirty-four/42 $(80.9\%)$ of patients with damage burden at baseline developed further organ damage during follow-up. Damage burden at baseline was associated with a 3-fold risk of developing damage accrual during follow-up [OD 3.4 (95% CI 1.6-7.0), $p = 0.002$ ].

In the multivariate logistic regression, venous thrombosis was associated by itself with an $82\%$ lower risk of developing early organ damage [OR 0.08 (95% CI 0.01-0.6), $\mathrm{p} = 0.02$ ].

# 3.2. Damage accrual in PAPS patients

Organ damage accrual was recorded in 80/282 $(28.4\%)$ of the PAPS patients at follow-up. Almost half of the patients (42, $52.5\%$ ) presented organ damage by the first six months from APS onset. Thirty-three $(41.3\%)$ patients had more than one organ damage. As illustrated in Fig. 1, neuropsychiatric involvement was the most frequent, presented in 31 $(38.8\%)$ PAPS patients. It was characterized by hemiparesis in 10 $(32.3\%)$ , epilepsy in 11 $(35.5\%)$ , cognitive dysfunction/dementia in 7 $(22.6\%)$ , neurosensorial hearing loss in 2 $(6.5\%)$ patients, and 1 $(3.2\%)$ case of optic neuritis. Peripheral vasculopathy and renal involvement were detected in 28 $(35\%)$ patients. Peripheral vasculopathy consists of major tissue loss in 7 patients $(25.0\%)$ , minor tissue loss in 5 patients $(17.8\%)$ , and vascular venous insufficiency in 16 patients $(57.2\%)$ . Thrombotic microangiopathy characterized renal involvement with acute renal failure in about one-third of the patients $10/28$ $(35.7\%)$ . Five out of twenty-eight $(17.9\%)$ patients required kidney transplantation, three $(60.0\%)$ developed renal failure respectively by 7, 10, and 25 years after transplantation, and 2 of them are currently in hemodialysis three times weekly. Eight/19 $(42.1\%)$ developed aPL heart valve disease, six mitral, and two aortic valvulopathies, 5 of them $(62.5\%)$ requiring valve replacement with a mechanical valve in four and bioprosthetic in one case. One patient died from complications after valve replacement. Six out of 19 $(31.6\%)$ underwent coronary artery bypass/percutaneous revascularization due to myocardial infarction,

and 5/19 $(26.3\%)$ suffered from cardiomyopathy. The latter developed ventricular dysfunction, and two were treated with an Implantable Cardioverter Defibrillator due to severe ventricular dysfunction. We found five $(6.3\%)$ patients with pulmonary involvement, respectively, 3 $(60\%)$ with alveolar hemorrhage, and 2 $(40\%)$ with pulmonary arterial hypertension. Furthermore, chronic cutaneous ulcers, visual loss, Budd-Chiari syndrome and ischemic colitis, suprarenal insufficiency, and hypopituitarism were reported in 9 $(11.25\%)$ , 2 $(2.5\%)$ , 4 $(5.0\%)$ , and 2 $(2.5\%)$ , respectively. No case of musculoskeletal involvement was recorded.

Death was observed in 7 (2.5%) patients, 6 (85.7%), and 1 (14.3%), respectively, in those with and without damage accrual $(\mathrm{p} = 0.04)$ . Damage accrual was associated with a 6-fold risk of death [OR 6.7 (95% CI 1.3-35.1), $\mathrm{p} = 0.03$ ]. Instead, there was no association between death and damage burden at baseline. The Kaplan-Meier curves showing the estimated survival probability of PAPS patients with damage burden at baseline and those with damage accrual during the follow-up are given in Fig. 2A and B.

# 3.3. Association of organ damage accrual with different clinical and laboratory subsets of APS

The cumulative incidence (Fig. 3) of the primary endpoint in the patients with microangiopathy and non-criteria manifestations were respectively [OR 5.7 (95% CI 3.9-8.3), $p < 0.0001$ and OR 3.6 (95% CI 2.7-4.9), $p < 0.0001$ ].

Table 3 and Fig. 4 highlight the association between damage accrual at follow-up and APS's different clinical and laboratory subsets.

At the multivariate analysis, microangiopathy and non-criteria manifestations were found to be independent risk factors for damage accrual in PAPS patients with 5-fold higher risk and 4-fold higher risk, respectively [(OR 4.9 (95% CI 2.1-11.7), $p < 0.0001$ and (OR 3.8 (95% CI 1.5-10.1), $p = 0.007$ ).

Fifty-seven $(20.2\%)$ patients had developed organ damage after six months of disease onset; of note, one-third of them $(19, 33.3\%)$ developed a further organ impairment during the follow-up. Microangiopathy and non-criteria manifestations beard a rough 6-fold increased risk of a new organ impairment in PAPS patients, respectively [OR 5.8 (95% CI 1.4-21.9), $p = 0.02$ and OR 6.5 (95% CI 1.6-22.1), $p = 0.009$ ].

On the other hand, new clues on the pathogenesis of APS are emerging, indicating the occurrence of an inflammatory phenotype intertwined with the thrombotic one. This novel concept, tromboinflammation, coupling inflammation and thrombosis, involves innate and adaptive immunity pathways [31]. One-third of our cohort's patients $(28.8\%)$ developed damage accrual during follow-up; half presented at least one organ dysfunction within 6 months from disease onset. Two decades ago, a study by Erkan et al. [7] reported a prevalence of $38.9\%$ of organ damage, much higher

than that reported by us. More recent data confirm our findings, observing $29\%$ of damage accrual, after a mean follow-up of 7.55 [5] and 18 years [8], respectively. This reduced prevalence of damage accrual may be explained by a better understanding of clinical and laboratory characteristics of the diseases, leading to an earlier diagnosis

<!-- page 7 -->
and accurate monitoring and treatment [34]. However, there is still much to do. A recent retrospective single-center study reported early damage within 6 months after disease onset in $35\%$ of the patients. A one-point increase in DIAPS score in the first five years after disease onset predicts mortality [HR 5.4 (95% CI 2.33-12.52), $\mathrm{p} < 0.001$ ], even when adjusted for confounders, APS category, sex, and age [12]. Furthermore, in a retrospective cohort followed longitudinally for 10 years, it was found that patients with PAPS presented a higher damage score early in the disease course $(1.72 \pm 1.17$ vs. $0.82 \pm 0.96$ , $\mathrm{p} < 0.001)$ than those with SAPS where the damage score increased over time $(0.43 \pm 0.30$ vs. $1.22 \pm 1.24$ , $\mathrm{p} < 0.001)$ , probably due to corticosteroids therapy [13].

The neuropsychiatric involvement, mostly characterized by epilepsy and cognitive dysfunction/dementia, was the most observed, followed by peripheral vasculopathy, renal, and cardiac involvement. Although neuropsychiatric manifestations prevail in the various studies [6,8,13, 14,35,36], there is a difference regarding organ damage across the studies, reflecting the sample size and study population. Interestingly, Balbi et al. found a higher rate of peripheral vascular involvement in patients with thrombotic PAPS. At the same time, the neuropsychiatric and cardiovascular involvement were most frequently reported in the non-thrombotic aPL positive [14]. Once again, these findings confirm the complexity of the pathogenesis of APS, where anti-thrombotic therapy is not enough to fight against the disease and to avoid organ impairment.

Another relevant finding of our study was the significantly higher rate of damage accrual associated with both thrombosis and pregnancy subset rather than with thrombosis or pregnancy alone[F1]. In contrast, pregnancy morbidity alone was significantly associated with a lower risk of damage accrual. Previously, Grika et al. [F1]Please, check if the revised sentence is correct [5] found that pregnancy morbidity was associated with less damage than thrombosis at APS presentation (b coefficient $= -1.01$ , $p = 0.04$ ). Thrombosis, particularly in the arterial district, had been described as associated with organ damage [5,8]. This concept of both thrombosis and pregnancy morbidity is important in stratifying the risk of organ damage.

Furthermore, the type of thrombosis event provides important clues to stratify the risk of damage accrual. We found that venous thrombosis alone at disease onset was associated with a lower risk of damage accrual during follow-up. On the other hand, patients with both arterial and venous events throughout the follow-up presented a significantly higher risk of damage accrual. Recently, Gaspar et al. [12] showed that the presence of venous thrombosis was significantly associated with a lower risk of early organ damage ( $\beta$ coefficient $= -1.67$ , $\mathsf{p} < 0.001$ ). Also, in a retrospective cohort from Greece stratified according to the initial clinical manifestations, venous thrombosis was associated with less organ damage than the arterial events [5].

Regarding the aPL profile, both aCL and anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ IgG isotypes and their high titers (data not shown) were significantly associated with damage accrual. In contrast, aCL and anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ IgM isotypes did not, confirming the importance of the IgG isotype in APS pathogenesis [37]. LA and triple aPL positive, known as essential risk factors for thrombotic recurrence and pregnancy morbidity [4,38,39], were both associated with an increased risk of damage accrual.

Traditional cardiovascular risk factors, namely sex, hypertension, diabetes, and obesity, were significantly higher in patients with damage accrual at baseline. However, the multivariate analysis did not result in an independent risk factor for organ impairment (data not shown) at the follow-up. A recent study from the APS Action registry [14] found a significant association between hyperlipidemia, obesity, hypertension, and high damage $(\mathrm{DIAPS} \geq 3)$ in thrombotic PAPS and between hypertension and damage in non-thrombotic aPL patients. However, earlier studies evaluating the long-term outcome of APS patients did not observe any significant association between organ damage and traditional cardiovascular risk factors. As the authors themselves discussed, this discrepancy could depend on the nature of the data's cross-sectional

retrospective analysis. Thus, further extensive cohort prospective studies are needed to provide conclusive data on the impact of traditional cardiovascular risk factors on organ damage in the APS population.

We are aware that our study has some limitations. First, this is a retrospective study with some patients followed for more than 40 years, thereby missing some data. Second, referral bias should be considered as we are a tertiary center, and nearly one-third of the patients in our department came from smaller units, which could lead to a selection bias. However, our study has some strengths. First, this is the largest single-center cohort of well-characterized PAPS patients evaluating damage accrual. Moreover, we evaluated the patients using standard methodology and protocols.

In conclusion, PAPS patients showed a higher damage accrual rate, half presenting within six months from disease onset. Microangiopathy and non-criteria manifestations were independent risk factors for damage accrual regardless of APS clinical subsets, laboratory profile, and first thrombotic event. These findings should be considered when counseling APS patients and may help clinicians make more appropriate therapeutic decisions.
