# The role of interferon-mediated suppression of monocyte immunothrombosis in infection susceptibility in systemic lupus erythematosus

## Abstract

Patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) exhibit significant susceptibility to severe bacterial infections, a leading cause of mortality. A key host defence mechanism is immunothrombosis, wherein activated monocytes rapidly upregulate tissue factor (TF) to initiate localized fibrin deposition that traps and contains pathogens. Effective immunothrombosis is therefore critical for preventing microbial dissemination. This process appears deficient in SLE, a disease defined by a systemic prothrombotic state, yet poor infection outcomes. A recently discovered molecular interaction suggests that TF directly binds to the interferon-α receptor (IFNAR1), acting as a rheostat to suppress interferon signalling. We hypothesize that in SLE, this regulatory axis is disrupted. The dominant, sustained interferon-stimulated gene (ISG) signatures in monocytes limit their capacity for TF upregulation in response to bacterial challenge, thereby impairing immunothrombosis and compromising bacterial containment. Supporting this, SLE patients with secondary antiphospholipid syndrome who have lower interferon signatures display markedly elevated TF levels and a different thrombotic profile, demonstrating the inverse relationship in a clinical subset. Furthermore, TF induction in monocytes is glycolysis-dependent, and SLE monocytes are known to have profound metabolic alterations. The chronic interferon state may thus impose a metabolic constraint that further limits the bioenergetic capacity for a robust TF response. Therefore, the confluence of interferon-driven suppression and metabolic dysfunction in SLE monocytes provides a compelling explanation for the failure of immunothrombosis, directly linking a core disease feature to infection susceptibility.

In SLE, chronic type I interferon signalling and intrinsic monocyte metabolic defects perturb tissue factor (TF) induction, impairing TF-dependent immunothrombosis and predisposing patients to severe infection despite systemic hypercoagulability. The TF–IFNAR1 “molecular switch” linking coagulation and interferon pathways appears maladaptively rewired in SLE, with contrasting high-TF/low-IFN signatures seen in SLE patients with secondary APS. Therapeutically targeting interferon signalling and monocyte metabolism may restore antimicrobial TF responses without exacerbating thrombosis, but validation in primary SLE monocyte subsets is needed.

## Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a complex autoimmune disease driven by dysregulated immunity, aberrant immune cell metabolism, and increased levels of circulating IFNα. Although therapeutic advances have improved life expectancy, SLE mortality rates remain 3-fold higher than in the general population, with infections as a leading cause of death [ 1 ]. Despite optimized immunosuppressive therapies, SLE patients face heightened susceptibility to severe infections—spanning opportunistic pathogens, common bacterial/viral agents, and nosocomial infections—often with atypical clinical presentations.

Coagulation is a core component in the host response to infection [ 2 ]. Under pathogenic circumstances, such as the detection of microbes by innate immune cells, the procoagulant activity of TF is amplified up to 100-fold, resulting in clot formation with the dual role of not only preventing bleeding but also inhibiting the dissemination of the pathogen in a process termed immunothrombosis [ 3 ]. Immunothrombosis is defined as the interaction and crosstalk between the coagulation and immune system [ 4 ]. It refers to the formation of thrombi supported by immune cells and specific thrombosis-related molecules to facilitate the recognition of pathogens and damaged cells, as well as inhibiting pathogen proliferation. Both systems are individually important in maintaining homeostasis in response to infection and tissue damage [ 5 ]. The balance between the coagulation and immune pathways is essential in maintaining homeostasis, as overactivation of the coagulation pathway can lead to thrombosis and coagulopathy [ 6 ], while a hyperimmune response can cause allergic reactions, autoimmune diseases, and chronic inflammation [ 7 ].

The monocyte response to pathogens links innate immunity and coagulation. Through the induction of tissue factor (TF), these cells promote fibrin deposition that forms a physical barrier, effectively trapping pathogens and limiting their spread [ 8–10 ]. TF complexes with factor VIIa (FVIIa) and initiates blood coagulation, fibrin formation, and finally platelet and immune cell activation [ 11 ]. Fibrin has also been found to activate immune cells, allowing for further induction of thrombosis as a positive feedback mechanism of bacterial containment [ 10 ].

The process of immunothrombosis is paradoxical in SLE. Patients are susceptible to infection but also have high thrombosis risk driven by a state of chronic thromboinflammation [ 12 , 13 ]. Thromboinflammation is a process whereby platelets and the coagulation cascade can influence inflammatory responses through interactions with immune cells, endothelium, or complement system. These interactions can lead to a state of heightened inflammation resulting from thrombotic processes. This suggests an innate defect such that this hypercoagulable state characteristic of SLE may paradoxically impair the mechanism of effective immunothrombosis required for containing pathogens. While SLE research has justifiably focused on thrombotic risk, the consequential impact of this dysregulated coagulation on innate immune defence and bacterial containment remains an unexplored question.

## Immunothrombosis: TF and monocytes in host defence

TF is expressed in ∼1.5% of monocytes under normal conditions [ 14 ]. When these monocytes are exposed to lipopolysaccharide (LPS), TF expression is rapidly expanded in a nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) dependent manner [ 14–16 ]. TF occurs on cells in two conditions: encrypted TF on unperturbed cells and de-encrypted TF on damaged/activated cells [ 17 ]. The procoagulant activity of TF is low at resting conditions, but damage or activation of cells increases the procoagulant activity levels of TF [ 18 ]. This activation ensures that coagulation is spatially confined to sites of injury or infection, limiting systemic thrombosis while enabling localised fibrin deposition and immune cell recruitment.

Upon activation, TF and FVIIa form a complex that activates protease-activated receptor-2 (PAR-2), which enhances antibacterial toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signalling and reduces antiviral responses via TLR3 [ 19 , 20 ]. On the other hand, PAR-1 is activated by thrombin and can promote TF, creating a feedback loop where TF and PAR-1 amplify each other’s activity [ 21 ]. In TF deficient mice, a lack of myeloid TF increased chemokine ligand 1 (CXCL1) expression, suggesting that myeloid TF restrains macrophage recruitment to sites of infection [ 22 ]. Myeloid TF is needed to control bacterial growth, as shown in a study where a lack of TF expression resulted in less fibrin deposition, while creating an anti-inflammatory and reduced apoptotic macrophage phenotype [ 23 ]. While this role of bacterial dissemination and growth was not seen in a global TF deficient mouse model, it suggests this effect may be unique to myeloid cells [ 24 ]. This cell-specific effect implies that the antibacterial function of TF is compartmentalized within the immune system and may depend on localized interactions between monocytes, platelets, and endothelial cells. Furthermore, low TF-expressing endotoxemia mouse models have been shown to reduce IL-6 expression and increase survival, while this does not occur when thrombin, PAR-1 or PAR-2 is inhibited, suggesting that this antibacterial mechanism is a TF-dependent pathway [ 25 ].

A key link between inflammation and thrombosis is the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), whereby activated neutrophils release DNA, histones, and antimicrobial proteins into the extracellular space in a procoagulant and prothrombotic web [ 26 , 27 ]. These NETs can activate monocytes to express both TF and IFNs, causing fibrin generation and vascular damage ( Fig. 1 ) [ 28 ]. NETs also skew monocytes toward pathogenic antigen-presenting phenotypes, allowing the activation of other immune cells [ 29 ]. The increased production of IFNs by NET-activated monocytes can induce a positive feedback loop, allowing more NET formation and thrombosis.

Circulating monocytes are adaptable and dynamic cells, varying in phenotype and genotype based on their location [ 30 ]. Initially defined by CX3CR1 levels, classical monocytes are CX3CR1low CCR2high and characterized as a short-lived population that migrates to inflammatory sites via C-C chemokine receptor type 2 (CCR2)/monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (MCP-1) interactions [ 31 , 32 ]. MCP-1 is released during inflammatory responses. It binds CCR2 on monocytes, promoting chemotaxis and activating the Janus Kinases (JAK)–Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription (STAT) signalling cascade. This migration is essential for the early innate immune response, positioning classical monocytes as first responders in tissue injury and infection. Classical monocytes that remain in circulation have been shown to either differentiate into intermediate monocytes or undergo death by apoptosis or necrosis. Similarly, intermediate monocytes can either migrate out of the circulation or remain in circulation and differentiate into non-classical monocytes [ 32 ].

Intermediate and non-classical monocytes are characterized by high CX3CR1 but low CCR2 expression. They are described as a patrolling or a resident monocyte subset and demonstrate CX3CR1-dependent recruitment to resting tissues [ 33 , 34 ]. Intermediate monocytes express the highest levels of antigen presentation-related markers and have been shown to secrete pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines upon TLR stimulation [ 35–37 ]. Intermediate monocytes (CD14high CD16high) are expanded in chronic infections and are critical for antibacterial defence through TF-dependent immunothrombosis, phagocytosis, and antigen presentation through the CD16 receptor [ 37–39 ]. For example, these cells are expanded in drug-resistant and drug-sensitive tuberculosis (TB), suggesting that classical monocytes have migrated to the site of infection or mature into intermediates to enhance the antibacterial response within the circulation [ 40 ]. This expansion may also reflect an adaptable reprogramming of monocyte function aimed at sustaining antimicrobial activity during prolonged infection. CD16+ monocytes illustrate a mature phenotype and are associated with cell-to-cell adhesion, cell trafficking, proliferation, and differentiation [ 41 ]. Their high motility and adhesive capacity may facilitate interaction with platelets and endothelial cells during immunothrombotic responses.

In the context of SLE, however, this otherwise protective intermediate subset appears to undergo pathogenic reprogramming, contributing to autoimmunity, hyperinflammation, and vascular complications. The precise mechanisms driving this dysregulation remain poorly understood but may involve chronic exposure to type I interferons, persistent immune complexes, and altered cytokine milieus that skew differentiation and function. Elucidating how classical and intermediate monocytes are reprogrammed in SLE, and how this impacts their roles in immunothrombosis, pathogen defence, and tissue homeostasis, represents a critical avenue for understanding disease pathogenesis and identifying potential therapeutic targets.

## The IFNAR-TF axis is a molecular switch governing immunothrombosis

Type I interferon (IFN) signalling has been increasingly recognized as an essential driver of thrombosis, with animal studies providing compelling mechanistic evidence. Both IFN-alpha/beta receptor 1 knockout ( Ifnar−/− ) and TIR domain-containing adaptor inducing interferon-β knockout ( Trif−/− ) mice are protected from thrombotic complications, exhibiting reduced thrombin generation, fibrin deposition, and systemic coagulopathy [ 42 ]. Interestingly, structural homology has been reported between IFNAR1 and transmembrane TF, showing that crosstalk between IFN-driven inflammatory pathways and TF-mediated coagulation exists [ 43 ] ( Fig. 1 ).

A recent mechanistic discovery by Manoharan et al. reveals a previously unrecognized immunomodulatory function of TF beyond its canonical role in coagulation [ 44 ]. The study demonstrates that TF directly binds to IFNAR1 via its C-terminal fibronectin III domain, antagonizing IFNAR1 signalling and thereby preventing spontaneous, sterile inflammation. In TF-deficient podocytes, loss of this interaction triggered the constitutive activation of the type I IFN and JAK–STAT pathways, leading to upregulation of interferon-stimulated genes (ISG), cytokine release, and glomerular inflammation. Additionally, siRNA-mediated TF depletion in THP1 monocytes led to increased ISG expression, an effect which was abrogated by concurrent IFNAR1 silencing. This shows that the antagonization of IFNAR1 by TF occurs in monocytes, suggesting this axis is found in the circulatory immune system. It is important to note, however, that the direct validation in SLE monocytes has not yet been confirmed.

Pharmacological inhibition of JAK1/2 with ruxolitinib abrogated these effects. Conversely, overexpression or the use of recombinant TF suppressed IFN-α–induced interferon-stimulated response element (ISRE) activity and ISG expression in both THP1 monocytes and podocytes, confirming the role of TF as a negative regulator of IFNAR1 signalling. Furthermore, when podocytes were stimulated with LPS or poly(I:C), the heteromeric complex of TF-IFNAR1 is lost, allowing for both TF and IFNAR1 to signal and induce an immunothrombotic response. Collectively, these findings establish TF as a molecular ‘thrombo-inflammatory switch’ that integrates coagulation and innate immune signalling, functioning either in isolation or in coordination to drive inflammatory or procoagulant states depending on the context.

## SLE monocytes: interferon dominance and TF dysregulation

Monocyte enrichment has been illustrated in SLE, but their subsets vary depending on disease severity. While some studies show that classical monocytes are upregulated in active SLE, other studies describe an increase in CD16+ monocyte frequency in SLE patients [ 45 , 46 ]. This discrepancy may reflect differences in disease stage, treatment exposure, or the dynamic constant redistributive nature of monocytes between the circulation and inflamed tissues. The enrichment of non-classical monocytes correlates with levels of anti-dsDNA antibodies in SLE serum, while also playing roles in antigen presentation, adaptive immune cell activation, and IL-1β secretion [ 46–48 ]. This correlation implies that non-classical monocytes may contribute to autoantibody generation by facilitating antigen presentation to autoreactive T cells. In addition, their capacity to secrete IL-1β links them to endothelial activation and vascular injury, halslmarks of lupus-associated vasculopathy.

Monocytes are a significant key contributor to the interferon signature in SLE and have been shown to exhibit a 3-fold higher ISG transcript burden compared to T and B cells, with this inflammatory phenotype being uniquely sustained over time [ 49 ]. This phenotype in SLE monocytes becomes critically relevant to host defence in light of the recent mechanistic finding by Manoharan et al. described above [ 44 ]. Thus, in the context of SLE, the TF-IFNAR axis may be maladaptively altered. Such maladaptation may decouple the normal coordination between coagulation and innate immunity, leading to impaired immunothrombosis and inadequate bacterial containment.

As mentioned previously, IFNs are a key pathogenic factor in SLE [ 50 ]. Type I IFN signalling upregulates suppressor of cytokine signalling 1 (SOCS1), which is a negative regulator of NF-κB and inhibitor of JAK-STAT signalling [ 51 ]. The upregulation of SOCS1 provides a feedback brake that normally limits excessive inflammation, yet in SLE, this regulatory pathway inadvertently suppresses NF-κB-dependent transcription of genes such as TF. Consistent with the findings of Manoharan et al., heightened IFN/IFNAR signalling is associated with reduced TF expression, given that TF induction is NF-κB dependent. This creates a paradox in SLE, where patients display persistently elevated circulating type I IFNs [ 52 ], while also exhibiting standard TF levels [ 53 ] ( Fig. 2 ). Nonetheless, this imbalance may predispose patients to defective antimicrobial immunity while maintaining a risk for thrombotic complications under inflammatory stress.

Thus, the chronically elevated ISGs, which are characteristic of SLE monocytes ‘lock’ the TF switch into an inhibitory position, impairing the capacity of monocytes to mount timely immunothrombotic responses required for effective bacterial clearance. SLE patients with antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), a condition with an increased risk of blood clots, consistently demonstrate reduced type I interferon (IFN) signatures and markedly elevated TF in their monocytes and periphery, when compared to SLE patients without APS [ 54–56 ]. These findings suggest an inverse relationship between TF expression and IFN activity, supporting the mechanistic model proposed by Manoharan et al., in which TF functions as a negative regulator of IFNAR1 signalling.

Taken together, these findings illustrate that SLE monocytes occupy a uniquely dysregulated immunological niche. Rather than fulfilling their physiological roles in pathogen defence, vascular repair, and immune regulation, SLE monocytes become hyperactivated yet functionally compromised, simultaneously driving autoimmunity and sustaining inflammation [ 57 , 58 ]. Importantly, therapeutic strategies targeting type I IFN signalling, such as JAK inhibition or IFNAR1 blockade by anifrolumab, demonstrate that modulation of this axis can attenuate both inflammatory and thrombotic processes, providing proof-of-concept for interventions that recalibrate monocyte function. Integrating clinical, molecular, and single-cell data from treated patients could clarify how such interventions reshape the TF–IFNAR network in vivo .

## TF dysregulation in SLE: insights from APS

Although SLE patients display similar TF antigen and activity levels to healthy controls at baseline, they exhibit increased tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI) free antigen and decreased TFPI activity, suggesting that the TF signalling pathway has impaired regulation in circulatory cells. In fact, when TF activity to TFPI ratios were performed, an increased ratio was seen in SLE patients compared to controls, further suggesting that TF activity is not being inhibited to the same extent. Therefore, a pro-coagulant phenotype with increased TF signalling and downstream processes may exist. Furthermore, TFPI activity is increased in those with thrombosis when compared to SLE patients without thrombosis, suggesting SLE patients without thrombosis have a blunted inhibitory capacity of TFPI [ 53 ]. Overall, these findings indicate that despite comparable baseline TF levels, SLE patients exhibit dysfunctional regulation of the TF–TFPI axis, leading to insufficient inhibition of TF activity and a predisposition toward a procoagulant state that may contribute to thrombotic risk.

However, the relationship between TF and IFNAR1 in SLE appears highly context dependent. TF expression is elevated in SLE patients with active arthritis yet decreased in those receiving non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) therapy or with a history of thrombosis [ 59 ]. Such findings suggest dysregulation of TF–IFNAR1 complex formation, in which disengagement of the complex may drive simultaneous overactivation of both IFN and TF pathways. This raises the possibility of a dichotomous role for TF. In resting or homeostatic states, TF–IFNAR1 engagement restrains both IFN signalling and inflammation, whereas in disease settings such as SLE, disruption of this complex may lead to hyperinflammation or hypercoagulation.

On the other hand, in SLE patients with secondary APS, TF expression and its signalling pathway are altered ( Table 1 ). APS is a multisystemic autoimmune disorder characterized by the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies (aPLs) in circulation, which play key roles in thrombosis or pregnancy loss [ 60 ]. The best characterized aPLs include anti-cardiolipin/β2-glycoprotein I antibodies (anti-CL/β2-GPI), and lupus anticoagulant [ 61 ]. These aPLs bind to complexes of phospholipid and plasma proteins such as β2-GPI and prothrombin which are synthesized by monocytes [ 62 ]. In SLE, β2-GPI expression is increased in patients with aPL expression [ 63 ]. Interestingly, β2-GPI expression on monocytes correlates with TF expression. Conti et al. suggest that increased β2-GPI expression allows for persistently high TF expression on monocytes, causing predisposition to prothrombotic events. Many studies suggest that the increased TF found in SLE patients with secondary APS may be due to these aPL antibodies, as anti-β2-GPI antibodies have been shown to enhance monocyte TF activity and levels of TF mRNA in a β 2 GPI-dependent manner [ 64–66 ].

A murine study that tested the cross-reactivity of monoclonal anti-CL-β 2 GPI antibody WB-6 to CL-β 2 GPI and DNA and found that WB-6 induced the expression of TF, which was not seen in other anti-DNA antibodies and was suppressed by TLR9-specific antagonists, suggests that, in this model, TF induction was dependent on the presence of DNA [ 67 ]. The study suggests that DNA stimulates the TLR9 and AIM-2 pathways, resulting in the expression of cytokines relevant to SLE pathogenesis [ 67 , 68 ]. SLE patients with thrombotic events have a higher frequency of TF+ monocytes, when compared to SLE patients without thrombotic events. Additionally, this study showed that the presence of anti-CL/β2-GPI was strongly associated with a high frequency of TF+ monocytes. Moreover, the study saw a higher frequency of TF+ monocytes in SLE compared to healthy controls, although this combined both the patients with and without thrombotic events. Separation of these groups to identify whether SLE patients without thrombotic events have different levels of TF expression compared to healthy controls would be an important step in understanding the mechanism of immunothrombosis in SLE [ 69 ]. Moreover, it would be useful to identify aPL positivity within this patient cohort, as monocytes from aPL+ patients express TF through autoantibodies (as described above) while those from SLE patients that are aPL− must engage with alternative pathways.

Plasma micro-particles (PMP) are membrane-bound vesicles, released during cell activation, which have the capacity to express TF. They can induce adhesion molecules, resulting in increased inflammation at the site of atherosclerotic plaque and subsequently favour a pro-thrombotic response. PMPs are increased in SLE compared to healthy controls and correlate with disease activity, although aPL positivity was not accounted for in this study [ 70 ]. Statins, inhibitors of cholesterol synthesis which decrease the expression of adhesion molecules, have been shown to decrease TF expression and IgG-mediated TF activity in monocytes [ 71 ]. Therefore, cholesterol signalling may play a role in immunothrombosis and bacterial containment.

Thus, SLE patients with secondary APS provide critical insights into TF regulation. Unlike the broader SLE population, APS patients exhibit reduced interferon signatures alongside markedly elevated TF levels. This relationship supports the TF-IFNAR1 switch model. In non-APS SLE patients, chronic interferon exposure may dampen TF suppression, explaining the immunothrombosis defect despite systemic hypercoagulability.

## Metabolic constraints on immunothrombosis in SLE

Emerging evidence suggests that metabolic and redox imbalances critically influence monocyte TF expression in SLE, providing a mechanistic bridge between immune dysregulation, inflammation, and thrombosis. Nutrient-derived modulators such as vitamin D and vitamin E have been shown to alter cytokine profiles and oxidative stress responses, both of which directly impact TF regulation and activity.

Vitamin D deficiency has recently been associated with the pathogenesis of SLE, as reduced vitamin D levels increase IL-6 and reduce transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β). TGF-β plays a key role in inducing Treg differentiation, but high IL-6 instead pushes a Th17 phenotype, inducing pro-inflammatory cytokines [ 72 ]. Vitamin D has also emerged as having a potential anti-thrombotic effect [ 73 ]. In a study where SLE patients with hypovitaminosis of vitamin D were treated with 1,25(OH)2D3, the active form of vitamin D, no change in monocyte TF levels was seen, although vitamin D reduced monocyte TF levels in healthy controls [ 74 ]. This may suggest that TF expression occurs through different pathways in SLE relative to healthy controls.

Ferro et al. examined the use of vitamin E as an antioxidant in APL+ patients. The study proposed that anti-β2GP1 antibodies may play a key role by stimulating monocytes to generate reactive oxygen species [ 75 ]. They found that anti-β2GP1 antibodies increased monocyte production of superoxide anion and TF in a dose-dependent manner, an effect significantly reduced by vitamin E. These findings indicate that in APL-positive patients, oxidative stress contributes to activation of the coagulation system through upregulation of monocyte TF. Collectively, these findings highlight that altered metabolic and oxidative pathways in SLE monocytes, whether driven by vitamin deficiency or by autoantibody-induced reactive oxygen species, may converge to sustain aberrant TF expression.

These nutrient- and redox-driven alterations in monocyte function suggest that metabolic reprogramming extends beyond antioxidant imbalance, implicating core bioenergetic pathways such as glycolysis in the regulation of TF expression and thromboinflammatory activity in SLE. Recent evidence also indicates that TF induction in myeloid cells is tightly coupled to glycolytic reprogramming [ 76 ]. Inhibition of glycolysis in monocyte-derived macrophages not only attenuates TF activity but also promotes fibrinolysis and prolongs the lag phase of thrombin generation. Conversely, thrombin itself appears to drive an anti-inflammatory metabolic profile, characterized by reduced expression of Jak2 and Irf7 [ 77 ]. These findings reinforce the broader principle that immune activation requires metabolic reprogramming to support effector function [ 78 ]. In fact, IFN-α has been found to induce oxidative phosphorylation in pDCs, while IFN-β was shown to blunt glycolysis in monocyte-derived macrophages [ 79 , 80 ]. This may suggest that SLE monocytes are stuck between an oxidative phosphorylated state from IFN-α, reducing the capacity for glycolysis, which is needed for TF production.

Monocytes from SLE patients exhibit profound metabolic alterations, including spontaneous nitric oxide (NO) overproduction, mitochondrial hyperpolarisation (MHP), and enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis, while SLE T cells show parallel features such as MHP, increased mitochondrial mass, and elevated intracellular Ca2+ upon activation [ 81 , 82 ]. Notably, SLE monocytes with high IFNα signatures demonstrate increased cholesterol accumulation, lipid droplet formation, and transcriptional enrichment for glycolytic and lipid metabolic pathways, phenotypes absent in IFNα-low monocytes [ 83 , 84 ]. Pharmacological blockade of glycolysis or cholesterol biosynthesis reduces proinflammatory cytokine secretion, including IL-6, suggesting that IFN-driven metabolic rewiring directly sustains inflammatory output.

The dependence of TF on glycolysis provides a potential mechanistic link to impaired TF control described in SLE, where we and others have shown defective monocyte metabolism [ 53 , 76 , 85 ]. This defective monocyte metabolism may blunt SLE monocyte’s capacity to induce, maintain, and regulate TF. Future studies employing single-cell metabolic profiling, such as Scenith [ 86 ], could delineate whether TF+ monocytes occupy distinct metabolic niches relative to TF− cells, and how chronic IFNα exposure influences mitochondrial stress and mTOR signalling. Restoring mitochondrial function through metabolic interventions—such as pyruvate or fructose-1,6-bisphosphate supplementation—has been proposed as a potential therapeutic avenue for correcting the metabolic dysfunction observed in SLE monocytes.

## Therapeutic implications and future directions

The emerging literature highlights that SLE monocytes are metabolically constrained, caught between immunothrombotic failure and thromboinflammation. On one hand, interferons drive a shift toward glycolysis that supports immunothrombotic responses, while thrombin production promotes both glycolysis and mitochondrial oxygen consumption [ 77 , 87 ]. This duality creates a pathogenic ‘metabolic trap,’ whereby attempts to mount effective antimicrobial defences may inadvertently potentiate prothrombotic pathways. Such findings raise critical questions about whether metabolic reprogramming in SLE is primarily adaptive, maladaptive, or context-dependent, and whether interventions should seek to restore balance rather than promote one pathway over another.

Therapeutically, the possibility of restoring glycolysis to improve bacterial clearance without exacerbating thrombosis is compelling but remains insufficiently validated. Preclinical models have yet to resolve whether manipulation of glycolytic flux can consistently dissociate antimicrobial function from thrombotic risk, or whether such interventions risk further destabilizing the delicate metabolic equilibrium in SLE. Importantly, this underscores a broader research gap: most studies have focused on discrete metabolic endpoints, while longitudinal and systems-level analyses omit immunometabolic outputs. This implies that studies considering clinical and metabolic endpoints together remain scarce.

The recognition of heterogeneity in monocyte metabolic phenotypes further reinforces the need for personalized approaches. Stratifying patients by metabolic profile could inform tailored therapeutic interventions—for instance, deploying glycolysis-promoting strategies in infection-prone individuals while exercising caution in those with heightened thrombotic risk. However, such stratification is currently limited by the absence of validated biomarkers capable of reliably defining these phenotypes in clinical settings.

Taken together, the literature suggests that targeting monocyte metabolism in SLE holds significant promise but also presents complex challenges. Future work must bridge mechanistic insights with translational studies, establish robust metabolic biomarkers, and carefully weigh the risks of immunometabolic modulation in a disease where infection and thrombosis coexist in precarious balance. Only through such integrative efforts can the therapeutic potential of metabolic interventions be realised in a patient-specific and clinically meaningful way.

## Outstanding questions

Despite substantial advances in defining the molecular and immunometabolic landscape of SLE monocytes, several fundamental questions remain unresolved. Foremost is the need to validate the IFNAR1–TF interaction in primary SLE monocytes and to determine how chronic type I IFN exposure alters the stoichiometry, stability, and signalling outcome of this complex in vivo . Whether defects in TF induction during infection are uniform across monocyte subsets, or confined to distinct classical, intermediate, or TF⁺ subpopulations—also remains unknown. A second major gap concerns the immunometabolic constraints that shape monocyte-dependent immunothrombosis: it is unclear how IFN-driven mitochondrial stress, glycolytic insufficiency, and lipid accumulation collectively govern TF expression, or whether metabolic interventions can restore antimicrobial TF responses without exacerbating thrombotic risk. The field also lacks single-cell or temporal analyses capable of defining the metabolic niches of TF⁺ monocytes or mapping how NETs, cholesterol signalling, and aPL antibodies intersect with monocyte metabolism to modulate TF regulation. Clinically, it is unknown whether therapies targeting JAK–STAT, IFNAR1, glycolysis, or cholesterol biosynthesis recalibrate the TF–IFNAR axis in patients, or whether specific metabolic signatures could stratify individuals at highest risk of immunothrombotic failure. Addressing these gaps will require integrative systems-level approaches combining primary human monocyte biology, advanced metabolic profiling, and longitudinal clinical phenotyping to delineate how coagulation, interferon signalling, and metabolism converge to drive infection susceptibility and thrombosis in SLE.
