# Rare case of catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome with spontaneous intracranial haemorrhage

# SUMMARY

Catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (CAPS) is a rare but severe form of antiphospholipid syndrome (APS). The syndrome manifests itself as a rapidly progressive multiorgan failure that is believed to be caused by widespread micro-thrombosis. Seldom does bleeding comanifest with thrombosis. We present a patient with APS who presented with nausea, vomiting and fatigue, and rapidly progressed into multiorgan failure before being diagnosed with CAPS. The clinical course was complicated by an atraumatic intracranial haemorrhage which demanded discontinuation of anticoagulation. The patient was treated with high dose steroid, intravenous immunoglobulin, followed by weekly rituximab infusion. Although the trigger for CAPS was not obvious during her hospital stay, she was diagnosed with acute cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection soon after discharge. In this case report, we explore the differential diagnoses of CAPS, investigate the possibility of CMV infection as a potential trigger, present the therapeutic challenges of anticoagulation and discuss the emerging use of rituximab.

# BACKGROUND

Catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (CAPS) is a rare variant of antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) that is characterised by diffuse thrombotic microangiopathy in the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies (ie, anticardiolipin antibody and anti B2-glycoprotein-I antibody). This potentially life-threatening syndrome has a mortality rate of $50\%$ and represents less than $1\%$ of all APS. Patients typically present with a rapidly progressing multi-organ failure. As opposed to large vessel thrombosis that is typically seen in APS, diffuse small vessel thrombosis dominates the clinical picture of CAPS. Widespread thrombosis can sometimes coexist with or even lead to haemorrhage, further complicating its management. Cerebral involvement is seen in around $62\%$ of patients with CAPS. Common neuropsychiatrist manifestations include transient ischaemic attack (TIA), ischaemic stroke, seizure, headache, depression, psychosis and multiple sclerosis-like disease. In a review of 250 patients with CAPS, cerebral haemorrhage was reported in only four patients. Triggering factors are recognised in approximately $50\%$ of all CAPS. However, the mechanism of how these factors trigger a cascade of prothrombotic activity leading to multiorgan involvement remains poorly understood. We present a case of a young female with APS who developed CAPS which was further complicated by a seizure and intracranial haemorrhage (ICH).

# CASE PRESENTATION

A 38-year-old woman with APS and lower extremity deep vein thrombosis (DVT) presented to the emergency department with nausea and vomiting of 10 days' duration. She reported decreased appetite, fatigue and weight loss. She had recently travelled to Albania and had encountered individuals with flu-like symptoms. Her medical history was significant for APS which was diagnosed at age 27, two first trimester spontaneous abortions, two second trimester abortions, and a lower extremity DVT treated with anticoagulation. She also had non-infective endocarditis with moderate mitral regurgitation. She had been prescribed enoxaparin for secondary prevention of thromboembolism a month prior to presentation.

On presentation, she had a fever of $38.3^{\circ}C$, tachycardia and mild hypotension. Physical examination was non-focal. Initial laboratory findings are given in table 1. She appeared to have a systemic inflammatory reaction syndrome (SIRS) with multi-organ dysfunction. Given her symptoms of nausea and vomiting, a potential gastrointestinal pathology was investigated. Neither an ultrasound nor a CT of abdomen/pelvis revealed any abnormality. A normal hepatobiliary iminodiacetic acid (HIDA) scan further ruled out acute cholecystitis. On day 2 of admission, the patient developed left-sided facial droop, left hemineglect and weakness in the left upper extremity. An urgent head CT demonstrated an acute subarachnoid and parenchymal haemorrhages in the right frontal, temporal and occipital lobes with a small right subdural haematoma (figure 1). An MRI further displayed reactive dural thickening with enhancement suggesting an underlying inflammatory process (figure 2). At this point, her platelet count was 84 bil/L, international normalised ratio (INR) was 1.4 and activated partial thromboplastin time was 56.7s. Enoxaparin was stopped immediately. Antiphospholipid antibodies: anticardiolipin IgM and IgG, beta-2 glycoprotein IgM and IgG titres were elevated, and Dilute Russell's viper venom time (DRVVT) test was consistent with presence of lupus anticoagulant. Additional labs showed an elevated D-dimer at $4846 ng/mL$ and fibrinogen at $680 mg/dL$, and normal haptoglobin. With these systemic and haematological findings, CAPS was suspected. Peripheral blood smear did not show schistocytes but showed occasional atypical lymphocytes. Given the patient's history of sick contacts in Albania, an extensive infectious workup was done. Serology was negative for acute infection by Epstein-Barr virus, cytomegalovirus (CMV), herpes simplex virus, HIV, histoplasma, Brucella or Coxiella.

# DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

## Disseminated intravascular coagulation

CAPS with systemic micro-thrombosis closely resembles disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Our patient had an increased PT and aPTT with a high D-Dimer. Her fibrinogen was elevated and did not support DIC. However, it should be noted that fibrinogen may be elevated in around $50\%$ of cases of DIC as an acute phase reactant. The remarkable response to high dose steroids and intravenous immunoglobulin in our patient suggested that CAPS was more likely.

## Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura

Patient presented with acute thrombocytopenia, acute kidney injury and anaemia. Furthermore, the patient also had a fever and developed neurological changes that would form the classic presentation of thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), also known as the 'TTP pentad'. However, there was no evidence of haemolysis with normal serum haptoglobin and no schistocytes on a peripheral blood smear.

## Heparin induced thrombocytopenia

Heparin induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) should always be considered in patients who have received heparin for more than 5 days (or sooner if exposed to heparin in the past) who develop acute thrombocytopenia and skin necrosis. Platelet factor four is a very sensitive test to detect HIT, which was negative in our patient.

# TREATMENT

Patient was treated with intravenous immunoglobulin dosed at $0.4 g/kg/day$ and intravenous dexamethasone $10 mg$ once a day. Plasmapheresis was considered. However, due to the risk of worsening bleeding and the need for invasive lines, she opted for medical management. After 3 days of initiation of treatment, an improvement of her renal function, liver enzymes and neurological functions were observed. Intravenous immunoglobulin and dexamethasone were stopped. She was then transitioned to prednisone $40 mg$ once a day. The patient showed significant neurological improvement, although she continued to have persistent headaches and a depressed mood.

# OUTCOME AND FOLLOW-UP

After discharge, the patient received rituximab infusion at a dose of $375 mg/m^2$ body surface area each week for 4 weeks. Steroids were gradually tapered and stopped. Two weeks following discharge, the patient was readmitted to the hospital with fever, myalgia and fatigue. Her hepatic enzymes were elevated. An acute CMV infection was diagnosed by an elevated CMV IgM antibody titre of $114 AU/mL$. She was treated supportively without any complications and had a complete recovery. After complete resolution of ICH, lifelong anticoagulation was recommended. However, given recent ICH, she decided to avoid anticoagulation. Instead, she was started on low dose aspirin. Patient continues to be closely monitored.

# DISCUSSION

CAPS may occur in both primary and secondary APS. Well-known factors that increase the risk of thromboses like immobilisation, obesity, nephrotic syndrome and hereditary coagulopathies (protein C/S deficiency, antithrombin III deficiency, Factor V Leiden or prothrombin mutation) do not seem to be associated with the pathogenesis of CAPS. Triggers are recognised in almost $50\%$ of the patients and include infection, endothelial dysfunction (trauma, surgery), malignancies and obstetric conditions. Pathogenesis of CAPS involves immune activation leading to SIRS and small vessels thrombosis. Infection is the most common trigger for CAPS, and therefore an appropriate infectious workup is indicated in all patients. Our patient did not have a recognisable trigger during her initial hospital stay that could have led to CAPS. However, 2 weeks after discharge, she was diagnosed with CMV infection with a significant IgM titre. Hence, it was hypothesised that an early CMV infection or its incubation could have triggered the CAPS. There are more than 10 cases of CMV associated APS that have been reported in the literature. CMV associated thrombosis has also been reported with or without APS as well. Molecular mimicry by the virus has been proposed to be the major mechanism for immune activation that leads to APS, while vascular endothelial injury has been proposed to be the cause of thrombosis.

Organ-systems that are involved in CAPS are illustrated in table 2. Common manifestations include fever, fatigue, acute renal failure, pulmonary oedema, acute respiratory distress syndrome, stroke and TIA. TIA can present as amaurosis fugax, paraesthesia, ataxia, motor weakness, vertigo and global ischaemia. Less common cerebral manifestations of APS include seizure, chronic headache, chorea, transverse myelitis and multiple sclerosis-like picture. Our patient had cerebral, renal, hepatic and cardiac derangements without significant pulmonary or cutaneous involvement. Evidence of micro-thrombosis is usually not found unless a tissue biopsy is performed.

Anticoagulation is recommended for secondary prevention in patients with APS to prevent recurrent thromboembolic events. Primary prevention with anticoagulation in patients with antiphospholipid antibodies without APS is not recommended. Use of warfarin with a target INR of 2.5 is recommended for patients with venous thromboembolism while the consensus on target INR is lacking for patients with arterial thromboembolism. For such patients, combining warfarin with a target INR of 2-3 with low dose aspirin is agreed on by most experts. However, guidelines by the 13th International Congress on APS recommended a target INR $>3$. Studies have not been conducted comparing direct oral anticoagulant with higher intensity warfarin. It has been found that an increase in INR by 1 in patients with APS increases bleeding risk by $42\%$. It should be noted that bleeding diathesis, although not a typical manifestation of APS, occurs in $10\%$ of patients. Bleeding is seen in patients with anticoagulation, capillaritis, thrombocytopenia or antiprothrombin antibodies. Our patient had thrombocytopenia and inflammatory changes in the meninges and was on anticoagulants. It was evident that she was at a higher risk of bleeding. Since the mainstay of therapy for CAPS is anticoagulation, patients with bleeding manifestations pose a therapeutic challenge. Anticoagulation is held in cases where the haemorrhagic risk is felt to be more than the thrombotic risk. Currently, there is no consensus on the safety of resuming anticoagulation after such life-threatening bleed. Similarly, there is a lack of data on when to resume anticoagulation or in what dosage.

High-dose steroid of $500-1000 mg$ of methylprednisolone daily for at least 3 days is recommended for CAPS. Intravenous immunoglobulin infusion and plasma exchange are recommended for severe CAPS. The use of rituximab has also been suggested, especially to control non-criteria APS manifestations like thrombocytopenia, nephropathy, cardiac valve disease, cognitive dysfunction, skin ulcers, diffuse pulmonary haemorrhage and refractory CAPS. A study done by the European Forum of APS using a CAPS registry indicated that of the 20 patients that were given rituximab, 15 patients showed a positive response. However, the number of patients taking rituximab was too low to determine its effectiveness or safety profile.[13] It is believed that rituximab reduces cytokine production by reducing B cells and minimises the systemic inflammatory response.[14] Despite treatment with high dose steroid, intravenous immunoglobulin, plasma exchange and immunomodulators like rituximab, CAPS has shown to have high mortality that approaches $50\%$.

# Learning points

Catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (CAPS) represents less than $1\%$ of all antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) but is a life-threatening syndrome which has a mortality rate of $50\%$.
Triggering factors can be identified in $60\%$ of all CAPS. The most common trigger is an infection. There is growing evidence that viral infections like Epstein Barr virus and cytomegalovirus can trigger CAPS.
The syndrome manifests as a rapidly progressive multiorgan failure caused by widespread micro-thrombosis. Rarely, bleeding can comanifest along with thrombosis. Anticoagulated patients are at a higher risk of bleeding including a higher risk of haemorrhagic stroke.
There are no guidelines of resuming anticoagulation after APS-associated bleeding diathesis. More studies are needed to support the safety of resuming anticoagulation in APS after major bleeding.
Rituximab has been shown to be effective in the treatment of refractory APS and non-criteria APS manifestations. However, larger studies are necessary to further demonstrate its effectiveness and safety.
