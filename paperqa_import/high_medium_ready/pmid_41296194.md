# Abstract

Purpose of Review An international multi-disciplinary initiative resulted in the development of 2023 ACR/EULAR Antiphospholipid Syndrome (APS) Classification Criteria to identify patients with high likelihood of APS for research. Phase I/II resulted in 27 candidate criteria organized into six clinical and laboratory domains. Here, we summarize early Phase III efforts to better define and structure candidate criteria within clinical and laboratory domains.

Recent Findings Using comprehensive literature reviews and expert consensus, domain subcommittees developed definitions for candidate criteria. Prevalence information was incorporated when available. Definitions were finalized and approved by the Steering Committee for future use during real-world case collection (derivation cohort), multi-criteria decision analysis, and validation. Clinical domain items defined were: (a) macrovascular thrombosis (venous thromboembolism including superficial venous thrombosis, arterial thrombosis, and transient ischemic attack) and associated provoking risk factors; (b) microvascular disease (livedo racemosa, livedoid vasculopathy, antiphospholipid-antibody-nephropathy, diffuse alveolar hemorrhage, cardiac microthrombosis, adrenal hemorrhage, and acute ischemic encephalopathy); (c) pregnancy morbidity (pre-fetal death, fetal death, and pre-eclampsia and placental insufficiency with severe features); (d) cardiac valve disease (thickening or vegetation); and (e) thrombocytopenia. Laboratory domain items defined were coagulation-based functional assay (lupus anticoagulant) and solid phase-based assays (anticardiolipin antibody IgG/M and anti- $\beta_{2}$ -Glycoprotein-I antibody IgG/M).

Summary Based on comprehensive literature review and Steering Committee consensus, we defined and structured APS clinical and laboratory domains. Preliminary definitions were subsequently evaluated and confirmed in late Phase III using the derivation cohort and multicriteria decision analysis prior to validation the 2023 ACR/EULAR APS Classification Criteria.

# Introduction

Antiphospholipid Syndrome (APS) is a systemic autoimmune disease characterized by thrombosis and/or pregnancy morbidity in patients with persistent antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL). Until recently, classification of APS for clinical trials and research studies was based on clinical and

laboratory criteria described in the Revised Sapporo Classification Criteria [1-3]. Given the limitations of the Revised Sapporo APS Classification Criteria [4] an international effort, jointly supported by the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) and European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR), developed and published the 2023 ACR/EULAR APS Classification Criteria [5, 6].

<!-- page 2 -->
The new APS classification criteria development involved a four-phase methodology [5-8]. We have published the results of the needs assessment survey and the first two phases [4] resulting in candidate criteria hierarchically organized into additive and independent domains for APS classification (Table 1). Here, we summarize the first part of our Phase III efforts to define and structure clinical and laboratory domains, using data-driven and consensus-based rationale.

# Methods

After the completion of Phase I/II, the overall Phase III goals were to: (a) agree on precise definitions of the 27 candidate criteria from Phase I/II for their consistent application (Phase III-A/B; reported here); (b) further reduce the number of candidate criteria using expert consensus and real-world patient scenarios and finalize the classification criteria entry criteria (Phase III-C [9]); and (c) assign weights to candidate criteria to determine a threshold score above which cases would be consistently classified as APS (Phase III-D [10]).

# Establishment of Domain Subcommittee

Based on decisions made during Phase I/II [4], the Steering Committee (SC) (see 'Acknowledgements' section) established Domain Subcommittee (DSc) to interpret the literature and provide expert opinion on issues related to the domain items. Domain subcommittees included SC members with representation from Europe, North America, and South America.

# Phase I/II Results Review

Domain subcommittees reviewed and discussed the needs assessment survey and mean Phase II item reduction survey scores [4]. In Phase II, using their individual clinical judgment, our collaborators were asked to assess each item generated in Phase I based on the specificity of each feature in differentiating APS from other similar conditions (using a Likert scale $[-5$ to $+5]$ , with $[-5]$ extremely against APS and $[+5]$ extremely specific for APS); eventually items deemed of low specificity were eliminated [4].

# Phase III Literature Review and Domain Subcommittee Discussions

Using available literature, published definitions when available, expert opinion and consensus discussions, the DSc proposed definitions for candidate criteria identified during Phase I/II. For the clinical definitions, subcommittees reviewed: (a) whether any known association existed for the candidate clinical criteria with aPL and criteria prevalence in aPL-positive individuals; (b) if other "more" or "equally" likely etiologies besides APS existed; and (c) which venous thromboembolism (VTE) and cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factors, relevant during Phase I/II, should be included. # Results

# Phase I/II Results Review (details Published Elsewhere [4])

We used both consensus- and evidence-based methods (e.g. SC surveys, literature review) to generate a comprehensive list of candidate criteria (Phase I), which were reduced using systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and expert consensus and organized into domains by specificity for APS (Phase II). Phase I/II resulted in 27 candidate criteria hierarchically organized into six additive and independent domains. Candidate clinical and laboratory criteria identified during Phase I/II are shown in Table 1 [4].

For clinical candidate criteria, Phase II surveys demonstrated high survey scores (i.e., high specificity for APS) for items such as arterial and venous thrombosis in the absence of concomitant non-aPL CVD and VTE risk factors, microvascular disease items, obstetric events, cardiac valve disease, and thrombocytopenia. Laboratory criteria Phase II results demonstrated higher scores for "persistent" LA tests, compared to transient (or single) LA test positivity [4].

Similarly, Phase II survey results demonstrated higher survey scores for: (a) higher aPL versus lower titer levels; (b) "persistent" aPL (compared to transient aPL determination); and (c) IgG isotype (compared to IgM) [4].

# Literature Review Summary and Domain Subcommittee Discussions for Defining and Structuring Domains

Here we summarize the association between each candidate criteria and aPL positivity or APS, based on literature review, to develop definitions for use in the clinical and laboratory domains. Within each domain, the SC agreed that only the highest specificity criteria item should be scored, consistent with past classification criteria methodology. In addition, the SC agreed on the need for "entry criteria", i.e., minimum criteria required to identify the relevant patient population to whom the classification criteria would be applied. Table 2 includes critical discussions by the DSc and SC related to structuring domains and defining candidate criteria items (final published definitions are included in Table 3) [5].

# Clinical Domains

# 1. Macrovascular:

Venous thromboembolism and arterial thrombosis have strongly positive association with aPL positivity [11-13]. In a large international registry of 804 patients with positive aPL (APS ACTION), 568 $(71\%)$ of patients had a history of any vascular event ( $61\%$ had venous events and $53\%$ had arterial events) [14]. Based on literature review, VTE most commonly includes deep vein thrombosis (DVT), with clot formation typically beginning in a deep calf vein and propagating proximally; VTE can include pulmonary embolism (PE), as well as thrombosis/occlusion of other areas including splanchnic, renal veins, cerebral, retinal and upper extremity venous circulation [15]. Superficial vein thrombosis (SVT), a less severe form of VTE than DVT and PE, is common in the general population and typically associated with varicose veins, but is inconsistently correlated with aPL or APS [15, 16]. While arterial thrombosis definitions are well-accepted to include thromboses and/or embolic events in coronary arteries, cerebral arteries, or peripheral/splanchnic/retinal artery; organ infarcts (e.g., kidney, liver, or spleen) in the absence of visualized thrombus are also included in the definition [17, 18]. Literature review demonstrated that transient ischemic attack (TIA) definitions are not well-defined and lacking data regarding its relevance in APS [19, 20]. Recent international guidelines supported Phase II survey results demonstrating the importance of risk stratification of venous and arterial thrombotic events in the context of underlying CVD or 'provoking' VTE risk factors [21-23].

Skin lesions related to livedoid vasculopathy, secondary to thrombus formation in small blood vessels, are distinct from livedo reticularis or racemosa, characterized by purpuric macules and papules, cutaneous nodules, and/or painful ulcerations on the lower legs and feet, and are less common but also linked to APS [28, 29].

aPL-Nephropathy: Estimates of the true prevalence of aPL- nephropathy are limited by inconsistent definitions, statistical heterogeneity, and requirement for pathologic confirmation of the diagnosis. Biopsy-proven aPL-associated nephropathy developed in $\sim 3 - 4\%$ of aPL-positive patients in one large cohort [30]. While a large international cohort of 755 aPL-positive patients found aPL-nephropathy in 30 individuals $(4\%)$ [14], others estimate aPL-nephropathy in up to $39\%$ of aPL-positive individuals [31]. As our literature review and meta-analysis (27) also supported the association of nephropathy in aPL-positive patients and highlighted the lack of standardized terminology and definitions, the pathology domain subcommittee evaluated and updated the Revised Sapporo Classification Criteria definition of aPL-nephropathy, stratified as acute and chronic lesions (published separately [32]). We describe that chronic aPL-nephropathy is typically characterized as slowly progressive kidney disease with proteinuria, microscopic hematuria, and/or hypertension; given that biopsy is not always feasible in APS patients, it is also important to incorporate clinical and laboratory parameters to define aPL nephropathy as part of the classification criteria. Our findings also demonstrated that over $80\%$ of pathologists indicated high confidence in diagnosing aPL nephropathy in patients with concomitant SLE; while other causes of TMA-mimicking lesions (e.g. hyalin thrombi related to intraluminal immune complex deposits) should be differentiated from TMA lesions, acute TMA lesions in patients with lupus nephritis strongly raise suspicion for aPL-N in the setting of positive aPL. Given that $30 - 40\%$ of SLE patients may have positive aPL serologies [33]the importance of identifying aPL-N in patients with SLE is relevant.

Pulmonary hemorrhage: Diffuse alveolar hemorrhage (DAH) is characterized by leakage of red blood cells from alveolar capillaries into the intra-alveolar space [34-36]. The prevalence of DAH was $2\%$ in a large case series of primary APS patients [35] and $12\%$ in the international catastrophic APS registry [36]. However, prevalence has been difficult to estimate due to non-specific clinical and radiographic findings, and inconsistent ability to perform invasive diagnostic testing, i.e. bronchoscopy [35, 36]. Pulmonary capillaritis with or without microthrombosis has

been detected in approximately half of lung pathology specimens from aPL-positive patients with DAH [34].

Myocardial disease: Myocardial disease in aPL-positive patients may occur secondary to coronary thromboembolism, accelerated atherosclerosis, or microvascular thrombosis with a normal coronary vascular bed confirmed by angiography [37, 38]. The few studies estimating cardiac microvascular disease frequency in APS provide varied estimates. In catastrophic APS, myocardial infarction was demonstrated in up to $6\%$ of patients [39]. Studies using echocardiogram and computed tomography detected abnormalities in myocardial perfusion in up to $30 - 40\%$ of primary APS patients [40]. In primary APS patients undergoing cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), microvascular myocardial ischemic disease suggested by late gadolinium enhancement was present in 8/27 $(29\%)$ patients, significantly higher than healthy controls, although only 3/27 $(11\%)$ had a typical pattern of transmural myocardial infarction [41]. In a study using stress cardiac MRI to evaluate silent myocardial ischemia and fibrosis, 44 patients with APS had highly reduced myocardial perfusion reserve index compared to 44 age-and sex- matched healthy controls, irrespective of the presence of late gadolinium enhancement. Specific MRI expert consensus guidelines used to define myocardial disease are outlined in Tables 2 and 3 [42].

Adrenal hemorrhage or microthrombosis: Loss of adrenal function due to bilateral adrenal vein thrombosis, resulting in hemorrhagic infarction, may occur in association with aPL positivity and is reported in $13\%$ of cases of catastrophic APS [43, 44]. Adrenal hemorrhage secondary to APS may be challenging to diagnose due to atypical symptoms; these may include hyponatremia, abdominal pain, fever, altered mental status [45]. Half of patients with adrenal hemorrhage have a history of venous or arterial thrombosis [46].

Acute ischemic encephalopathy: Acute ischemic encephalopathy characterized clinically by diffuse brain dysfunction, confusion, disorientation, hyperreflexia, and asymmetrical quadriparesis, was found in $1\%$ of the 1000 APS patients included in the EuroPhospholipid Project [39]. Literature review demonstrated lack of data regarding specificity of acute ischemic encephalopathy, as well as inconsistencies in defining acute ischemic encephalopathy.

# 4.Cardiac Valve:

Cardiac valve disease, i.e., valvular thickening or vegetation, may be a frequent feature in aPL-positive SLE patients, occurring in up to $43\%$ [63]. Furthermore, aPL-positivity is associated with a significant 3-fold increased risk of valve disease in SLE patients [62]. In primary APS patients, cardiac valve disease was estimated to occur in $10\%$ to greater than $60\%$ of patients, the latter being found in studies using transesophageal echocardiogram [47], with mitral valve thickening being the most frequent abnormality [63-65]. However, prevalence may be overestimated based on the low threshold for identifying valve thickening. However, valvular lesions in primary APS patients are significantly more prevalent than in healthy individuals [66-68] associated with high risk of arterial embolic events [63, 69] and may progress to cardiac failure requiring valve replacement [70]. Vegetations, which can be found in $25\%$ of APS patients [71] (i.e., Libman-Sacks endocarditis) are usually small (less than $4\mathrm{mm}$ ) verrucae, composed of sterile immune complexes, hematoxylin bodies, and/or platelet thrombi [72]. These irregular nodules are typically found on the atrial side of the mitral valve, followed by the vascular side of the aortic valve [73]. Specific echocardiographic guidelines used to define valvular thickening and vegetation are provided in Tables 2 and 3 [74, 75].

# 5. Hematology:

Thrombocytopenia is a common $(30 - 50\%)$ APS hematological manifestation, with estimated prevalence varying depending on the platelet cut-off level used [14, 73, 76]. In patients with SLE, aPL positivity is associated with a 2.5-fold increased risk of thrombocytopenia, varying by aPL profile [77]. Thrombocytopenia is typically reported as 'mild to moderate' in studies of aPL positivity, but is relevant as it is potentially associated with up to 3-fold risk of thrombotic events, obstetric mortality or all-cause death [78-80]. While the correlation of aPL

and idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP) has been examined, the frequency and clinical implications are still unknown [16, 81]. Although rare and with limited data published specifically on this topic, severe thrombocytopenia, often defined as $< 20 \times 10^{9} / \mathrm{L}$ , is more frequently due to other etiologies, such as ITP, aplastic anemia, certain drug-induced thrombocytopenias, and consumptive coagulopathies (i.e. disseminated intravascular coagulation, thrombotic thrombocytopenia purpura, hemolytic uremic syndrome and severe sepsis) [82-88] and less likely to be associated with aPL positivity alone. While further research is needed, a recent meta-analysis of aPL studies in patients with SLE confirmed a lack of association between aPL and thrombocytopenia defined by a low threshold of platelet count of $50 \times 10^{9} / \mathrm{L}$ or below, in comparison to a significant association with a higher platelet count threshold of $100 \times 10^{9} / \mathrm{L}$ [77].

# Laboratory Domains

# Lupus Anticoagulant

A positive LA test is considered the strongest predictor of aPL-related thrombosis and pregnancy morbidity [81, 89, 90]. Although isolated LA positivity is associated with a lower incidence of a first thrombotic event compared to triple aPL positivity (6% vs. 37% at 10 years) [91-93] several studies also demonstrate the important role of isolated LA positivity alone with negative anticardiolipin antibody (aCL) and anti- $\beta_{2}$ -glycoprotein-I (a $\beta_{2}$ GPI) [14, 94]. Similarly, both single (aCL or a $\beta_{2}$ GPI) and double (aCL and a $\beta_{2}$ GPI) aPL positivity are associated with aPL-related clinical events; however: (a) the association is weaker compared to LA- or triple-aPL positive patients [3]; and (b) the likelihood of false positivity is higher, especially with lower titers [95]. Based on external quality assessment studies, high rates of false-positive or false-negative LA test results exist [96].

A high titer, single positive aPL determination by solid phase assays may not require repeat testing after 12 weeks as the results are highly likely to be persistent [97, 98]; single LA positivity may also be relevant when repeat testing is unavailable or uninterpretable, e.g., after initiation of anticoagulation.

# Anticardiolipin Antibody and Anti- $\beta_{2}$ Glycoprotein-I Antibody IgG/M

Moderate-to-high titers of aCL IgG/M (40 GPL/MPL or 99th percentile) or moderate-to-high titers (99th percentile) of aβ2GPI IgG/IgM correlate more strongly with aPL-related clinical events than do lower titers [3]. Unfortunately, the

<!-- page 11 -->
definition of "moderate-to-high" titer has not been well established; in theory, each laboratory should calculate the aCL/aβ2GPI positivity cut-off (above which test is regarded as positive) based on the 99th percentile of at least 120 healthy donor samples [97]. Calculation of in-house cutoff values is generally not feasible, and instead, the assay manufacturers' recommended threshold for aPL-positivity is widely used by laboratories [99]which reduces variability between cut-offs.

Low titer aCL/aβ2GPI is often transiently positive [100, 101]; and several studies have suggested that aCL/aβ2GPI≥40U, compared to lower titers, correlate more strongly with aPL-related clinical events [63, 102–105]. However, inter-laboratory and inter-method variability does not allow comparison between numerical values [93, 98, 100, 106]. Accordingly, due to the lack of strong data, the subcommittee agreed that there is currently no single numeric cut-off value above which one can discriminate between low and moderate-to-high aPL levels. The cut-off value of 40U, as indicated by the Revised Sapporo Criteria, applies to aCL IgG/IgM measured by standardized ELISA; and it was emphasized that this definition is based on the best available evidence until an international consensus is reached [98]. Nevertheless, it is well accepted that the higher the titer, the higher the risk of aPL-related clinical manifestations [106, 107].

Studies evaluating IgG versus IgM isotypes demonstrated higher specificity of aCL/a $\beta_{2}$ GPI IgG than IgM isotype for thrombosis and APS [99, 108]. Isolated IgM positivity is frequently debated regarding the association with APS, and IgM isotype is considered not as strongly associated as IgG for APS diagnosis [101]; only few studies report a pathogenic role for IgM aPL in experimental models and associations with arterial thrombotic events [105].

# Antiphospholipid Antibody Persistence

The Revised Sapporo Classification Criteria included confirmation of a positive result after 12 weeks as necessary to exclude "transient" positivity of aPL testing [3]. This recommended 12-week interval was largely derived from expert opinion and the committee agreed to evaluate literature related to time interval duration. While aPL "persistence" is clearly relevant to the evaluation of the aPL profile, prospective studies demonstrated that certain aPL profiles, e.g., triple aPL-positivity on single occasion, may predict aPL persistence after 12 weeks [97, 109, 110]. A single determination of high titer positive aPL may also predict positive repeat testing after 12 weeks [98, 111]; however, these findings are yet to be confirmed in large, prospective population-based studies.

# Discussion

During Phase IIIA/B of the APS classification criteria development, we applied a data-driven and consensus-based approach to defining clinical candidate criteria. Using literature review, we: (a) determined the associations between aPL-related clinical events and aPL tests, aPL solid phase-based assay levels and isotypes, and aPL persistence; and (b) developed clinical and laboratory candidate criteria and domain definitions and structure for APS classification. Throughout phase IIIA/B, SC consensus was based on the guiding principles that each clinical criterion should be evaluated in the context of a clinically acceptable aPL profile, and equally or more likely causes than APS should be thoroughly ruled out. Our approach resulting in the organization of criteria into independent clinical and laboratory domains aimed at improving the validity and reliability of the new classification system.

First, the SC agreed that macrovascular events should be considered in the context of provoking non-aPL VTE or CVD risk factors, which may affect the relative weight of the thrombosis event—introducing a unique aspect to APS classification criteria supported by international thrombosis guidelines [3, 21]. While the Revised Sapporo Classification Criteria acknowledged the importance of non-aPL thrombosis risk factors, previously no system quantified the thrombotic event weight in aPL-positive individuals by additional risk factor presence [3]. The SC also agreed that future APS classification criteria updates would allow for the inclusion of newer guidelines if needed.

Second, the SC introduced a new domain for microvascular APS; this is relevant as microvascular disease differs from moderate-to large vessel disease from a mechanistic, clinical, and treatment point-of-view. During development of the microvascular domain definitions, the most challenging was defining 'aPL nephropathy', published separately [32]. To enhance applicability of microvascular domain items, the committee also acknowledged that pathologic confirmation (biopsy) is not always feasible; thus, at times, physical examination, laboratory, and/or imaging parameters, can be counted towards the microvascular domain score.

Third, the SC made innovative decisions related to the obstetric domain. The committee stressed the importance of excluding 'other' potential causes of pregnancy loss but also acknowledged that full evaluation for known or suspected causes of pre-fetal (pre-embryonic or embryonic) deaths (e.g., conceptus aneuploidy, uterine malformation) or fetal deaths (e.g., conceptus aneuploidy, major structural defects) may not always be feasible; in such cases, reasonable clinical judgement should be exercised. As most early pregnancy losses are due to chromosomal abnormalities or otherwise

<!-- page 12 -->
unexplained, and the role of aPL in early pregnancy loss is not well established, the DSc recommended further assessment of fetal death in two groups (from 10 weeks to 15 weeks 6 days, and 16 weeks to 34 weeks) during phase III. Additionally, the committee agreed on the current definition of "recurrent" as three or more [112] pregnancy losses, but acknowledged that investigators may choose to study women with fewer pre-embryonic or embryonic in individual clinical studies/trials if deemed important.

Fourth, the addition of cardiac valve disease and thrombocytopenia to the APS classification criteria are also novel based on the accumulating evidence that these aPL-related manifestations are important to capture the full spectrum of APS.

Fifth, the SC agreed that not every "positive" aPL result is clinically relevant; key points for the determination of clinically relevant aPL include LA positivity, moderate-to-high aCL/a $\beta_{2}$ GPI levels (higher risk on a continuum compared to lower titers), IgG isotype (higher risk compared to IgM), persistence (compared to single one-time positivity), and combination of aPL tests (triple aPL positivity compared to double or single aPL positivity). Despite LA test limitations, i.e., false negative/positive results, the SC agreed that testing should be performed according to International Society of Thrombosis and Hemostasis (ISTH) guidelines and interpreted by an expert for the assessment of aPL-positive patients [113]. The laboratory DSc emphasized the importance of knowing the method of aPL immunoassay testing and ensuring use of the same platform during follow-up testing. While new automated platforms with variations of the solid phase (e.g., magnetic microparticles and microspheres) and various detection systems (e.g. chemiluminescence, flow cytometry and multiplex systems) aim to have certain advantages (e.g. harmonized and strict protocols, lower inter-laboratory variation, rapid results, and less labor intensive), they often have varied manufacturers, wide laboratory normal ranges and a paucity of data regarding "moderate" and "high" cut off levels. Thus, for APS classification, the DSc agreed that only moderate- and high-positive aCL/ a $\beta_{2}$ GPI ELISA tests (thresholds to be determined in Phase III-C) should be included to ensure homogeneity.

This work has several strengths and limitations. Decision-making for clinical and laboratory domain items was supported by data-driven and expert-based consensus. This was particularly important in the absence of published literature on the prospective association of clinical items with positive aPL tests, performance characteristics of individual aPL tests, and data on specific laboratory definitions (i.e. "persistent" definition, or aPL ELISA test positivity cut-offs). In situations in which the committee felt strongly that certain items, e.g., TIA, should be considered despite a paucity of supporting data, the decision was made to preliminarily

collect information on this item using real-world case scenarios for discussions during Phase III. Furthermore, the SC proposal to incorporate microvascular items by "clinical suspicion", i.e., physical examination, laboratory, and/or imaging findings, as opposed to definitive pathologic diagnosis, was facilitated by use of a hierarchical, additive, domain-based classification system, allowing for higher "weight" attribution to items with higher specificity. The SC acknowledged that a major strength of the classification system design is its ability to incorporate additional laboratory tests and platforms when feasible.

In next steps, these proposed candidate criteria definitions were used for Phase III-C real-world case collection (derivation cohort), followed by further refinement of definitions. Candidate criteria with refined definitions were incorporated into MCDA methodology using an advanced computing software $(1000\mathrm{Minds}^{\mathrm{TM}})$ to weight each item and determine a threshold for APS classification (Phase III-D) [10] followed by the validation of the new APS classification criteria (Phase IV) [5, 6].
