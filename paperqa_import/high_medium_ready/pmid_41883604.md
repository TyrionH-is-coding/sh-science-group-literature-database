## Abstract

Background: Catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (CAPS) is a rare, life-threatening variant of antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), characterized by rapid-onset thrombosis affecting multiple organs in the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies. Catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome is often triggered by an underlying condition, such as infection or malignancy. Early diagnosis and initiation of combination therapy are crucial, given the associated high morbidity and mortality.

Case summary: A 74-year-old woman presented with progressive dyspnoea and acute right-hand ischaemia. Imaging and laboratory work-up revealed widespread arterial and venous thromboses, including pulmonary embolism, brachial artery embolism, carotid artery involvement, and popliteal vein thrombosis. Laboratory tests showed positive lupus anticoagulant, raising suspicion for CAPS. She was treated with intravenous unfractionated heparin, high-dose corticosteroids, and intravenous immunoglobulins. A PET-CT revealed suspected pulmonary adenocarcinoma, which was confirmed via lymph node biopsy. Following initial clinical improvement and discharge on warfarin, she was readmitted with critical limb ischaemia and later on she suffered an ischaemic stroke. Despite endovascular interventions, her condition deteriorated. Given her poor prognosis and ineligibility for oncologic therapy, comfort care was initiated. The patient passed away a few days later.

Discussion: This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of probable CAPS in the context of occult malignancy. Catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome should be considered in patients presenting with simultaneous arterial and venous thrombotic events. Underlying malignancy may contribute to treatment resistance and worsen prognosis, emphasizing the need for early recognition and multidisciplinary management.

## Introduction

The antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a systemic autoimmune disease defined by thrombotic and/or obstetric events in patients with antiphospholipid antibodies (aPLs). Antiphospholipid antibodies comprise lupus anticoagulants (LAs), anticardiolipin antibodies (aCL), and anti-β2 glycoprotein-I-antibodies (a-β2GPI).

A subset of patients, estimated at 1% of patients with APS, develop a fulminant form characterized by thrombosis in multiple organs. This form is termed the catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (CAPS). Catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome is typically preceded by a precipitating event, such as infections or neoplasia. In patients with underlying malignancies, lymphoproliferative disorders and lung adenocarcinomas are the most prevalent. 1–4

Catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome can be probable or definitive. It is considered definitive when the patient fulfils all the criteria as proposed by Asherson et al . 2 Catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome can also be deemed probable when some but not all criteria are present, as outlined in Table 1 . Multimodality imaging is typically required and recommended to determine the extent of the cardiovascular thromboses. 5

Catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome is associated with significant morbidity and mortality of up to 44% at the time of the catastrophic APS event. Treatment of CAPS comprises adequate anticoagulation, typically via vitamin K antagonists such as warfarin, intravenously (IV) administered corticosteroids, IV immunoglobulins, and/or therapeutic plasma exchange. 1 , 3

In the following case, we describe a patient presenting with rapidly progressive multi-organ thrombotic events with underlying lung adenocarcinoma, illustrating the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of CAPS.

## Case presentation

A 74-year-old woman with a history of arterial hypertension and mild aortic insufficiency presented to the emergency department with progressive exertional shortness of breath and a tingling sensation in her right hand for 3 days. She denied fever, chest pain, or systemic symptoms. She was a current cigarette smoker with 50 pack-years and consumed two alcoholic beverages daily. There was no consumption of illicit drugs. Her outpatient medication consisted of amlodipine 5 mg once daily.

Vital signs on admission were stable. Physical examination revealed bilateral pitting oedema of the lower limbs and a cold, cyanotic right hand with absent radial pulses but preserved brachial pulse.

Laboratory tests are shown in Table 2 . Most notably are the normocytic anaemia in combination with a normal haptoglobin, thrombocytopenia, leukocytosis, elevated C-reactive protein (133 mg/L) and lactate dehydrogenase (749 U/L), D-dimers >20 000 ng/mL, troponin-T 286 pg/mL, and NT-proBNP 6378 pg/mL. Arterial blood gas analysis indicated hypoxaemia with respiratory alkalosis.

Chest X-ray revealed bilateral hilar enlargement, interstitial markings, and pleural effusions. Computed tomography (CT) of the pulmonary vessels confirmed bilateral segmental pulmonary emboli ( Figure 1A ) and lymphangitic spread with mediastinal adenopathy. Furthermore, a left lower lobe pneumonia was seen. Computed tomography angiography of the upper limbs revealed a brachial artery embolus extending into the radial and ulnar arteries and sub-occlusive emboli in the left internal and external carotid arteries.

Transthoracic echocardiography showed preserved biventricular function and the known mild aortic insufficiency. There were no significant stenoses nor insufficiencies of other cardiac valves. There were no endocardial thrombi nor thrombotic or infectious endocarditis of the valves observed. The interatrial septum appeared normal without arguments for atrial septum defects. Duplex ultrasound detected a right popliteal vein thrombosis. Given simultaneous venous and arterial thromboembolism, the patient was admitted to the cardiac care unit and started on intravenous unfractionated heparin (UFH) with dosing based on a target aPTT (activated partial thromboplastin time) of 60–80 s. A blood sample taken prior to starting the UFH revealed the presence of LA. Anti-cardiolipin and anti-β2-glycoprotein I antibodies were negative.

Due to extensive thrombotic involvement with acute onset, CAPS was suspected. Apart from the UFH, the patient received IV methylprednisolone 500 mg during 3 days and IV immunoglobulins (IVIG) at a dosage of 1 g/kg/day during 2 days. Empirical antibiotics were initiated for lobar pneumonia, which resolved after 5 days.

Due to ongoing pain and paraesthesia in her right arm, a surgical embolectomy was performed and resolved the right-hand ischaemia. Transoesophageal echocardiography uncovered a small patent foramen ovale, insufficient to explain the massive arterial embolisms. A PET-CT scan revealed findings suspicious for primary lung adenocarcinoma with lymphangitic carcinomatosis and widespread nodal involvement. Anatomopathological examination of a resected cervical lymph node revealed metastasis of an adenocarcinoma, compatible with pulmonary origin. She was transitioned from UFH to warfarin, with a target international normalized ratio (INR) of 2.5–3.5, and discharged awaiting ambulatory consultation with a pulmonologist a week later.

Six days post discharge, she was readmitted with signs and symptoms of left foot ischaemia and an INR of 11. Examination revealed skin necrosis ( Figure 1B ), poor distal pulses, and mild sensory loss. Computed tomography angiography showed occlusion of the anterior and posterior tibial arteries. Warfarin was stopped, intravenous vitamin K given, and she was started on subcutaneous low molecular weight heparin (enoxaparin, with a dosing of 1 mg/kg, twice daily). Percutaneous transluminal angioplasty with stenting was unsuccessful and ischaemia persisted. Anatomopathological examination of necrotic zone of the forefoot revealed intravascular thrombotic occlusion of the small vessels, compatible with CAPS and less so with warfarin-induced skin necrosis.

Ten days after this readmission, the patient developed sudden aphasia and left hemiparesis. Computed tomography scan following stroke protocol showed right M2 occlusion with ischaemic changes. Mechanical thrombectomy ( Figure 1C ) was performed with successful reperfusion. Despite this, her neurological recovery was poor, with persistent deficits.

Given her poor performance status, she was deemed ineligible for oncologic treatment. In agreement with her family, comfort care was initiated. A few days later, the patient passed away.

## Discussion

This case illustrates a rare but severe manifestation of probable CAPS in the setting of an underlying pulmonary adenocarcinoma. Catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome is defined by rapid-onset, multi-organ thrombosis and the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies, most often lupus anticoagulant (LA), anticardiolipin, or anti-β2 glycoprotein I antibodies. 1

As CAPS can be probable, the differential diagnosis at admission should be broad. The most notable to differentiate conditions are diffuse intravascular coagulation, thrombotic microangiopathies, such as thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS), and small-vessel vasculitis such as antineutrophil cytoplastic antibody (ANCA) vasculitis. In our case, there were no bleeding events and PT was slightly prolonged, but fibrinogen was normal and there was no biochemical evidence of haemolysis rendering the former two diagnoses unlikely. Furthermore, aPLs are not typically positive in ANCA vasculitis. Antineutrophil cytoplastic antibody was planned 12 weeks after the initial event, however, could not be tested anymore as the patient had passed away.

Our patient met the criteria for probable CAPS: acute involvement of three or more organs (lungs, upper and lower limbs, central nervous system), histologic evidence of small-vessel thrombosis, and positive LA, though other aPLs were negative at first measurement. While definitive CAPS requires all criteria to be met, early diagnosis and treatment initiation are crucial due to the high mortality. 2 However, while these criteria provide a clear overview, they have only been validated for research purposes. This in combination with the extreme rarity of the syndrome, the absence of pathognomonic features, and the high mortality makes CAPS a difficult entity to diagnose and to treat. 2

Treatment in our case followed established triple therapy: anticoagulation, high-dose corticosteroids, and intravenous immunoglobulins. Therapeutic plasma exchange can be an alternative for IVIG and is most commonly used in rapidly progressive multi-organ failure or when there are microangiopathy features. 6 There are however no head to head, randomized controlled trials comparing both treatment strategies. Despite initial improvement, the patient experienced recurrent arterial thrombotic events. The presence of malignancy likely contributed to treatment resistance, as disease control of the underlying cancer is required for effective CAPS management. 4

The underlying mechanisms in patients with an active malignancy leading to CAPS are still unclear. It is suggested that haemostatic changes caused by the malignancy, such as platelet and endothelial cell activation and the expression of tissue factor, might be the most important factors. Notably, these mechanisms are also relevant for the induction of APS itself. 4

This case underscores the diagnostic and therapeutic complexity and poor prognosis of CAPS, particularly when associated with advanced malignancy. Early recognition and aggressive multimodal therapy remain essential, though outcomes are often limited by underlying disease burden.
