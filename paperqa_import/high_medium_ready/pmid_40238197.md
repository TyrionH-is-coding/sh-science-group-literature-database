# Anti-β2glycoprotein I-induced neutrophil extracellular traps cause endothelial activation

## Abstract

Objective: Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) involvement in antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) pathogenesis is known, but the role of anti-β2glycoprotein I (aβ2GPI) antibodies-induced NETs in triggering a procoagulant and proinflammatory phenotype in endothelial cells (ECs) remains to be evaluated. This study investigated whether NET-aβ2GPI can activate ECs and whether NET-aβ2GPI and NET-phorbol myristate acetate (PMA) have different proteomic profiles.

Methods: Healthy donor (HD) neutrophils were stimulated with APS-aβ2GPI, normal human IgG or PMA. NETs were stained with anti-neutrophil elastase and 4’6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), and the ability of aβ2GPI to bind NETs and inhibit DNA degradation was investigated. Following aβ2GPI, NET-aβ2GPI and NET-PMA stimuli, we evaluated EC activation investigating intra-cellular adhesion molecule (ICAM), vascular cell adhesion molecule (VCAM) and tissue factor (TF) expression using flow cytometry and reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR); and EC dysfunction analysing extracellular microvesicles (EMVs) release via flow cytometry and NanoSight analysis. Mass spectrometry-based proteomics was performed on NET-aβ2GPI and NET-PMA.

Results: Unlike normal IgG, aβ2GPI induced NET formation and bound to NETs by colocalizing with the neutrophil elastase signal at 93.6% without preventing NET degradation. Compared with unstimulated EC, NET-aβ2GPI triggered higher mRNA and a robust expression of TF, VCAM and ICAM in EC with a change-fold median fluorescence intensity (MFI) of 6, 4.2 and 2.3. aβ2GPI induced a significant increase in EMVs compared with untreated samples and those treated with NETs. Fifty-six proteins were identified, seven resulted upregulated in NET-aβ2GPI and downregulated in PMA-induced ones. GO enrichment analysis revealed that proteins upregulated in NET-aβ2GPI were enriched for ubiquitin protein ligase binding and SLC2A4 translocation to the plasma membrane.

Conclusion: aβ2GPI-induced NETs can cause EC activation and TF expression.

Keywords: NET formation, anti-β2glycoprotein I, endothelial cell activation

## Full Text

## Introduction

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a systemic autoimmune disorder characterized by thrombotic and/or obstetric manifestations with circulating antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL) [ 1 ].

The pathogenesis of APS has not yet been fully elucidated; we know that anti-β2glycoprotein I (aβ2GPI) can activate several cells—platelets, monocytes, endothelial cells (ECs)—causing thrombosis. Yalavarthi et al. revealed the role of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) formation in the pathogenesis of APS [ 2 ]. They detected increased levels of NETs markers in the plasma of APS patients compared with healthy donors (HDs), and they also found an enhanced spontaneous NET formation in APS neutrophils. Furthermore, they demonstrated the ability of monoclonal aβ2GPI to induce NETs release in HD neutrophils in a phorbol myristate acetate (PMA)-like manner [ 2 ]. There is growing evidence on the role of NETs in the pathogenesis of APS [ 3 ], but to date, the direct role of aβ2GPI-induced NETs in the pathogenesis of APS has not yet addressed.

In vitro studies on non-aPL-related thrombosis have revealed that NETs, generated by stimulating neutrophils with PMA, can elicit EC activation and increase adhesiveness through the expression of ICAM-1 (intra-cellular adhesion molecule 1) and VCAM-1 (vascular cell adhesion molecule 1); in addition, NETs induce tissue factor (TF) expression [ 4 ].

Similarly, in vitro studies have demonstrated that aβ2GPI can trigger a procoagulant and proinflammatory response in ECs by promoting the expression of adhesion molecules [ 5–7 ]. This occurs through a transduction pathway that involves IRAK phosphorylation and NF-κB activation and translocation [ 8 ], leading to the release of tissue factor (TF) [ 9 ].

The role of ECs in the pathogenesis of APS is well-established, as does the close relationship between ECs activation and thrombosis [ 10 ].

To date, the role of aβ2GPI-induced NETs in triggering a procoagulant and proinflammatory phenotype in ECs has never been evaluated.

Given that PMA-induced NETs contribute to endothelial activation and considering the ability of aβ2GPI to directly activate both NET formation and endothelium, it can be hypothesized that aβ2GPI antibodies can enhance EC activation both directly and indirectly, through NETs.

This study tested the hypothesis that NETs induced by aβ2GPI and PMA differ in their protein composition and function. Additionally, we assessed the capacity of aβ2GPI-induced NETs to trigger endothelial activation and dysfunction.

## Patients and methods

### Patients and aβ2GPI antibody purification

Human aβ2GPI antibodies were purified from a pool of primary APS patient sera by affinity chromatography, according to the technique reported by McNeil et al. [ 11 ]. We enrolled three consecutive outpatients referred to the Sapienza Lupus Clinic of Rome, with a diagnosis of APS in accordance with the latest classification criteria [ 12 ], tested positive for aβ2GPI IgG at high titer. Exclusion criteria for all study participants were other systemic autoimmune conditions, infections and cancer. The patients gave written informed consent, in compliance with the Helsinki Declaration. The research protocol was approved by the ethic committees of Sapienza University of Rome.

### Neutrophil isolation and NET induction

Neutrophils were isolated from one HD undergoing peripheral venous blood sampling. Blood treated with 2 nM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) was stratified on Histopaque 1119/1077 and centrifuged. Neutrophils, separated by gradient, were collected, and washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and resuspended in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) medium.

Neutrophils were cultured at 37°C, 5% CO 2 and untreated or treated, for 3 h, with affinity-purified aβ2GPI antibodies (25 µg/ml), normal human IgG (25 µg/ml), or PMA (100 nM) as positive control.

### NET immunofluorescence

Neutrophils, after fixing with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), were incubated with rabbit anti-human neutrophil elastase followed by the addition of Alexa Fluor 555-anti-rabbit secondary antibody and 4’6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) to visualize nuclear DNA. Furthermore, the same samples, to visualize NETs, were incubated with SYBR green, a commonly used fluorescent dye that binds double-stranded DNA. Immunofluorescence images were acquired with Olympus BX60 microscope and processed with Fiji.

Quantification of NETs was performed with NETQUANT, which allows automated determination of the percentage of neutrophils that release NETs on direct immunofluorescence images presenting a DNA marker (DAPI) and a marker for NETs (neutrophil elastase) [ 13 ].

### Binding of aβ2GPI antibodies to NETs

Human neutrophils were stimulated with 100 nM PMA to form NETs, fixed with 4% PFA, and then incubated with rabbit anti-neutrophil elastase, followed by Alexa Fluor 555-anti-rabbit secondary antibody, SYBR green. Furthermore, to visualize the binding of aβ2GPI to NETs, affinity-purified aβ2GPI antibodies (5 µg/ml) followed by Alexa Fluor 647-anti-human secondary antibody were added to the same neutrophil samples. As a control, neutrophils were also incubated with normal human IgG and Alexa Fluor 647-anti-human secondary antibody. Signal colocalization of NETs and aβ2GPI antibodies was evaluated with JACoP, a Fiji plugin.

### Degradation of NETs by DNase I

To evaluate the ability of NET binding aβ2GPI antibodies to prevent DNA degradation by DNase I, two sets of experiments were performed.

In the first one, HD neutrophils stimulated with PMA 100 nM for 3 h were incubated for 1 h with affinity-purified aβ2GPI IgG (5 µg/ml) and then with DNase I (10 µg/ml) for 1 h.

In the second experiment, human neutrophils were stimulated with aβ2GPI IgG (25 µg/ml) for 3 h and then incubated with DNase I (10 µg/ml) for 1 h. We detected DNA degradation using immunofluorescence as shown above.

### NET isolation and EC in vitro treatment

NETs, obtained from HD neutrophils treated with affinity-purified aβ2GPI antibodies (25 µg/dl) and PMA (100 nM) for 3 h as reported above, were isolated and digested as described by Zuo [ 14 ]. NETs were digested using Micrococcal nuclease in the presence of 1x Micrococcal nuclease reaction buffer for 20 min at 37°C. The digested material was evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis to confirm a consistent level of digestion. Additionally, we ensured that the DNA and protein concentrations were within the expected ranges (DNA: 0.6–1.1 µg per million neutrophils; Protein: 3–6 µg per million neutrophils) [ 14 ]. Then, they were added to the cell cultures of EA.hy926, a human EC line.

EA.hy926 cells were maintained at 37°C and 5% CO 2 in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (high glucose), containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), hypoxanthine/aminopterin/thymidine (HAT supplement), 100 U/ml penicillin and 100 mg/ml streptomycin. Cells, grown to 60–70% confluence, were incubated with affinity-purified aβ2GPI antibodies (200 µg/ml), normal human IgG (200 µg/ml), PMA-induced NETs and aβ2GPI-induced NETs, and also untreated EA.hy926 cells. After treatment, cells were detached, and washed and the pellets and supernatants obtained were collected and used in the experiments described below.

### EC activation

To investigated EC activation, EA.hy926 cells were analysed by cytofluorimetric analysis to evaluate VCAM-1, ICAM-1 and TF expression. Cells were incubated with the followed antibodies (diluted 1:100): PE-anti-CD106 (VCAM-1), PerCP-anti-CD54 (ICAM-1) and FITC-anti-CD142 (TF). Non-specific bindings were blocked with 20% FBS in PBS. The cells were evaluated by flow cytometry, acquiring 10 000 events per sample. The results were reported as fold-change of median fluorescence intensity (MFI) compared with the MFI of unstimulated EA.hy926. The fluorescence-minus-one (FMO) control included all antibodies with the conjugated fluorochromes and was prepared for each antibody.

To further support our findings, we have repeated the experiment using a primary cell line (human umbilical vein ECs—HUVEC) and employed an alternative method (reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction—RT-qPCR). The mRNA levels of the genes tested (TF, VCAM, ICAM) were normalized to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH).

### Detection of extracellular microvesicles in the supernatant of ECs

The in vitro generation of extracellular microvesicles (EMVs) in ECs was detected based on the Pericleous protocol [ 15 ]. Briefly, EA.hy926 cell supernatants were centrifuged at 3000 × g for 5 min to remove debris. The supernatants, recovered and transferred in a new tube, were centrifuged at 15 000 × g for 60 min to obtain endothelial EMVs. Pellets were resuspended in a filtered binding buffer (10 mM Hepes, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCL, 1 mM MgCl 2 , 1.8 mM CaCl 2 +H 2 O, pH 7.4) and then incubated with anti-CD31-(PE) and FITC-conjugated Annexin-V (AV). Incubation was performed at RT for 30 min in binding buffer, then EMV suspensions were processed by flow cytometry. Forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) were adjusted to logarithmic gain. EMV gating was performed using 1-μm beads (Sigma-Aldrich) for setting upper limits in both FSC and SSC signals, whereas a lower limit was placed to exclude buffer noise ( Supplementary Fig. S1 , available at Rheumatology online).

In parallel experiments, it was performed a NanoSight NS300 (Malvern Panalytical) analysis to measure size and concentration of EMVs isolated from EA.hy926 cell supernatants. Briefly, EMVs were diluted 1:100 in particle-free PBS (0.02 μm filtered) and then analysed. The instrument was equipped with a 488 nm laser (blue), a high sensitivity sCMOS camera and a syringe pump. The NTA 3.4 Build 3.4.4 software was used to analyse the experiment videos, at 20°C, after capture in script control mode (5 videos of 60 s per 24 measurement) using syringe pump speed 30.

### Mass spectrometry-based proteomics of NETs induced by PMA and aβ2GPI-treated neutrophils

#### Sample preparation

NETs, obtained from human neutrophils treated with affinity-purified aβ2GPI antibodies (25 µg/dl) and PMA (100 nM) for 3 h as reported above, were isolated as described by Zuo et al. [ 14 ]. NET samples (100 µL) were denatured, reduced and alkylated with in-StageTip (iST)-LYSE buffer (PreOmics) for 10 min at 95°C on an Eppendorf ThermoMixer at 1000 rpm. Proteins were isolated by Protein Aggregation Capture (PAC) [ 16 ] using 1:1 Sera-Mag™ SpeedBead Carboxylate-Modified Magnetic Particles 45152105050250 and 65152105050250 (Cytiva) in a protein/bead ratio of 1:4 (w/w). 70% acetonitrile (ACN) was added to samples to induce protein precipitation and sequential washes were performed with 100% ACN, 70% ethanol and 100% isopropanol. Protein digestion was carried out in 100 µL 25 mM Tris, pH 8, with 0.5 µg trypsin (Promega) and 0.2 µg Lys-C (Wako) for 2.5 h at 37°C. Peptide mixtures were acidified with 2% trifluoroacetic acid to quench protease activity and desalted according to the iST method [ 17 ]. Eluted peptides were dried by vacuum centrifugation and resuspended in 2% ACN, 0.1% formic acid (FA) in water.

#### Nanoflow LC–MS/MS

Samples were analysed on an UltiMate 3000 RSLCnano system coupled to a Q-Exactive Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptides were separated on an EASY-Spray PepMap RSLC C18 column (50 cm × 75 μm, 2 μm particle size, Thermo Scientific) using a 50 min gradient ranging from 2% solution B [80% ACN, 5% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and 0.1% FA] to 45% B in 50 min. The mass spectrometer was operated in positive polarity and data-dependent acquisition (DDA) top10 scan mode. Full scans (m/z 375–1500) were acquired in the Orbitrap at 70 000 resolutions with the automatic gain control (AGC) target set to 3 × 10 6 . The higher collisional dissociation energy for MS/MS fragmentation was set to 27%. MS2 scans were acquired at 17 500 resolutions with 1 × 10 5 AGC target.

### Proteomics data analysis

Raw files were analysed by MaxQuant v2.4.9.0 [ 18 ] using the Andromeda Search engine against the UniProt Human database (release UP000005640_9606, July 2021). Protein identification was performed with a 1% FDR and requiring at least seven amino acids per peptide. The MaxLFQ algorithm was used for protein quantification with the ‘match between runs’ option to minimize missing values.

The ProteinGroup table was imported into Perseus software version 1.6.15.0 [ 19 ] for data cleaning. Protein groups were filtered to exclude contaminants and to require at least 70% valid intensity values within at least one experimental group. Label-free peak intensities were log2-transformed and missing values were imputed with random numbers drawn from a normal distribution for each column to best simulate low abundance values close to noise levels. Analysis of pre-processed data was performed using R v 4.3.3 and Bioconductor packages EdgeR for differentially expressed genes analysis and clusterProfiler for gene enrichment analysis, interrogating three different databases: Gene Ontology, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes and Reactome. Network analysis was performed using Cytoscape software version 3.8.2 and stringAPP version 2.0.3.

Functional enrichment analysis was also performed using ShinyGO v0.80 [ 20 ].

Proteomics data are featured in the ProteomeXchange consortium via the PRIDE partner repository [ 21 ], dataset identifier PXD052010. Proteomics data are also available in Supplementary Fig. S2 (available at Rheumatology online).

### Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean and standard deviation (SD), standard error (SE) or median and interquartile range (IQR) based on the normal distribution of variables. The χ 2 test or Fisher’s exact test was used for comparison of categorical variables. The differences between the continuous variables were evaluated with two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test and Dunnett’s multiple comparison test. Prism 5 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) was used for the above statistical tests.

P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

## Results

### aβ2GPI antibodies induce NET formation

We enrolled 3 APS patients, mean age of 39 years (SD 6.48), presenting a history of ischemic stroke, resulted positive for IgG aβ2GPI at high titer (mean 4334 UA/ml; SD 2461.5). HD neutrophils were stimulated with affinity-purified aβ2GPI resulting in NET formation ( Fig. 1A and B ), differently from normal IgG ( Fig. 1C ).

( A ) Representative immunofluorescence microscopy of NETs; ( B ) quantification of NETs, performed automatically with NETQUANT, a MATLAB application, based on the analysis of five images at 20X magnification obtained from five ROI of the central well area. ** P <0.01. **** P <0.0001. DAPI: 4’6-diamidino-2-phenylindole; PMA: phorbol myristate acetate

### aβ2GPI antibodies bind to NETs

We found that aβ2GPI IgG, unlike normal human IgG, bind to NETs, as shown in Fig. 2A and B . Moreover, the aβ2GPI signal colocalized with the anti-neutrophil elastase at 93.6%, suggesting that aβ2GPI has an anti-NET reactivity ( Fig. 2A ). The binding of aβ2GPI to NETs did not prevent its degradation when treated with DNase I.

Representative immunofluorescence microscopy of healthy donor neutrophils stimulated with PMA and incubated with aβ2GPI antibodies ( A ) or normal IgG ( B ). aβ2GPI antibodies bind to NETs released by PMA-stimulated healthy donor neutrophils with a colocalization signal of 93.6%. Red = neutrophil elastase, green = DNA, cyan = aβ2GPI IgG

### aβ2GPI-induced NETs trigger EC activation

To test the hypothesis that aβ2GPI-induced NETs activate ECs, we evaluated the expression of TF, VCAM-1 and ICAM-1 in EA.hy926 cells. Compared with unstimulated cells and normal IgG, the MFI of ICAM-1, VCAM-1 and TF surface expression was higher in cells treated with aβ2GPI antibodies, PMA-induced NETs and aβ2GPI-induced NET ( P values <0.0001). In particular, aβ2GPI-induced NETs, compared with untreated cells, triggered high expression of TF, VCAM-1 and ICAM-1 in EA.hy926 cells resulting in a change-fold MFI of 6 (SE 0.1), 4.2 (SE 0.09) and 2.3 (SE 0.09), respectively. VCAM-1 and ICAM-1 were higher expressed in EA.hy926 treated with aβ2GPI-induced NET than those treated with aβ2GPI ( P < 0.0001 in all instances) ( Fig. 3 ).

To further support our findings, we have repeated the experiment using HUVEC. Compared with unstimulated control and normal IgG control, ECs treated with PMA-induced NETs and aβ2GPI-induced NET exhibited higher mRNA of TF, VCAM, and ICAM ( P < 0.0001 in all instances) ( Supplementary Fig. S3 , available at Rheumatology online).

### aβ2GPI antibodies but not NETs induce EMVs release in the supernatant of ECs

Cytofluorimetric analysis was used to analyse EMVs isolated from EA.hy926 cell supernatants, also verifying the cellular origin by use of anti-CD31 antibody. Differently from NETs, aβ2GPI induced a significant increase in EMVs (CD31+/annexin V+) release from EA.hy926 compared with untreated samples ( P -value <0.001) ( Fig. 4A ). Results were confirmed at quantitative analysis performed by NanoSight (Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis). Results showed in Fig. 4B revealed that the number of EMVs in the sample treated with aβ2GPI was significantly higher than the untreated sample [concentration: 4.74 × 10^8 EMVs/ml (S.D. 0.13) compared with 2.66 × 10^8 EMVs/ml (S.D. 0.11), P -value <0.0001] ( Fig. 4B ). Furthermore, NanoSight analysis highlighted that EMVs release in cells treated with NETs induced by aβ2GPI or PMA was not significantly increased as compared with untreated cells. These results may likely indicate that NETs are not implicated in the EC dysfunction related to EMVs release.

### Comparative proteomics: aβ2GPI-induced NETs vs PMA-induced NETs

A total of 56 proteins were identified and quantified ( Fig. 5A ; Supplementary Fig. S1 , available at Rheumatology online).

**Fig:** Figure 5. Proteomic analysis of aβ2GPI and PMA-induced NETs. ( A ) Heatmap of NET proteins differentially represented in aβ2GPI-induced NETs and PMA-induced NETs. ( B ) Volcano plot represents seven proteins upregulated in aβ2GPI-induced NETs on the right and six downregulated on the left; the six proteins upregulated in PMA-induced NETs showed the opposite pattern. ( C ) Enrichment analysis. Gene Ontology, Kegg and Reactome databases were employed to define the pathways involved. Pathways were selected if significant ( P value < 0.05) and based on the cell type analysed (B cells). Pathways both up and downregulated were excluded. Thirteen out of 56 proteins were differentially represented between the two experimental groups. Specifically, seven proteins resulted upregulated in aβ2GPI-induced NETs and downregulated in PMA-induced ones, while six proteins displayed the opposite pattern, as reported in the heatmap ( Fig. 5B ).

We performed GO enrichment analysis on the differentially expressed proteins and found that proteins upregulated in aβ2GPI-induced NET were enriched for ubiquitin protein ligase binding and translocation of SLC2A4 (GLUT4) to the plasma membrane, while proteins downregulated in aβ2GPI-induced NET were enriched for tertiary granule and neutrophil degranulation ( Fig. 5C ).

To gain further insight into the functional differences between aβ2GPI- and PMA-induced NETs, we performed STRING network analysis on the differentially expressed proteins.

Our findings revealed functional connections between some proteins in each NET group. Notably, triosephosphate isomerase 1 (TPI1), 14–3-3 epsilon protein (YWHAE) and tubulin alpha-1B (TUBA1B) proteins, upregulated in aβ2GPI-induced NET, showed functional relationships among themselves at network analysis that were distinct from other proteins, indicating unique interconnections within some aβ2GPI-induced NET proteins that differentiate them from PMA-induced NET proteins ( Fig. 5B , right panel).

## Discussion

The present study revealed that aβ2GPI from APS patients can induce NET formation and bind to NET. Furthermore, NETs induced by aβ2GPI differ from those induced by PMA and can activate ECs.

In 1995, it was shown, for the first time, that aβ2GPI can activate neutrophils by inducing cytoplasmic granule release and hydrogen peroxide production [ 22 ]. Twenty years later, Yalavarthi et al. revealed that aβ2GPI can trigger NETs release in PMA-like manner [ 2 ].

One of the pathogenic mechanisms of NETs is secondary to the exposure of autoantigens in the extracellular environment which are the potential targets of autoantibodies. It is not surprising that anti-NET is detectable in almost half of the aPL-positive subjects and APS patients. Additionally, anti-NET has been associated with lesions affecting the white matter of the brain [ 23 ].

Prior to our study, it was not known whether aβ2GPI could represent one of anti-NET. Indeed, we identified the ability of aβ2GPI to bind PMA-induced NETs, unlike normal human IgG. In addition, the colocalization of aβ2GPI and neutrophil elastase showed an almost complete overlap of signals. Therefore, aβ2GPI are not only capable of inducing NETs formation, but also exhibit anti-NET reactivity.

Unsurprisingly, literature results revealed that circulating aβ2GPI were predictive of a higher prevalence of low-density granulocytes (LDGs), a neutrophil subpopulation with enhanced spontaneous NETotic capacity [ 24 ]. Therefore, aβ2GPI may play a central role in NET formation.

It is currently known that β2GPI binds DNA and NET strands [ 25 ], so it is possible that β2GPI, present in the NETs, becomes a target for aβ2GPI. Since a recent study has shown that aβ2GPI are able to bind DNA directly [ 26 ], we can hypothesize that aβ2GPI directly bind the DNA of NETs.

Zuo et al. showed a direct correlation between anti-NET levels and a reduced ability to degrade NETs in vitro [ 14 ]. Possible explanations could be that anti-NET can reduce DNA degradation either by binding NET, thus acting as a ‘screen’, or antagonizing the DNase I enzyme. In this concern, we tested the ability of aβ2GPI binding NET in preventing degradation by DNase I and we found that these antibodies do not exhibit such ability. We therefore suggest that this function can be performed by anti-NETs aimed at other targets.

The growing interest of NETs in APS arises from studies investigating the connections between NETs and autoimmunity, as well as NET formation and thrombosis.

Indeed, studies on other autoimmune diseases have revealed that NETs can dysregulate innate and adaptive immunity through various mechanisms: by inducing the alternative complement pathway [ 27 ], by activating caspase 1 leading to inflammasome formation [ 28 ], triggering dendritic cells to produce INFα via TLR-9 [ 29 ], causing macrophages to release pro-inflammatory cytokines via TLR-2 and TLR-4 [ 30 ], acting as primers on T cells by reducing their activation threshold [ 31 ]. In addition, cytotoxic proteins of NETs can cause direct organ damage [ 32 ] and behave like a scaffold for thrombosis [ 33 ].

Since ECs have a crucial role in the pathogenesis of thrombosis both in APS and in non-autoimmune conditions, maintaining blood fluidity by balancing procoagulant and anticoagulant mechanisms [ 10 ], we also evaluated the role of NETs induced by aβ2GPI in triggering a procoagulant and proinflammatory phenotype in ECs.

In this regard, we found that aβ2GPI-induced NETs caused an increased expression of VCAM-1 ICAM-1 and TF in ECs; furthermore, the expression of ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 was higher than that given by aβ2GPI.

Therefore, taken together, our results revealed that aβ2GPI-induced NETs can activate ECs, also amplifying the already known role of aβ2GPI.

Moreover, we analysed the presence of CD31+/V-annexin+ EMVs, a known biomarker for endothelial dysfunction [ 34 , 35 ]. Results showed an increase in the concentration of CD31+/V-annexin+ EMVs in the supernatant of EA.hy926 cells treated with aβ2GPI unlike the samples treated with NETs, indicating a role for aβ2GPI in inducing endothelial dysfunction.

This is the first study aimed to evaluate the possible effects of aβ2GPI-induced NETs, without relying on PMA-induced NETs as a surrogate.

Based on previous findings that highlighted variations in protein composition among NETs triggered by different stimuli (PMA, A23187, LPS, or spontaneous NET formation) [ 36 ], we also conducted a proteomic analysis to elucidate the difference between aβ2GPI-induced NETs and those induced by PMA.

Our results revealed that certain NET proteins are differently represented in aβ2GPI-induced NET and PMA-induced NET. Among the proteins upregulated in aβ2GPI-induced NETs, TPI1, YWHAE and TUBA1B showed functional relationships with each other distinct from those observed in PMA-induced NETs; in addition, prothymosin alpha (PTMA) was functionally distinct from other proteins.

TPI1, a glycolytic enzyme, is the target of autoantibodies (anti-TPI1) that have been found in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients with multiple sclerosis (MS) [ 37 ] and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), particularly those with focal neuropsychiatric SLE (NPSLE) and would appear to play a pathogenetic role [ 38 ]. Notably, APS patients may present with an MS-like syndrome [ 39 ], whereas NPSLE is mainly associated with aPLs [ 40 ]. The 14–3-3 protein, particularly the epsilon isoform (YWHAE), plays a role in cell survival and protects the brain from ischaemia-induced damage and apoptosis caused by ischemic stroke [ 41 ]. Also, TUBA1B, a cytoskeleton protein, has an unclear role in NETs, but it is present in spontaneous NETs and absent in stimulus-induced NETs (PMA, A23187, LPS) [ 36 ] resulted to be upregulated in patients with myocardial infarction and ischemic stroke [ 42 ].

PTMA, a protein with a known role in immune response regulation, acts as an alarmin. Extracellular PTMA is involved in T cell expression of IL-2 and induces the expression of costimulatory molecules in dendritic cells. Like aβ2GPI, PTMA binds TLR4 and activates immune cells, upregulating the expression of IRAK4 through TRIF-dependent and MyD88 pathways, leading to the induction of proinflammatory cytokines [ 43 ]. Consequently, PTMA appears to have the same pathway of aβ2GPI.

Proteins upregulated in NETs by aβ2GPI demonstrate a distinct functional difference from those induced by PMA, particularly in their promotion of glycolytic activity. This metabolic shift towards glycolysis plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of autoimmune rheumatic diseases, especially SLE and Rheumatoid Arthritis [ 44 ].

However, the mechanism(s) responsible for the specificity of the aβ2GPI effect on NET-response are not fully understood. The key difference between aβ2GPI-induced and PMA-induced NET formation lies in their oxygen consumption patterns. Specifically, aβ2GPI stimulation leads to a gradual and sustained increase in extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) in neutrophils, persisting over several h. In contrast, PMA stimulation results in a rapid peak in both ECAR and OCR, occurring approximately one h after stimulation [ 45 ].

It is unlike that the different observations are related to NOX-dependent vs NOX-independent NETs, since both aβ2GPI-induced NETs and PMA-induced NETs are sensitive to inhibition by diphenyleneiodonium chloride (DPI) [ 45 ], a NOX inhibitor. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that it may be related to the N-terminal histone tails in NETs, as reported by Pieterse et al. [ 46 ].

Overall, these results emphasize that NET proteins stimulated by aβ2GPI and PMA are different in both composition and biological function. Our study has certain limitations that should be acknowledged.

The use of pooled aβ2GPI from three APS patients introduces potential heterogeneity in the antibody population. However, pooling the antibodies allowed us to minimize the impact of individual variability and focus on broader trends.

Additionally, NETs were obtained from a single HD, since this approach prompts to standardize experimental conditions and focus on the effects of the different stimuli.

In conclusion, our findings describe how aβ2GPI-induced NET amplify EC activation and TF induction, unveiling a novel mechanism connecting the process of NET formation to thrombotic pathogenesis in APS.
