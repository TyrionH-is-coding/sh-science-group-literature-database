# ABSTRACT

Background: To clarify the association and diagnostic utility of measuring antiphosphatidyl serine (aPS), antiprothrombin (aPT) and the antiphosphatidyl serine/prothrombin complex (aPS/PT) antibodies in patients with antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) and recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL).

Method: We measured IgG and IgM anti-aPS/PT (2 different kits) as well as the aPS and aPT autoantibodies by ELISA. All subjects were also tested for the presence of Lupus anticoagulant (LA), IgG and IgM anticardiolipin (aCL) and anti- $\beta$ -2 glycoprotein I (aS2GPI), and these served as the gold standard unless otherwise indicated. Two groups of patients were studied: (i) APS with RPL and/or thrombosis $(n = 62)$ ; (ii) RPL only $(n = 66)$ and a group of healthy women with successful pregnancies (WSP; $n = 30$ ).

Results: The area under curve (AUC) analyses demonstrated significant differences between the aPS/PT (0.980) and aPT (0.850) assays $(p = 0.002)$ with no significant differences between the 2 aPS/PT kits or the aPS/PT and aPS assays. The overall agreement between the tested aPL antibodies and lupus anticoagulant (LA) for the APS cohort was very poor but associations between aPL assays were substantial $(\kappa > 0.5)$ as determined by Cohen $\kappa$ analysis.

Conclusions: Our data show a lack of association between aPS/PT antibodies and LA in APS patients with recurrent pregnancy loss. In the evaluation of these patients, there may be redundancy in testing all three markers as the aPT and aPS assays formed part of the aPS/PT antibody repertoire.

# 1. Introduction

The hallmark of antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is thrombosis and/or adverse obstetric outcomes in the presence of antiphospholipid (aPL) antibodies [1]. Current laboratory recommendations for the diagnosis of definite APS include demonstration of persistent and elevated levels of anti- $\beta$ -2 glycoprotein I $(\mathrm{a}\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI})$ IgG and IgM, anticardiolipin (aCL) IgG and IgM as well as the lupus anticoagulant (LA) [1]. APL antibodies represent a heterogeneous group of autoantibodies that recognize various phospholipids (PL), PL-binding plasma proteins, and/or PL-protein complexes. It has been proposed that the involved plasma proteins may include $(\beta$ -2-glycoprotein I $(\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI})$ prothrombin (PT), protein C, protein S and annexin V [2-4].

The aPL antibodies associated with LA activity are detected by their abilities to prolong certain in vitro phospholipids (PL)-restricted blood

clotting tests [1,5,6]. There are a number of inhibitors and analytical variables that can cause abnormal test results, making the assay fraught with interpretive and technical challenges [5,6]. An early study showed antiprothrombin (aPT) antibodies in $72\%$ of LA-positive patients [2]. This and other studies showing aPT antibodies in LA-positive APS patients have led investigators to ask if alternative reliable and robust methodologies can be implemented in place of the LA assay.

Arvieux et al. developed an ELISA for the detection of aPT antibodies [7]. Since this publication, several formulations of aPT-based assays have been developed, evaluated and are being used in some research and clinical settings [8-13]. In some of these published studies, investigators have asked whether the different forms of aPT antibodies exist with LA, aCL and/or $\mathsf{a}\beta_{2}\mathsf{GPI}$ . Matsuda et al. [8] reported the presence of aPT and aPS/PT in LA-positive patients while Atsumi et al. [9] showed that aPS/PT and aCL have similar diagnostic value for APS patients and proposed that the aPS/PT marker be explored as a clinical test.

The conditions necessary to detect aPT antibodies associated with APS as well as questions regarding their clinical utility in these

<!-- page 2 -->
patients remain contentious. It is recognized that the presentation of PT in solid phase appears to be important in the detection of aPT antibodies. Indeed, these antibodies have been shown to bind only if immobilized on a suitable anionic surface or exposed to an anionic phospholipid [9,14] or adsorbed on an irradiated surface [7]. Thus, studies in which the diagnostic and/or prognostic role of these assays have been sought have yielded controversies as demonstrated by a recent comparative study of in-house developed and commercial assays [15]. Furthermore, a previous study by Matsuda et al. [10] showed that aPT and aPT/PS antibodies show partial, but not total, identity. Thus, neither the aPT nor aPS/PT assay is currently recommended to make a diagnosis of definite APS [1].

In this study, we investigated the relevance of prothrombin (PT) alone, phosphatidyl serine/prothrombin complex (PS/PT), and phosphatidyl serine (PS) with $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ as cofactor in the diagnosis of patients with APS and RPL. We also evaluated the diagnostic performance of 2 commercial aPS/PT antibody kits and asked if they can be used interchangeably. In addition, we determined if these markers provide additional diagnostic utility in the setting of patients with recurrent pregnancy loss.

# 2. Materials and method

# 2.1. Study population

This is a retrospective study made up of 158 individuals in 3 distinct groups. These include 62 antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (APS) patients, 66 women with recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL) and 30 women with one or more successful pregnancies (WSP). The characteristics of the study cohorts have previously been described in our 2 published studies [16,17]. Briefly, patients in the APS group were selected from a group of previously diagnosed individuals based on the revised International Consensus criteria for the diagnosis of definite APS [1]. All patients in this group were recruited at the University of Utah Teaching Hospital for the APS Registry (APSCORE), while women in the RPL cohort were enrolled at the University of Utah Hospital and LDS Hospital, Salt Lake City, Utah. In addition to testing for LA, aCL and $\alpha \beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ (IgG and IgM) antibodies, RPL patients were also evaluated for Factor V Leiden, prothrombin mutation G20210, thyroid stimulating hormone and by hysterosalpingography (HSG). In specific cases with RPL, the luteal phase progesterone levels were tested and individuals karyotyped. All samples from the APS and RPL patients were collected between July 2003 and October 2006. Sera from the WSP group were obtained in February 2007. All samples were aliquoted and stored at $-80^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ until used. The study protocol received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the University of Utah, Salt Lake City and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

# 2.2. Lupus ANTICOAGULANT and ELISA for the detection of aPL antibodies

Lupus anticoagulant (LA) testing was performed according to the guidelines of the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis, ISTH [6] for patients in the APS and RPL groups, with screening using a lupus anticoagulant-sensitive activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) and dilute Russell's viper venom time (DRVVT). All positive results were confirmed by mixing studies as recommended by the ISTH guidelines [6]. As previously reported [17], assays for aCL were performed either using a previously published "in-house" assay [18] employing serum standards derived from the Antiphospholipid Standardization Laboratory [19,20] or aCL kit from INOVA Diagnostics (San Diego, California). All subjects were tested for IgG and IgM $\mathsf{a}\beta_{2}\mathsf{GPI}$ using kits from INOVA. The cut-off values for the aCL and $\mathsf{a}\beta_{2}\mathsf{GPI}$ assays were determined by the manufacturer (INOVA) using a percentile-based method as described in our earlier study [17]. For aCL, antibody levels $>20$ GPL or MPL (IgG or IgM phospholipid

antibodies) was considered positive. In the case of $\mathsf{a}\beta_{2}\mathsf{GPI}$ antibodies, antibody levels $>20$ SGU or MGU (standard IgG or standard IgM units respectively) were considered positive. For this study, we evaluated 2 aPS/PT kits (INOVA and AESKU Diagnostics, Wendelsheim, Germany) as well as individual aPS and aPT (IgG and IgM) assays from AESKU. All assays utilized a six-point calibration and were performed manually by a single individual with experience in ELISA methodology. The cut-off determination for all the AESKU aPL assays was 15 Units while that of INOVA aPS/PT assays were set at 30 Units.

# 2.3. Statistical analysis

For binary analysis of the data, cut-offs were applied as suggested by the manufacturers to create positive and negative values from the continuous original observations. Composite tests were also derived from combinations of these binary outcomes; if either parent test was positive, derived composite was positive for all composite tests. Diagnostic performance was assessed by calculating sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) for each antibody assay considering aCL and $\mathsf{a}\beta_{2}\mathsf{GPI}$ as the gold standard unless otherwise stated. Receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to compare sensitivity and specificity thresholds for varying cut-offs for all assays. In the absence of LA for the WSP group, aCL and $\mathsf{a}\beta_{2}\mathsf{GPI}$ were used as the gold standard unless otherwise stated. Agreement between assays was then quantified using Cohen's $\kappa$ statistic. $\kappa$ coefficients $>0.75$ signify substantial agreement [21]. Three Venn diagrams were produced via 2-way tables to visually demonstrate positive concordance between the antibody assays in all study groups. Statistical analysis, tables for Venn diagrams, confidence interval estimation, and post-hoc power calculations were performed using SAS software, ver. 9.1 of the SAS System SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC. ROC curves were created using STATA software, StataCorp. 2007. Stata Statistical Software: Release 10. College Station, TX.

# 3. Results

# 3.1. Population characteristics and distribution of aPL antibodies

The study population comprised of 62 patients with APS associated with adverse pregnancy outcome and/or thrombosis. The mean age $(\pm \mathrm{SD}$ years) of patients in the APS group was $35.7\pm 11.5$ years. Of these patients, 23 $(37.1\%)$ had adverse pregnancy outcome only, 13 $(21.0\%)$ had thrombosis only, and 26 $(41.9\%)$ had adverse pregnancy outcome and thrombosis. The disease control group consisted of 66 women (mean $\pm \mathrm{SD}$ : $33.1\pm 5.7$ years) with RPL, while 30 healthy women with successful pregnancies (WSP) made up the third group (mean $\pm \mathrm{SD}$ : $34.0\pm 8.5$ years). Of the original 62 patients in the APS group, serum samples were available for 58 patients for the measurement of aPS/PT and aPT antibodies. As reported in our 2 previous studies [16,17], LA activity was found in 27/59 $(45.8\%)$ patients of the APS cohort (data not shown). The prevalence of single marker PL IgG or IgM antibodies ranged from $39.9\%$ (aPT IgG/IgM) to $83.9\%$ (aCL IgG/IgM). The distribution of the various aPL antibodies in the different groups is detailed in Table 1.

The frequency of the aPS or aPT in combination with aCL and $\mathsf{a}\beta_{2}\mathsf{GP1}$ IgG or IgM antibodies was 93.6 and $93.1\%$ respectively for this group. When the LA test was replaced by the aPS/PT assays in the APS group, the per cent of APS positive individuals was $87.9\%$ for the AESKU kits and $96.6\%$ for the INOVA assays. Overall, the number of aPT antibody-positive individuals was higher in the RPL group than the aPS or aPS/PT markers. Of the 12 RPL aPT antibody-positive women $(18.5\%)$ , 5 had antibody levels between 16-19 Units (cut-off $= 15$ Units). Three of the 7 women with aPT antibody levels $>20$ Units were weakly positive for either aPS/PT or aPS antibodies.

# 3.2. Diagnostic performance of aPS/PT, aPS and aPT antibodies in APS and RPL

To determine the clinical significance of aPS/PT, aPS or aPT antibodies in the evaluation of APS patients, especially in individuals with RPL, we calculated the sensitivities, specificities, positive and negative predictive values for each marker (Table 2). These analyses were performed for each disease group; women with history of successful pregnancies served as healthy controls. The sensitivities were of the order aPS/PT > aPS > aPT for the APS group and aPT > aPS/PT > aPS for the RPL cohort. When the sensitivities were assessed for the APS with RPL as disease controls, discordant hierarchies were observed due to differences in the performance of the aPS/PT assays and high prevalence of aPT antibodies in the RPL patients (compared to the APS vs. WSP).

Overall, significant differences in the sensitivities for all markers were observed between the APS vs. WSP and the RPL vs. WSP. This is reflected in the NPVs for the aPS/PT IgG/IgM (AESKU and INOVA), aPS/PT IgG (AESKU) and the aPS IgG and IgM assays (Table 2). No differences in specificities were observed between these groups. ROC analyses were also performed to determine the overall diagnostic performance of the different assays for the diagnosis of APS (Fig. 1). The results demonstrated no significant difference in the area under curve (AUC) analyses for the 2 aPS/PT assays. In addition, no difference in diagnostic accuracy was observed between the aPS/PT and the aPS tests (AUC: aPS/PT 0.990 vs. aPS 0.980). The AUC, however, revealed significant differences between the aPS/PT (0.980) and the aPT (0.850) assays $(p = 0.002)$ . We had $>80\%$ power to detect at least a $15\%$ difference in diagnostic statistics.

# 3.3. Agreement between aPL antibodies in study population

We first determined the correlation between the antibodies investigated in this study with LA activity in the evaluation of individuals with APS. For these analyses, we assessed the relationship between specific aPL antibodies and LA using the Cohen's $\kappa$ statistic. The agreement between LA and the 2 aPS/PT assays as well as those between the aPS or aPT and LA was poor as revealed by the $\kappa$ coefficients (Table 3). The $\kappa$ coefficient demonstrated substantial agreement between the 2 aPS/PT assays.

To ascertain the diagnostic utility of testing either aPS or aPT antibodies in addition to the aPS/PT markers for APS and RPL, we also evaluated the agreement for these markers in the study cohort. As with the 2 aPS/PT assays, the aPS and aPS/PT as well as the aPT and aPS/PT assays showed considerable but not total agreement (Table 3). This report describes the diagnostic performance of 2 formulations of aPT (aPT and aPS/PT) as well as aPS IgG and IgM assays for the evaluation of APS. We also determined the performance characteristics of these antibodies in women with RPL and asked if there is redundancy in testing for all three markers. Overall, our results showed that the aPS/PT as well as the aPS and aPT assays cannot be used in place of the LA for the evaluation of patients with APS. The ROC analyses showed significant difference in diagnostic performance between the aPT and aPS/PT assays but none between the aPS/PT and aPS tests. Most significantly, no difference in the diagnostic accuracies was observed between the 2 aPS/PT assays.

A key challenge for the laboratory is to offer clinically relevant diagnostic tools to the clinician. In this regard, proper test utilization can only be achieved when the limitations of diagnostic tools are recognized by both the clinical scientists and clinicians. The case of APS and the numerous tests offered presents a diagnostic and clinical dilemma when the only test positive is one whose performance characteristics are largely unknown and/or debatable. In our previous studies, we showed that IgG and IgM antibodies to phosphatidic acid, phosphatidyl choline, phosphatidyl ethanolamine, phosphatidyl glycerol, phosphatidyl inositol and phosphatidyl serine with and without $\beta$ -2 glycoprotein I ( $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ ) are not clinically relevant in the diagnosis of APS [16,17]. In this study, we extended the breadth of markers associated with APS to include aPT and aPS/PT antibodies which have been identified to have some probable relevance for these conditions [1,8-13]. Realizing that phosphatidyl serine is a component of the aPS/PT complex, we also included these assays in our current analyses. Three important factors that make our investigation clinically relevant include i) the disease and control groups studied, ii) simultaneous measurements for all components of the aPS/PT complex for these individuals and iii) strict adherence to the recommended guidelines for diagnosing definite APS.

Our data clearly demonstrate that the aPS or aPT antibodies are components of the aPS/PT antibody repertoire in the APS patients. Although the finding that aPT and aPS/PT antibodies show partial but not complete identity is not entirely new [10], this result is worth reiterating since we have used 2 commercially available assays. We were very surprised by the lack of agreement with the 2 aPS/PT assays and the LA tests in the APS group as several studies have suggested an exclusive association between these 2 analytes [7-9]. The lack of agreement between the LA and the 2 aPS/PT assays tested in this study may suggest that this marker form a minor part of the LA activity and as such cannot be used entirely to replace the later. Alternatively, the assays as designed are not optimized for this purpose.

It is possible that the aPS/PT assays may not be relevant in evaluating APS in patients with RPL, since the presence of aPS/PT antibodies in these patients may be independent of their LA status. Indeed, aPS/PT has been shown to predict thrombosis in individuals with APS and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [9] and to be of limited diagnostic utility in RPL [11]. With respect to aPT antibodies, increased prevalence of these antibodies is also associated with elevated risk for thrombosis [12,22,23]. As our cohort substantially lacks individuals with thrombosis only (13 out of 62 APS patients), we speculate that our study population might have created a bias in the correlation between the aPS/PT assays and LA activity. Further studies with thrombotic APS patients are needed to clarify the association between LA and aPS/PT assays especially as these tools are becoming readily available.

There are few limitations with our study which may constrain how some of our data is interpreted. For example, given the small sample size of the healthy control group, it is difficult to ascertain the significance of the increased prevalence of the aPT antibodies in the RPL cohort observed. Since most of the individuals in this group were lost to follow-up, the clinical significance of these findings remains unknown. Secondly, as earlier alluded, our APS population is largely

comprised of women with adverse pregnancy outcomes. The predictive values of these assays are very likely to change in different subsets of patients. Another potential drawback of our study is the use of archived samples which may have an impact on the stability of the antibodies tested. Although we have not addressed stability of these antibodies in this investigation, our previous studies [16,17] and unpublished data shows that these antibodies are very stable if stored properly. In addition, the use of previously frozen sera has been used by others in studies of this kind [8-14]. Lastly, a major limitation in evaluating newer diagnostic tools for APS is the inherent hurdle of comparing performance with existing gold standards. In the case of APS, the gold standard assays are less than perfect.

In conclusion, the results of this study show that the serologic markers aPS, aPT and aPS/PT show considerable overlap in the diagnosis of patients with APS associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes. Patients who test positive for the aPS/PT IgG and IgM antibodies are most likely to be positive for either aPS and/or aPT markers. The lack of agreement between the aPS/PT assays and LA in our study cohort may be attributable to the low prevalence of thrombosis in our patients. Further studies are needed to clarify the role of these antibodies in patients with APS who have either RPL or thrombosis only.
