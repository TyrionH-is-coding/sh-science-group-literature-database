# Problem

Anti- $\beta_{2}$ -Glicoprotein-1 antibodies (anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI-ab) have been related to recurrent miscarriage (RM) with conflicting results. The aim was to evaluate the role of anti- $\beta_{2}$ -GPI-ab as unique biological marker in RM related to antiphospholipid (aPL).

# Method of study

A cohort study that included 59 cases, divided in two groups, was designed: group 1 comprised 43 pregnant women with 'obstetric' antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) and group 2 included 16 cases with similar complaints but only having repeatedly anti- $\beta_{2}$ -GPI-ab. Previous thrombosis and/or inherited thrombophilia were excluded. Lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin antibodies (aCA), anti- $\beta_{2}$ -GPI-ab, and other autoantibodies were analyzed. Miscarriages, premature births, preeclampsia, live births, placental and systemic thromboses were studied.

# Results

No differences in previous obstetric complications were detected $(P = 1.00 - 0.164)$ . After the treatment, differences in number of obstetric complications were not seen $(P = 1.00)$ . Live births were similar in two groups (88.4% and 93.7%; $P = 1.00$ ). Placental thrombosis was equal in both groups, 93.3% versus 80% $(P = 1.00)$ .

# Conclusion

These results suggest that anti- $\beta_{2}$ -GPI-ab may be considered a biological marker for obstetric APS.

# Introduction

Anticardiolipin antibodies (aCL) and lupus anticoagulant (LAC) are associated with thrombosis, recurrent miscarriage, fetal demise and generally with poor fetal outcome. $^{1-5}$ The incidence of aCL in the general obstetric population is $1 - 5\%$ , $^{6,7}$ and ranging

from $20\%$ to $40\%$ in patients with a poor obstetric history. $^{1,7,8}$ Since the first description of this syndrome by Hughes et al., $^{3}$ as antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), some attempts were made to change the APS classification criteria. $^{9-11}$ Until the last workshop held in Sydney, Australia, in 2005 and until the publication of the new recommendations

<!-- page 2 -->
for the classification criteria for definite APS in January 2006, $^{12}$ only the presence of LAC and/or aCL were considered as laboratory markers. Before these recommendations, it was impossible to classify the patients with identical clinical manifestations without LAC or aCL, although other antiphospholipid (aPL) antibodies were present, or in the best of cases, it was classified as seronegative APS. $^{13,14}$ Therefore, the treatment for APS was difficult to recommend to seronegative patients.

Although it was initially thought that aPL reacts directly with phospholipids, subsequent studies have shown that critical epitopes also involve a number of phospholipids-binding proteins.[15] The most prevalent of proteins is $\beta_{2}$ -glycoprotein I.[15,16] Considering that $\beta_{2}$ -glycoprotein I ( $\beta_{2}$ GPI) is a main target of aPL antibodies, anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies may play a role in their pathogenesis and may also serve as a biological marker for APS.[15,17] In the end, members of the workshop panel meet in Sydney before the Eleventh International Congress on aPL antibodies, agreed that IgG and IgM anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies should be added to the definition of APS as part of the modified Sapporo criteria.[11,12] However, some members of this workshop panel still do not agree with these selection criteria.[12]

Therefore, the aim of our study was to determine whether those patients who tested positive for anti $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies will have the same maternal and fetal outcomes as the classical APS patients.

# Materials and methods

# Study Group

We prospectively studied a cohort of 200 women diagnosed as having 'idiopathic spontaneous recurrent miscarriages', recruited in our institutions, a tertiary teaching hospital (Vall d'Hebron University Hospital) and private university hospital (Dexeus University Institute) between January 1996 and December 2005. Almost 120 cases have been diagnosed as having some different diseases, such as, inherited thrombophilia, aPL-associated autoimmune disease, such as SLE, alloimmune abnormalities or combination of some of them. In approximately $20\%$ of them, exact etiology could not be determined. Finally, 59 cases were only positive for aPL and included into the study.

We gave verbal information to the patients. Besides, this study was approved by the ethics com

mittee of the Vall d'Hebron University Hospital (CEIC-HUVH).

# Patients' inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) women aged 18 or older with no history of previous live births; (ii) background of three or more consecutive unexplained spontaneous miscarriage before the 10th week of gestation (formerly referred to as habitual miscarriage). Only the cases with postembryonic loss after an ultrasound with fetal pulse were included in the study; (iii) one or more unexplained fetal losses beyond 10th week of gestational age; (iv) one or more premature births before 34 weeks of gestation on account of severe preeclampsia or placental insufficiency; (v) no maternal anatomic uterine malformations or hormonal dysfunctions; (vi) no chromosomal abnormalities in the couple; (vii) no previous arterial or venous thrombosis; (viii) absence of any clinical or laboratory data that may act as the marker for any systemic autoimmune disease, mainly systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE); and (ix) negativity of all blood test performed for heritable thrombophilias.

In the end, only pregnant women with clinical obstetric complications with APS or anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies were included. Therefore, the study group was composed of 59 cases that were divided in two groups: the first group (group 1) was constituted of 43 pregnant women that fulfilled the classical, full-blown Sapporo classification criteria for APS.[11] The second group (group 2) included 16 pregnant patients with bad obstetric outcomes, mainly recurrent miscarriages and/or fetal losses with IgM and/or IgG anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies repeatedly positive, at least twice 6 weeks apart, prior to becoming pregnant again, although they did not have aCL or LAC in their sera. Since January 2007, we required that positivity for aPL was confirmed at least twice, 12 weeks apart.

# Methods

# Clinical assessment

Patients were assisted by an obstetrician and by an internist or immunologist. All patients were asked about their medical history and after that, were submitted to our protocol for the diagnosis of recurrent or habitual miscarriage: glucose tolerance test, karyotypes of both parents, hysteroscopy or hysterosalpingogram, endometrial biopsy, hormonal deter

<!-- page 3 -->
minations (TSH, prolactine) and cervical cultures, with negative or normal results. Specific laboratory screening tests were: ANA, anti-DNA, aCL (IgG/IgM isotypes), lupus anticoagulant, anti-mitochondrial antibodies (AMA), rapid plasma reagin (RPR) or Venereal Disease Research Laboratory (VDRL) (associated with a treponemic test when necessary), antithyroglobulin/antimicrosomal (antithyroid) antibodies and in some patients in group 1 and in all patients in group 2, anti-beta2-glycoprotein-I antibodies (IgG/IgM isotypes), C4 complement and gammaglobulin levels were also performed. Blood test for inherited thrombophilias [C-Protein, S-Protein, activated protein C resistance, Anti-thrombin III (ATIII), factor II and factor VIII measurements was also done for all patients]. Factor II (G20210A) gene mutation was performed in all patients. Methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) gene mutations and Factor V mutation-Factor V Leiden were respectively analyzed only when plasma homocysteine levels were high and when activated protein C resistance were demonstrated.

After the patients became pregnant, the clinical assessment was carried out, including fetal-maternal ultrasound-Doppler studies from 20 weeks of gestation, according to the following schedule: each two months up to 24 weeks of gestation; each month between 24 and 32 weeks of gestation and, each 15 days until delivery. At least three blood samples looking for aPL were taken and analyzed in each patient through pregnancy.

# Therapeutic schedule

Low dose of acetylsalicylic acid (ASA) $100\mathrm{mg/day}$ prior to conception, plus enoxaparin $(40 - 60\mathrm{mg/day})$ when pregnancy was confirmed, was recommended to all pregnant women. When overweight and/or other non-inherited thrombophilic situations were present, a high prophylactic dose of enoxaparin $(1\mathrm{mg/kg/day})$ or higher dose (therapeutic dose, $1\mathrm{mg/kg/twice})$ was given.

# Laboratory Analysis

# Antiphospholipid antibody detection

Anticardiolipin antibodies were assayed by using standardized enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) according to Harris criteria, defining the normal cut-off value in the assay as the mean IgG or IgM units plus two standard deviations of a healthy patient group. Reagents were supplied by Wampole

Lab., Dist. (Division of Carter-Wallace, Inc, Cranbury, NJ, USA). Results were expressed in either GPL or MPL according to the following scale: aCL-IgG $< 10$ units negative $(-)$ ; 10-20 units low positive $(+)$ ; 20-60 units medium $(++)$ and $>60$ were considered high positive $(+++)$ and for aCL-IgM isotypes: $< 4$ units, negative $(-)$ ; 4-20 units low positive $(+)$ , 20-60 units medium $(++)$ and $>60$ units high positive $(+++)$ . A coagulation screening using standard methods consists of the prothrombin time and active partial thromboplastin time. We used reagents supplied by DADE Grifols Lab., Barcelona, Spain and Bio Merieux S.A., Marcy-l'Etoile, France. Lupus anticoagulant was confirmed according to the following steps: (i) prolonged APTT or dilute Russell's time, (ii) failure to correct the prolonged APTT or analogs tested by mixing with normal platelet-poor plasma (1:1), (iii) shortening or correcting the prolonged coagulation time on the initial test by the addition of phospholipids, and (iv) exclusion of other coagulopathies whenever it was considered. APTT p/c ratios between 1.3 and 1.8 were considered as a low potency, 1.9-2.5 medium and, $>2.5$ as a high LAC potency.

# Anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI Antibody Detection

To measure anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibody, we used an anti $\beta 2$ GPI antibody kit supplied for DLD Diagnostika GMBH, Hamburg (Germany). Reagents were all stored at $2 - 6^{\circ}C$ . All reagents were allowed to reach room temperature before its use. Purified human $\beta_{2}$ -GPI was coated onto the surface of the microwells. Diluted serum specimens (1:51) were incubated for $30\mathrm{min}$ to permit to $\beta_{2}$ -GPI to bind to the plastic surface. After washing away unbound antibodies and serum constituents, the specific antibodies were detected by anti-IgG peroxidase. The TMB/peroxidase reaction is monitored at $450~\mathrm{nm}$ optical density in a microplate photometer within $10\mathrm{min}$ . As indicated by the manufacturer, the positive results must be considered above $10~\mathrm{U / mL}$ . We tested it in our laboratory, obtaining the same observations, as well. The statistical basis for significant differences between values is established by the criterion (cut-off) of mean plus 2S.D. (versus healthy women control group).

# Determination of other autoantibodies

Antinuclear antibodies were determined by an indirect fluorescent antibody method using HEp-2 cells and reagents supplied by BioSystems, S.A., Barcelona, Spain. Sera with $1+$ or greater fluorescence at

Values $>15\mathrm{U}$ were considered as positive. Anti-Ro and anti-La antibodies were analyzed by hemagglutination method, supplied by Cormedica, Hemagen Diagnostics Inc., Boston, MA, USA. # Other tests analyzed

C4 was analyzed using a standard nephelometry method supplied by Array Systems, Beckman Instruments, Fullerton, CA, USA (NV: 20-45 mg/dL).

Homocysteine was assayed by quimiolumincence (Immulite 2000), supplied by DPC, Los Angeles, CA, USA, considering normal values lower than $15\mu \mathrm{mol} / \mathrm{L}$ in non-pregnant women and, lower than $12\mu \mathrm{mol} / \mathrm{L}$ in the pregnant women.

# Inherited thrombophilias determination

Activated protein C sensitivity ratio (APC-SR) was determined by the addition of APC to a PTT assay without factor V deficiency plasma dilution, using a Coatest APC resistance kit by Chromogenix (Milano, Italy). The mean value ratio in women was 2.4-4.5. Protein S activity was analyzed by coagulometric method, using a StaCLot Protein S Clotting assay supplied by Diagnostica Stago (Parsippany, NJ, USA). Values lower than $50\%$ were diagnostic of PS deficiency. Protein C activity was measured by a coagulometric method, using a StaCLot Protein C Clotting assay supplied also by Diagnostica Stago. Values lower than $70\%$ were diagnostic of PC deficiency. Levels of antithrombin were also measured by a chromogenic method using Berichrom Antithronbin III (A) supplied by Dade Behring Marburg GmbH, Marburg, Germany, with values below $80\%$ considered abnormal.

# Detection of thrombophilic polymorphisms

Factor V G1691A (factor V Leiden), factor II G20210A and MTHFR C677T polymorphisms were detected by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with further digestion. All retrieved fragments were stud

ied by electrophoresis on $10\%$ polyacrylamide gels and visualized by ethidium bromide staining.

# Statistical Analysis

The results are expressed as mean $\pm$ standard deviation (S.D.) and count (proportion). We analyzed relations between independent variables according to group using exact Fisher's test to compare categorical variables and Student's $t$ -test to compare continuous variables with normal distribution. A $P$ value $< 0.05$ was considered statistically significant.

# Results

There were a total of 59 patients selected for the study. The patients were classified in two groups: 43 of them in group 1 and 16 in group 2. The demographic characteristics and main clinical obstetrical features of the groups are listed in Table I. Briefly, no differences were observed in the medical history of patients before the treatment. The characteristics considered were: the number of first-trimester miscarriages, fetal losses, preeclampsia, abruption of placenta and premature births. Placental thrombosis was found in 14 of 15 cases $(93.3\%)$ from the group 1 and four of five cases $(80\%)$ from the group 2 $(P = 1.00)$ .

The Table I shows the outcome data for these groups after treatment. When we compared all the miscarriages (first-trimester miscarriages and fetal losses), we found no differences between them (10/43 versus 3/16), $(P = 1.000)$ .

The number of live births was similar in both groups, 38/43 cases $(88.4\%)$ in group 1, and 15/16 $(93.7\%)$ in group 2 $(P = 1.00)$ . Overall, the fetal survival rate in this series was $89\%$ . We have then evaluated thrombosis up to a mean of 5.6 years $(r: 1-10$ years) after pregnancies, and we have seen that thromboses appeared in 7/43 cases in group 1 $(16\%)$ and 1/16 $(6.5\%)$ in group 2 $(P = 0.42)$ . Arterial thrombosis (stroke) affected only one patient from group 1. The rest of the cases were diagnosed as having venous thromboembolic disease. And apart from systemic thromboses, we have also looked for livedo reticularis as a possible systemic cutaneous manifestation associated with aPL/APS, but no differences were recorded between the groups $(22\%$ versus $12.5\%; P = 0.71)$ .

This could be an important point, because some authors as Gleicher et al.[29,30] have found that there is an increase of miscarriages when either, hypergammaglobulinemia and/or non-organ-specific autoantibodies and/or low C4 complement level were present, independently of aPL.

The similar live birth rate in the two groups who were treated with the same therapeutic schedule, did not provide enough information about the role of anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies as a pathogenic molecule but it underlined its importance as a biological marker. Therefore, we were able to establish a new group of patients with increased risk of adverse pregnancy outcome related to aPL and this permitted us to offer standard treatment to these patients. It could be argued whether anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies have impact or not on the risk of obstetrical complications, irrespective of treatment with LMWH/ASA. However, in the past, patients of group 2 had an adverse pregnancy outcome without treatment with LMWH/ASA, which illustrated higher risk of obstetric complications.

No venous/arterial thrombosis, other than placental, was diagnosed during the pregnancies. In the follow-up, up to 10 years, the number of thrombotic episodes in both groups was lower than expected in APS,[36,37] only six cases in group 1 (16%) and one case (6.5%) in group 2. Arterial thrombosis (stroke) affected only one patient from group 1. We believe that these results could be explained by the specific and peculiar aPL-related injury during pregnancies (obstetric APS), and because all women were treated long term with low-dose aspirin after delivery. Apart from that, we looked for livedo reticularis as a possible systemic cutaneous manifestation associated with aPL/APS,[12] but we found no differences between the groups. The prevalence of thrombosis in live births and children from mothers with APS is very low.[38] Until now, we have not found any clinical, arterial or venous thromboses, in the babies and children of our patients.

As mentioned before, we conducted this study with LAC/aCL, which were the only accepted serological APS markers. At the time of the study, women that had the same complications as classic APS but were LAC and aCL negatives, were not classified or diagnosed as having seronegative APS (anti-cofactor syndrome). Therefore, the standard treatments for definite - seropositive-APS patients were not fully administered in the seronegative-APS patients.

This study has two limitations. The first one is that the number of patients included is relatively small, especially in the group 2, because the inclusion criteria were very strict. A second limitation of this study is a lack of a control group. It's difficult to have a control group of women with history of recurrent bad obstetric outcomes if we know that no treatment could mean a high risk of another miscarriage and a possible thrombotic risk for the mother. Besides, we had stored plasma samples of six cases included in group 2 before anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies were available. These cases were not treated and no live births were observed. Once again these results seem to give validity to anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies as markers of pregnancy risk.

As we mentioned above, the rest of known causes of bad obstetric outcomes, including inherited thrombophilias, were ruled out. It was mandatory for the group 2 to document negative results for all analyzed inherited thrombophilias, as well.

Although some of the recent papers suggested that the detection of anti $\beta_{2}$ -GPI antibodies is associated with an increased risk of pregnancy loss,[12,39-41] not all authors agree that these obstetric-related manifestations are associated with anti $\beta_{2}$ -GPI antibodies.[9,12,13,42] The lack of consistent data on the prevalence and association with clinical manifestations of anti $\beta_{2}$ -GPI antibodies may be on account of patient selection, antibody heterogeneity and their affinity for human $\beta_{2}$ GPI or methodological variation (irradiation of microplaques, coating procedure, calibration, and choices of cut-off levels).

In summary, our results have shown that there were no clinical differences between seropositive and seronegative - antibeta2-GPI antibody positive-groups when we looked into their obstetric complications and neonatal outcomes. Anti-beta2-glycoprotein I antibodies may act as markers of obstetric APS in pregnant women with poor obstetric outcomes and with recurrent negative blood tests for LAC/aCL. Thus, these cases, in the past named 'seronegative APS', could be diagnosed as having APS, and given appropriate treatment. In our series, this interpretation has allowed us to improve the number of live births in more than $90\%$ of treated cases.

Detection of anti-phospholipid-cofactor antibodies, 'atypical' aPL antibodies or other non-usually tested aPL antibodies, in addition to the classic aCL and LAC antibodies seem to be clinically important and should be considered in cases with adverse clinical obstetric history and negative tests for aCL/LAC.

However, the number and heterogeneity of the target antigens of aPL antibodies, and the fact that the aPLs may belong to various immunoglobulin classes, would require 10 to 12 different tests to provide a complete investigation and to obtain the maximum sensitivity, which would entail an unacceptable cost-benefit ratio. Urgent randomized clinical trials with a larger population are needed to offer more answers on this subject.
