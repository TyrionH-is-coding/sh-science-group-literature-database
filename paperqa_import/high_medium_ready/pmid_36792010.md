Running title: Anticoagulant as a precipitating factor of CAPS

# Essentials:

- The prevention of catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (CAPS) is a major goal.   
- We conducted a multicentre retrospective study of 112 patients with at least one CAPS episode.   
- A minority of patients were receiving optimal anticoagulant treatment when their CAPS occurred.   
- Suboptimal anticoagulation management can trigger CAPS in antiphospholipid syndrome.

<!-- page 4 -->
# Summary

Background. The prevention of catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (CAPS), a rare complication of antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), is a major goal.

Objectives. We analysed its precipitating factors, focusing on anticoagulation immediately before CAPS episodes.

Patients/Methods. We retrospectively analysed patients in the French multicentre APS/systemic lupus erythematosus database with at least one CAPS episode. Then we compared each patient with known APS before CAPS with two non-CAPS APS patients matched for age, sex, centre and APS phenotype.

Results. We included 112 CAPS patients (70% female, mean age 43±15 years). At least one standard precipitating factor of CAPS was observed for 67 patients (64%), mainly infections (n=28, 27%), pregnancy (n=23, 22%), and surgery (n=16, 15%).

Before the CAPS episode, 67 (60%) patients already had a diagnosis of APS. Of the 61 treated with anticoagulants, 32 (48%) received vitamin K antagonists (VKA), 23 (34%) heparin, and 2 (3%) a direct oral anticoagulant. They were less likely than their matched APS patients without CAPS to receive VKA (48% vs 66%, $P = 0.001$ ). Among those treated with VKA, 72% had a subtherapeutic INR (ie, $< 2$ ) versus 28% in APS patients without CAPS ( $P < 0.001$ ). Finally, excluding pregnant patients ( $n = 14$ ) for whom we could not differentiate the effect of treatment from that of pregnancy, left 47 cases, 32 (68%) of whom had recently begun a direct oral anticoagulant, planned bridging therapy, or had VKA treatment with INR $< 2$ .

Conclusion. These results strongly suggest that suboptimal anticoagulation management can trigger CAPS in patients with thrombotic APS.

Keywords: anticoagulant, antiphospholipid antibodies, antiphospholipid syndrome, autoimmune diseases, catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome.

# INTRODUCTION

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is defined by the combination of thrombosis and/or pregnancy morbidity, with persistent antiphospholipid (aPL) antibodies, specifically, various combinations of lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin antibodies (aCL), and anti-Beta2-glycoprotein-1 antibodies (antiB2GP1) [1]. The keystone of thrombotic APS treatment is long-term anticoagulant therapy by vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) maintaining an international normalised ratio (INR) $\geq 2$ [2]. Although relatively rare, with $1\%$ to $5\%$ of APS patients affected, the catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (CAPS) remains, as its name suggests, its most severe variant, with an overall mortality rate of $36\%$ [3-5]. This syndrome is characterised by a rapid onset of multiple microthromboses involving at least three organs or systems [6]. Knowledge about this condition comes mainly from the international CAPS registry, which collected data from 500 patients in 2016 [7].

The role of precipitating factors in CAPS development is well known. In the international registry, at least one factor has been identified in $65\%$ of the episodes (and two or more in $13\%$ ). The most frequent triggering factors listed are infections $(49\%)$ , surgery $(17\%)$ , malignancy $(16\%)$ , hormonal contraception $(10\%)$ , pregnancy $(8\%)$ , drugs $(5\%)$ , and lupus flares $(3\%)$ [7]. However, data on the type of anticoagulant (VKAs, heparin, or direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs)) and the INR levels in those on VKA before CAPS episodes is not available in the CAPS registry, and little is known about the specific role of anticoagulant treatment as a potential precipitating factor for CAPS.

In our experience, an imbalance of the long-term anticoagulant therapy, especially the cessation of VKAs or their inefficacy due to low INR, is frequently observed just before the onset of CAPS. We also recently reported two cases of CAPS occurring immediately after the introduction of DOACs [8]. Since a better understanding of the factors triggering CAPS is likely to help prevent it, we analysed precipitating factors, with a special focus on anticoagulation management just before its onset in a large cohort of patients. We aim to

<!-- page 6 -->
describe the precipitating factors of CAPS among patients with APS and to compare the anticoagulation management between patients who had developed CAPS and those with APS who did not.

# PATIENTS AND METHODS

Patients. We analysed patients included in the French multicentre APS/systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) register (clinicaltrials.gov NCT02782039, previously described by Morel et al [9]). This anonymised web-based register was created in 2015 by two French centres (Cochin Hospital in Paris and Claude Huriez Hospital in Lille). Eight additional centres have contributed to the inclusion of CAPS patients since then.

This study included all patients with at least one CAPS episode (n=112). These occurred between 1990 and 2019, including 108 (96%) since 2000. A senior physician (NM and/or NCC) carefully reviewed all the CAPS patients' files.

To assess anticoagulation management as a precipitating factor of CAPS, each noninaugural CAPS patient was matched by age, sex, centre and phenotype (arterial, venous, or obstetric) to two APS patients who have never had a CAPS episode also included in the APS/SLE register. These non-CAPS APS controls were selected among the 485 patients with definite APS included in the APS/SLE register. To compare INR, we studied all patients on VKA: for the cases, we used the INR at CAPS diagnosis if available, and for controls, we used the first INR available in our register.

In accordance with ethical standards in France for non-interventional research, written consent was not required for this observational study, but patients were informed of its existence and of their right to oppose the use of their data. The study has been approved by an Institutional Review Board (CPP Ile-de-France VI, Paris, February, 2014).

Definitions. Antiphospholipid syndrome was defined according to the Sydney APS

<!-- page 7 -->
classification criteria [1]. Cases were determined according to the international CAPS preliminary classification criteria [6] by: (1) evidence of involvement of three or more organs, systems, and/or tissues; (2) development of manifestations simultaneously or within less than one week; (3) confirmation by histopathology of small-vessel occlusion in at least one organ or tissue; and (4) laboratory confirmation of the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies (lupus anticoagulant and/or aCL of IgG and/or IgM isotype in titres $>40$ or $>99^{\text{th}}$ percentile measured by a standardised ELISA and/or anti-B2GP1 antibody of IgG and/or IgM isotype in titres $>99^{\text{th}}$ percentile measured by a standardised ELISA). Definite CAPS was defined by the presence of all four criteria. Probable CAPS was defined by: (a) all four criteria except only two organs, systems, and/or tissues involved; (b) all four criteria except the absence of laboratory confirmation (within at least 12 weeks), owing to the early death of the patient, never previously tested for aPL before the CAPS event; (c) absence of histopathology confirmation; (d) criteria (1), (3), and (4) with development of the manifestations within less than one month [6]. Both definite and probable CAPS are included in this study. Systemic lupus erythematosus was defined according to the Systemic Lupus International Collaboration Clinics classification criteria [10].

Thrombotic phenotypes (arterial or venous) among CAPS patients were defined by APS manifestations that had occurred before CAPS onset (thromboses during the CAPS episode were not taken into account). For patients who had presented more than one episode of CAPS, we considered the first episode only.

Data. We analysed the standard precipitating factors (infections, surgery, malignancy, pregnancy, and lupus flare) but also the management of the anticoagulant treatment (VKA/ heparin/DOAC) before the CAPS onset in patients with previously diagnosed APS (that is, non-inaugural CAPS). For patients treated with VKA, we collected the INR values when available.

<!-- page 8 -->
Statistical analysis. The categorical (qualitative) variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages, and the continuous (quantitative) variables as means $\pm$ standard deviations (SD) or medians [minimum-maximum (min-max)]. Patients' characteristics and treatments were compared by paired t-tests for continuous and paired chi-2 tests for categorical variables. The INRs of patients under VKA were compared by two different methods: continuous INR with Wilcoxon tests, and categorical INR (<2, 2-3, and >3). This analysis was conducted with R version 3.6.1. (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

# RESULTS

Patient characteristics. We included 112 patients with at least one episode of CAPS at a mean $\pm$ SD age of $43 \pm 15$ years; 78 (70%) were female. SLE was associated with APS in 43 patients (38%). Lupus anticoagulant was present in 100 patients (90%), aCL in 100 (90%), and anti-B2GP1 in 73 (66%), and triple positivity for all three antibodies observed in 65 (58%). The main clinical and biological characteristics of these patients are detailed in Table 1.

CAPS was inaugural in the history of APS of 45 patients $(40\%)$ , while it occurred a median of 13 years [1 month-48 years] after the diagnosis of APS in the other 67 $(60\%)$ . The clinical presentation of CAPS was highly variable. Renal, cardiac and cutaneous involvement were the most frequent manifestations, present respectively in 85 $(76\%)$ , 74 $(66\%)$ and 62 $(55\%)$ patients. Thrombocytopenia (platelets below $100,000 / \mathrm{mm}^3$ ) was present in 80 patients $(76\%, 7$ missing data) with a mean platelet level of $71,083 / \mathrm{mm}^3$ . The first CAPS episode was fatal for 8 $(7\%)$ patients.

Standard precipitating factors. At least one standard precipitating factor was found for 67 patients (64%, 7 missing data) including 21 (20%) who presented two or more such

<!-- page 9 -->
factors. They were infections (n=28, 27%), pregnancy (n=23, 22%), surgery (n=16, 15%), a lupus flare (n=7, 7%) and/or malignancy (n=2, 2%) (Table 1).

Anticoagulation as a precipitating factor. Among the 112 patients, 67 had been diagnosed with APS before the CAPS onset: 61 of them were treated with anticoagulants before that event, one was untreated as she had obstetric APS only, and data were missing for 5 patients (see Figure 1). The anticoagulants used were VKA $(n = 32$ , $48\%)$ , heparin $(n = 23$ , $34\%$ ; therapeutic dose for all but one), and DOAC $(n = 2$ , $3\%)$ (data unknown for four patients). Among the 29 patients treated with VKA with available INR data on admission (3 missing data), the mean INR was $2.23 \pm 1.90$ and the median INR was 1.4 [interquartile range 1.1-2.8]. Twenty-one $(72\%)$ had an INR $< 2$ , 2 $(7\%)$ an INR of 2-3, and 6 $(21\%)$ an INR $> 3$ . The reasons for heparin treatment $(n = 23)$ rather than VKA were pregnancy $(n = 14$ , $61\%)$ or bridging therapy $(n = 9$ , $39\%)$ (Figure 1). Heparin consisted in low molecular weight heparin $(n = 22)$ or unfractionated heparin $(n = 1)$ . Measurements of blood heparin level were available for 11 patients with a median of heparinemia of $0.21~\mathrm{UI/mL}$ . The CAPS of the 2 patients on DOAC occurred a median of 5 days after treatment started. Among the 67 non-inaugural CAPS patients, 6 $(9\%)$ died after the first CAPS episode.

The 67 non-inaugural CAPS patients were matched to 134 APS patients without CAPS for sex, age, centre and APS phenotype (arterial, venous or obstetrical) (Table 2). After matching, patients with non-inaugural CAPS were more likely to have positive ACL and/or LAC. They were also more likely to be treated with anticoagulants (91% versus 80%, $P = 0.024$ ), more frequently treated with heparin (34% vs 8%, $P < 0.001$ ) and less frequently with VKA (48% vs 66%, $P = 0.001$ ) before CAPS occurred.

Finally, the 29 patients treated with VKA with available INR levels at CAPS onset were compared with the 90 non-CAPS APS patients on VKA with an available INR and the same matching parameters (Table 3). INR was lower among CAPS patients compared with

<!-- page 10 -->
non-CAPS patients (median [IQR] INR: 1.40 [1.10-2.80] versus 2.20 [1.90-2.80]; $P = 0.001$ )

(Figure 2). When INR levels were divided into three categories (<2, 2-3, and >3), patients

with non-inaugural CAPS were more likely to have an INR $< 2$ (72% versus 28%, $P < 0.001$ ).

# DISCUSSION

In this cohort of 112 patients with at least one CAPS episode, we were able for the first time to describe thoroughly its precipitating factors, especially the role of anticoagulant treatment. At least one standard precipitating factor known from the literature was observed in $64\%$ of patients: infections, pregnancy, surgery, lupus flares, and malignancies. In addition, more than half of the CAPS episodes occurred in patients with known APS and treated with anticoagulants $(n = 61, 54\%)$ . Only 32 of these patients were treated with VKA, and their INR levels were most often subtherapeutic $(< 2)$ .

The profile of our patients with CAPS is similar to the most recent description of those in the international CAPS registry [7]: female (70% in our register vs 69% in the international registry), with a mean age of 43 years (vs 38) and triple positive for APS antibodies (58%, not available in the international registry). The catastrophic episodes inaugurated APS in 40% of our patients (vs 50% in the international registry). The affected organs were most often kidneys, the heart, and skin — again consistent with the literature.

The frequencies of the standard precipitating factors reported in our population were not markedly dissimilar to those of the international registry, with at least one standard precipitating factor found in $64\%$ vs $65\%$ in the registry [7]. These factors consisted in infections in $27\%$ (vs $49\%$ ), surgery in $15\%$ (vs $17\%$ ), and lupus flare in $7\%$ (vs $3\%$ ); inversely, malignancies were found in $2\%$ of our cases compared with $6\%$ and pregnancy in $22\%$ compared with $8\%$ [7]. The difference regarding infections may be related to our strict criterion for infections: we required laboratory proof. We note that CAPS episodes — due to

<!-- page 11 -->
the frequent presence of a systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) — may mimic infections [11]. The difference regarding pregnancy is probably due to the recruitment in our centres with expertise in pregnancy management of women with rare rheumatic diseases.

Besides these standard precipitating factors, our results suggest that the absence of optimal anticoagulant therapy may be a leading trigger of CAPS. Although CAPS frequently occurred de novo, it also complicated known APS treated with anticoagulants in more than half of the cases (n=61, 54%). Anticoagulants consisted in heparin (n=23), DOAC (n=2), and VKA in only 32 patients, at doses most often producing subtherapeutic levels of INR. Only 8 patients — 28% of those treated and 7% of the entire population — were receiving optimal treatment, that is, VKA with INR≥2, when their CAPS occurred. Comparing the 67 noninaugural CAPS patients to matched APS patients without CAPS showed that the patients with CAPS were less likely to be treated with VKA. Those treated with VKA were also more likely to have both lower INR (median INR 1.40 [1.10-2.80] vs 2.20 [1.90-2.80], $P = 0.001$ ) and subtherapeutic INR (72% vs 28%, $P < 0.001$ ). To our knowledge, this has not been studied previously. The only available information in the international CAPS registry dates from 2009; among the 280 patients then included, oral anticoagulation had been withdrawn or INR was low in 22, without additional information [12]. A case-control study comparing 14 patients with CAPS and 64 controls with APS without CAPS also reported a modification of the anticoagulant treatment for 3 patients before CAPS began [13]. This predominant role of anticoagulants is in keeping with our experience and with several case reports that have described CAPS onset just after cessation of antithrombotic therapy [14], in patients on bridging therapy (which replaces oral anticoagulation by parenteral anticoagulation before an invasive procedure) [15], and those who had recently switched to DOAC [2,8,16-18].

Arguably the modification of anticoagulants was closely interwoven with the standard CAPS risk factors. This makes it difficult to attribute a CAPS episode to one cause or the

<!-- page 12 -->
other, especially for pregnant patients on heparin. Nonetheless, the unusually low number of patients treated with VKA with an effective INR, especially compared with the rest of our APS/SLE cohort, suggests the specific effect of anticoagulant management. Additionally, both patients on DOAC included here had CAPS within a few days after its introduction and no other risk factors [8]. Other patients here had CAPS symptoms after heparin replaced VKA, but before the planned surgery and in the absence of any other trigger. This finding too implies an effect by anticoagulation management itself. To sum up, excluding pregnant patients for whom we could not differentiate the effect of treatment from that of pregnancy $(n = 14)$ , left 47 patients, 32 $(68\%)$ of whom had recently begun DOAC, planned bridging therapy, or had VKA treatment with INR $< 2$ . We thus believe that our results are compatible with the second (or third) hit theory [19]: suboptimal anticoagulation is one of the main hits.

Our results have very practical consequences for physicians managing patients with APS. Anticoagulation is the cornerstone of the prevention of recurrent thrombosis, and it is recommended to keep patients with thrombotic APS on VKA [2], given DOAC's lesser effectiveness [2,17,18,20]. Our results reinforce these recommendations: patients with APS must be educated about the importance of maintaining their VKA treatment and of keeping an INR no lower than 2 and preferably higher. Other important points include avoiding unnecessary surgery and avoiding switching to DOAC — associated with CAPS among APS patients previously stable under VKA [8,16]. When a switch to heparin is mandatory, especially for surgery, expert physicians should supervise it and institute close monitoring to recognise and treat early any manifestations of CAPS. In this setting, educating professionals is also important. In particular, physicians must consider the possible diagnosis of CAPS when new manifestations appear in patients stopping VKA treatment or with an ineffective INR [21].

Our study has some limitations, in particular, its retrospective nature. Despite our thorough

<!-- page 13 -->
examination of each file, retrospectively collected data are sometimes incomplete, and some INR values were unavailable. Second, the control group was matched for sex, age, centre and APS phenotype (arterial, venous or obstetric). Despite matching, patients with non-inaugural CAPS were more likely to be positive for ACL and/or LAC than non-CAPS APS patients. This suggests that these APS patients were probably not exactly the same as those who developed CAPS. Third, we compared the first available INR for the non-CAPS APS patients with the INR value at the onset of CAPS. As INR records in this registry differ between regular APS patients and patients with CAPS, this is a potential source of bias. Calculating time in therapeutic range would have been a better way to deal with INR fluctuation, but our registry did not collect serial INR values. As anti- $\beta 2$ GPI antibodies were first introduced in the Sydney classification criteria for APS published in 2006, but were not mentioned in the earlier Sapporo criteria, anti- $\beta 2$ GPI antibodies could have been missing for some patients before 2006 in both groups. Nevertheless, these patients were followed up in expert tertiary centres, which usually tested for these antibodies before 2006. If not, they were tested during the subsequent follow-up. Comparing non-CAPS APS patients and CAPS patients showed no difference in anti- $\beta 2$ GP1 antibody positivity between patients with CAPS episodes and those with non-CAPS APS diagnosed before 2006 (40% in both cases). Because of difficulties in obtaining histologic proof of small-vessel occlusion in emergency situations and as we usually did not perform biopsies when the diagnosis was considered certain, the histologic criterion was often missing in our patients: most patients were classified as probable and not definite CAPS. However, a strength of our study is that most of the patients and all of their files have been seen/reviewed by two senior physicians with substantial clinical experience with CAPS.

In conclusion, our results emphasise the importance of anticoagulation management in patients with APS and strongly suggest that CAPS is not governed by fate but is instead

<!-- page 14 -->
triggered by suboptimal VKA management and may thus sometimes be preventable. Education of both physicians and patients about the importance of maintaining VKA with an INR>2 appears important in this setting. Inability to do so should signal the need for additional prevention as well as for remaining alert to the possibility of an imminent CAPS episode.
