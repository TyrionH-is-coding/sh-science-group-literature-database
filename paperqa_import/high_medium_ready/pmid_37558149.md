# Assessing disease activity and damage in antiphospholipid syndrome

# ABSTRACT

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) has been characterized by a variety of vascular and pregnancy manifestations related to an interplay between thrombotic and inflammatory mechanisms, a progressive accrual of irreversible organ damage and increased morbidity and mortality rates, supporting a high need of optimal treatment approach. The lack of standardized outcome measures is a significant barrier in the design of clinical studies in APS. Disease activity (in principle reversible) and its distinction from disease damage (in principle irreversible) needs to be evaluated by validated scores for use in clinical trials but also in daily clinical practice in APS. A disease damage score in APS, the DIAPS score, has been developed and validated in external cohorts. The development of a disease activity score that will provide an accurate and reproducible rating of each disease domain, can help clinicians and researchers to comprehensively assess the activity of disease and the response to treatment, in an attempt to prevent future damage.

# 1. Introduction

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a complex and often devastating autoimmune disorder characterized by arterial and/or venous thrombotic manifestations and pregnancy morbidity in the presence of persistently positive antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL) [1,2]. The clinical presentation of APS is heterogeneous making challenging its diagnosis, disease severity assessment, and treatment. Accumulating evidence suggests that APS pathogenesis encompasses an inflammatory pattern in inter-relationship with classic thrombotic mechanisms. This novel concept involving both adaptive and innate immunity pathways is based on evidence showing that aPL can activate endothelial cells and monocytes leading to increased expression of vascular cell adhesion molecules, pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, type I interferon production, as well as complement, platelets and subsequent neutrophils activation, neutrophil extracellular traps release, and tissue factor expression promoting thrombus development and vascular injury [3-9]. Newly recognized pathophysiologic mechanisms may lead to the identification of new treatment targets [2,10], however, the

development of clinical trials in APS is strongly limited by the lack of validated outcome measures [11]. (See Fig. 1.)

# 2. Unmet need of outcome measures in APS

The recent 16th International Congress on Antiphospholipid Antibodies Task Force Report on Antiphospholipid Syndrome Treatment Trends concluded that "to enable the initiation and development of appropriate clinical studies, there needs to be a focus on devising suitable outcome measures, including disease activity index, disease damage index and APS-specific quality of life measures" [12]. A mere twenty years ago, consensus recommendations about endpoints in studies of rheumatic and musculoskeletal disorders (RMDs) suggested the measurement of five essential domains: disease activity, adverse effects of treatment, chronic damage, health-related quality of life, and economic impact of the disease [13]. Assessing disease activity in RMDs is crucial since disease activity predicts damage, and damage predicts mortality [14,15]. High disease activity is also associated with poor health-related quality of life and increased direct and indirect economic costs in

<!-- page 2 -->
rheumatic disorders [16,17]. Moreover, the prevention of organ damage is an overarching principle of the treat-to-target strategy in rheumatic diseases and a major therapeutic goal in clinical studies [18,19].

# 3. Disease activity and challenges of its assessment in APS

"Disease activity" is defined as aspects of the disease that are potentially reversible. This is differentiated by "disease damage," which refers to irreversible (implying permanent change) manifestations of the disease or its treatment. For example, it is important to distinguish an acute deep vein thrombosis (disease activity) in APS followed by thrombus resolution from a tissue loss due to peripheral vascular disease (damage), or a new-onset proteinuria and/or renal insufficiency that improves after treatment (activity) from end-stage renal disease (damage). Both disease activity and damage contribute to "disease severity".

Although the development of disease activity indices in systemic autoimmune disorders is difficult due to their clinical heterogeneity, a number of disease activity tools have been successfully developed and validated in complex disorders such as Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE), inflammatory myositis, systemic vasculitis, or Sjogren's syndrome [20]. The assessment of disease activity in APS is particularly challenging due to the multifaceted presentation of both thrombotic and/or obstetric manifestations, and several non-criteria features [21]. Thrombosis in APS can occur at any vascular bed including small, medium or large vessels, causing manifestations of various severity from almost every organ system. Obstetric APS also involves a broad spectrum of pregnancy complications ranging from early miscarriages to fetal death, preeclampsia or HELLP syndrome. Furthermore, a variety of non-thrombotic manifestations, the so-called non-criteria or extracriteria manifestations, are also very frequent in APS and seems to respond to immunoregulatory rather than anticoagulation treatments, including APS nephropathy, moderate/severe thrombocytopenia, hemolytic anemia, valve disease (mild to severe needing surgery), skin ulcers, alveolar hemorrhage, dementia, multiple-sclerosis like disease, or transverse myelitis [22,23].

Another challenge is that a disease activity or a disease damage score needs to include only features exclusively attributed to the disease itself, therefore, other potential causes or underlying disorders should be excluded [24]. Other causes for arterial and venous thrombotic events should be ruled out. The potential of a provoked thrombosis should be examined and patients should be asked/examined for the presence of venous thrombosis risk factors such as trauma, surgery, cancer, hospitalization or prolonged immobilization, and accordingly, for the presence of traditional cardiovascular risk factors. Cardiovascular disease is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in APS (1) and increasing evidence has shown an interplay between inflammation, thrombosis and atherogenesis in its pathogenesis [25,26]. A higher prevalence of traditional cardiovascular risk factors i.e. hypertension, current smoking, hyperlipidemia, and obesity [27-30] and a lower cardiovascular risk factor target attainment [31,32] has been demonstrated in APS compared to healthy controls and other high cardiovascular risk disorders such as Rheumatoid arthritis. Additionally, a 2.5 to 4-fold higher risk of subclinical atherosclerosis has been described in APS versus

healthy controls and patients with inflammatory arthritis or diabetes [33-35]. Furthermore, since SLE shares several common manifestations with APS such as thrombocytopenia, hemolytic anemia, valve disease, or alveolar hemorrhage [36], only patients with primary APS should be included in the development of APS outcome measures. It is also challenging how some APS manifestations should be categorized, since some are reversible (they can change over time) e.g. an acute-onset thrombocytopenia or alveolar hemorrhage, but others are irreversible (e.g. fetal loss). Questions are also raising about which laboratory markers can be included in an APS disease activity index. Although some laboratory tests, like acute moderate/severe thrombocytopenia and hemolytic anemia markers may indicate disease activity, there is uncertainty about others such as several inflammatory markers included in other rheumatic disease activity scores (e.g. CRP or ESR in Rheumatoid Arthritis) (DAS28) [37], or changes in autoantibody levels (e.g. anti-dsDNA or complement levels in SLE) [38]. For example, whether an increase in aPL titres or positivity for a new aPL type (e.g. anti- $\beta_{2}$ -glycoprotein I) or isotype (e.g. IgG isotype) not previously detected, should be considered as serologic disease activity in APS.

# 3.1. Development and validation of a disease activity index in APS

The development of a disease activity index in APS, as it was stressed by the Task Force Report on Antiphospholipid Syndrome Treatment Trends at the 16th International congress on aPL [12], would help clinicians to detect and rate disease activity and its changes over time, and facilitate the assessment of the efficacy of treatments in clinical trials. A validated score measuring disease activity could also reduce the heterogeneity between studies allowing comparisons about the efficacy of examined medications. The methodology of outcome measure development in systemic autoimmune disorders includes established group techniques and index development methods e.g. item generation and reduction, item weighting and scaling, refinement and validation [20,41]. The goal of the first phases is to develop a list of all possible items for inclusion but minimize redundancy. Consensus methods such as Delphi surveys and nominal group techniques have been commonly used in item generation and reduction phases of outcome tools development in rheumatology. The Delphi technique is a multistage method using anonymous responses in a series of questionnaires to determine consensus from a large group of participants [42]. Evaluation of face and content validity is essential to ensure that items are reasonable and cover all aspects of the disease [41]. Feasibility should also be assessed, and consideration should be given to ease of use. Once the candidate sets of domains are determined, they should be applied to a large and diverse group of patients. Assessment of sensitivity and specificity follows to evaluate which variables best discriminate "active" from "non-active" disease. As a final step, measures should be applied to an independent sample, for external validation [43].

The American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/EULAR Classification Criteria for APS have just been approved [44]. Some of the non-criteria APS manifestations, like thrombocytopenia, APS nephropathy, valvopathy, livedo racemosa, will be considered as part of the new criteria and this might help more precocious disease diagnosis.

Twenty percent of those with organ damage were unable to perform everyday activities impacting their quality of life because they were functionally impaired [45].

# 4.1. Development of a damage index

A first attempt for the development of a disease Damage Index in APS (DIAPS) was made by a group of APS experts in Mexico. An expert panel was formed to classify all APS related damage items; 37 items from 10 organs were selected and ranked by the panel, 22 of which were derived from the Systemic Collaborating Clinics / American College of Rheumatology (SLICC/ACR) Damage Index (SDI) [39]. In Phase I, epidemiologists and clinicians defined metrics (indices or scales) that could be used to quantify organ damage in these patients. Initially, experts identified 60 manifestations that could be related to organ damage in APS. Further validation refinements by Delphi exercise excluded low specificity items. Damage was characterized if the manifestations were present for at least 6 months, or caused permanent losses, like hearing loss and hemiplegia. Phase II was a multicentric cross-sectional validation of DIAPS in Latin America with further comparison to the EuroQol, a generic quality of life index, used as gold standard criterion validity [46]. The DIAPS had a high internal consistency ( $\alpha$ Cronbach 0.619) with a significant correlation with EuroQol for mobility, selfcare, usual activities, pain, anxiety and depression ( $P > 0.05$ ). One of its limitations was the exclusion of important APS-related manifestations such as livedo reticularis/racemosa, multiple sclerosis-like disease, diffuse pulmonary hemorrhage, and adrenal insufficiency. The authors also recognized that validation of DIAPS in other cohorts outside Mexico was necessary since most patients were Latin American Mestizos [39].

DIAPS was first compared to SLICC Damage Index (SDI) in a cross-sectional analysis of data from 50 patients with primary APS (PAPS), 50 lupus patients and 50 secondary APS patients in Brazil. Both indexes were highly correlated each other in all subgroups of patients (PAPS: $r = 0.9$ $P < 0.0001$ , APS + SLE: $r = 0.7$ $p < 0.0001$ and SLE: $r = 0.8$ $p < 0.0001$ ) [47]. Considering that DIAPS included APS specific disease manifestations, the next study utilized DIAPS instead of SDI, for the comparison of damage between primary and secondary APS patients in a retrospective cohort from Brazil, longitudinally evaluated for 10 years. The pattern of damage was different between APS subgroups. Patients with PAPS presented a higher damage score early in the disease course $(1.72 \pm 1.17$ vs. $0.82 \pm 0.96$ $p < 0.001$ ), while damage in the SLE-APS subgroup increased significantly over time $(0.43 \pm 0.30$ vs. $1.22 \pm 1.24$ $p < 0.001$ ), probably due to cumulative steroid treatment effect [40]. SDI has been first used to assess cumulative damage in a Greek population of primary and secondary APS patients. In their cohort, SDI increased over time in patients with arterial and venous events, but not in patients with exclusive pregnancy morbidity events $(p < 0.05$ , at all timepoints 0, 1, 2, 3, 5, and 7 years). In the multivariate analysis, diagnosis of CAPS and SLE at baseline were important predictors of future damage $(\beta$ coefficient $= 3.42$ $p = 0.01$ $\beta$ coefficient $= 1.11$ $p = 0.01$ , respectively). Moreover, SDI was associated with increased mortality (HR 1.31, $95\%$ CI 1.07-1.60, $p = 0.01$ , per 1-unit increase) [48].

# 4.2. Validation of DIAPS outside Latin America

The next validation effort of DIAPS was performed outside Latin America, in the APS ACTION cohort including 826 aPL-positive patients:240 SLE patients with positive aPL, (177 with SLE-APS), 10 patients with other diseases than SLE, and 576 aPL $+$ APS with no underlying systemic autoimmune diseases (412 with thrombotic PAPS and 164 with non-thrombotic disease [108 aPL only and 56 obstetric]). In the thrombotic group, hyperlipidemia (OR 1.75, $95\%$ CI 1.02-2.98, adjusted $p = 0.41$ ), obesity (OR 2.05, $95\%$ CI 1.19-3.52, adjusted $p = 0.009$ ) and $\alpha_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ high titers (OR 2.33 $95\%$ CI 1.19-3.52, adjusted $p = 0.009$ ) were independently associated with high damage at baseline. Hypertension (OR 4.55, $95\%$ CI 1.82-11.35, adjusted $p = 0.001$ ) and hyperlipidemia (OR 4.32, $95\%$ CI 1.37-13.65, adjusted $p = 0.013$ ) were independent predictors of damage in asymptomatic aPL carriers. Single aPL positivity was inversely correlated with damage (OR 0.24, $95\%$ CI 0.075-0.77, adjusted $p = 0.016$ ). In the thrombotic group, the most frequent domains contributing to damage were the peripheral vascular ( $n = 260$ , $63\%$ - mainly deep vein thrombosis), neuropsychiatric ( $n = 107$ , $30\%$ - mainly ischemic stroke with sequelae) and cardiovascular domain ( $n = 57$ , $14\%$ - mainly heart valve disease) [49,50]. For the nonthrombotic group, the most frequent domains contributing to damage were the neuropsychiatric ( $n = 22$ , $12.6\%$ - mainly cognitive impairment) and cardiovascular ( $n = 13$ , $7.5\%$ - mainly heart valve disease).

This was the first study that evaluated damage in a multiethnic APS population. Traditional cardiovascular risk factors were higher in both groups of patients and were also associated with a higher damage. The observed differences in damage between the thrombotic and nonthrombotic populations may be related to other factors that are not regularly taken into account, as the genetic background, presence of non-traditional CVD risk factors, socioeconomic status, access to medical care, and the time on therapeutic INR range for patients receiving vitamin K antagonists.

In a recently published single- center retrospective study of thrombotic APS patients showed that patients have presented damage already at six months after disease onset, defined as early damage. A one-point increase in DIAPS, entitled Delta DIAPS, in the first five years after disease onset, predicted mortality (HR 5.40, CI $95\%$ 2.33-12.52, $p < 0.001$ ), even after adjusting for APS category (PAPS vs SLE-APS), sex, age at APS onset ( $\geq 40$ years) and early damage (all $p \leq 0.005$ ). Arterial thrombosis was independently associated with early damage (OR 7.24, CI $95\%$ 3.74-14.03, $p < 0.001$ ) but not with damage increase, arbitrarily defined as, increase of at least one point, from the initial DIAPS score (OR 1.22, CI $95\%$ 0.64-2.33, $p = 0.539$ ) [51].

Over the past years, researchers have agreed upon that DIAPS needs to be improved, pointing out aspects for future considerations e.g. patients who do suffer from $>3$ thrombotic events are not stratified by severity, and also, not all APS-related manifestations neither bleeding-related events, and risk stratification based on aPL profile (LA presence, triple vs non-triple aPL positivity, IgG vs IgM or IgA isotype) have been considered by the score. Gaspar et all have shown that almost $20\%$ of patients from their cohort with bleeding events, presented a permanent disability from the central nervous system representing $8.3\%$ of all bleeding events [meningeal $(2.4\%)$ parenchymal / medullary $(4.1\%)$ , unknown $(1.8\%)]$ and from the genitourinary system: $[2.4\%$ of all events: menorrhagia] [52]. In another study, Gaspar et all also emphasized that not all neurologic events-related damage is not reported by DIAPS. In a single center retrospective study that evaluated all acute thrombotic neurologic events from 197 patients followed for 43 years (median 10 years), most events were due to motor deficit secondary to cortical strokes, well represented in the actual DIAPS but 35/197 $(17.8\%)$ patients, developed 29 damage events $(42.6\%$ of damage) not recorded by DIAPS including mostly sensory deficits, followed by cerebellar stroke and cerebral venous thrombosis [53].

In addition, data from other previous cohorts have also shown that patients with high-risk aPL profile are more prone to have worse

prognosis and higher damage. The cumulative incidence of thrombosis over a 10-year follow-up was $44.2\%$ (CI $95\%$ 38.6-49.8) in triple aPL positive patients with inadequate anticoagulation treatment [54]. In the multicentric, prospective PROMISSE (Predictors of Pregnancy Outcome: Biomarkers in Antiphospholipid Antibody Syndrome and Systemic Lupus Erythematosus) study, that reported outcomes from 144 pregnant women with SLE and APS, LA has been the sole serologic factor associated with poor pregnancy outcomes and thrombosis $(39\% \mathrm{LA}+$ vs $3\%$ LA-, $P < 0.00010$ [55].

# 5. The next steps to improve damage assessment

APS disease understanding has improved in the last years but we still need to improve disease recognition and management to minimize damage and improve quality of life in the long term.

Probably the best way to assess damage in these patients is to define a composite score that combines serology, number and type of thrombosis, other organ damage from concomitant chronic diseases not related to APS, bleeding, as well as the presence of traditional cardiovascular risk factors, such as age, smoking, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and body mass index.

A group of researchers from APS ACTION are actively involved in improving DIAPS. All items that were not considered in the previous score should be added and each variable should gain a proper weight relative to the risk that it may bring to accrual damage. Another set of cross-sectional and prospective validations in different APS cohorts should follow the re-design of the damage index for proper assessment.

# 6. Conclusions

After almost 50 years from the first description of APS, there are still important unmet needs in APS, including the lack of validated outcome measures, that should be addressed for a proper disease treatment. It is time to define new metrics for APS that will help to design clinical studies with new treatments and might allow physicians to use them in the daily practice to improve patient outcomes.
