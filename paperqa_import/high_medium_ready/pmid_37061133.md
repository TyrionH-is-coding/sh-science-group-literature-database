# Abstract

Background: The added value of antiphosphatidylserine/prothrombin antibodies (aPS/ PT) in the diagnostic workup of antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is unclear. Currently, diagnosis of thrombotic APS (TAPS) and obstetric APS (OAPS) requires persistent presence of lupus anticoagulant (LAC), anticardiolipin (aCL) immunoglobulin (Ig) G/IgM, or anti- $\beta 2$ -glycoprotein I (aβ2GPI) IgG/IgM antibodies.

Objectives: To evaluate the role of aPS/PT IgG and IgM in OAPS.

Methods: aPS/PT IgG/IgM, aCL IgG/IgM, aβ2GPI IgG/IgM, and LAC were determined in 653 patients (OAPS, TAPS, and controls). In-house aPS/PT cut-off values were calculated, titers and prevalence were compared between OAPS, TAPS, and controls and type of pregnancy morbidity. Sensitivity, specificity, likelihood ratios, and odds ratios (OR) with 95% CI were calculated.

Results: In OAPS, aPS/PT IgG and IgM showed an OR of 4.32 (95% CI, 2.54-7.36) and 3.37 (95% CI, 1.93-5.89), respectively, but the association was not independent of LAC. Prevalence and titers of aPS/PT IgG and IgM were lower in OAPS than in patients with TAPS. aPS/PT were more prevalent and showed higher titers in patients with late pregnancy loss than in patients with early pregnancy loss with a positivity of $86.4\%$ and $39.3\%$ , respectively. Higher aPS/PT titers did not increase the likelihood of having OAPS.

Conclusion: The added value of aPS/PT testing in the current diagnostic workup of OAPS seems limited compared with LAC, aCL, and aβ2GPI. aPS/PT might be useful in specific subsets of patients with OAPS. However, future multicentric studies are needed to elucidate the risk of less frequent and most severe obstetrical manifestations.

# 1 | INTRODUCTION

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is characterized by a variety of clinical events, mainly thrombosis (thrombotic APS [TAPS]) and/or pregnancy complications (obstetric APS [OAPS]), and the presence of persistent antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL) [1]. According to the current classification criteria, clinical OAPS is suspected in patients with 1 or more unexplained fetal deaths at or beyond the 10th gestational week, 3 or more miscarriages before the 10th week of gestation, or at least 1 premature birth of a morphologically normal neonate before the 34th gestational week because of (pre) eclampsia [2]. Obstetrical morbidity, such as pregnancy loss, is prevalent in the general population with various underlying causes and associated risk factors [3]. Correct identification of women with obstetrical morbidity as patients with OAPS is essential due to the need for specific therapeutic management, including low-dose aspirin (LDA) and heparin during pregnancy [4]. Therefore, adequate laboratory assessment of patients with suspected OAPS is vital. Currently, the presence of lupus anticoagulant (LAC), anticardiolipin antibodies (aCL) immunoglobulin (Ig) G and/or IgM, and/ or anti- $\beta_{2}$ -glycoprotein I antibodies (a $\beta$ 2GPI) IgG and/or IgM over at least 12 weeks are considered as positive laboratory criteria for classification or diagnosis of OAPS [2,5].

LAC is determined with functional coagulation assays, whereas the presence of aCL and aβ2GPI IgG/IgM is assessed with solid-phase immunoassays [6,7]. Assessing LAC is a technically complex process that is further complicated by potential interference from factors such as anticoagulant therapy and acute phase proteins, resulting in a difficult interpretation of the results [8-10]. In contrast, solid-phase assays are easier to perform and are less prone to interference, although they suffer from a lack of standardization [11]. In addition to aCL and aβ2GPI, antiphosphatidylserine/prothrombin antibodies (aPS/PT) are other aPL measured with solid-phase assays. aPS/PT have been investigated for their potential use as a surrogate marker for LAC and for their value in APS diagnosis and risk stratification, especially in patients with TAPS [12-16]. Although aPS/PT were not included in the current or upcoming new classification criteria for APS, there is ongoing debate about whether and how they should be incorporated into the diagnosis of APS [17-19].

We have recently demonstrated in a case-control study that the added value of aPS/PT compared with the currently used aPL is limited in the workup of patients with TAPS [15]. In this study, we investigated whether aPS/PT could improve the diagnostic assessment of patients with OAPS.

# Essentials

- Antiphosphatidylserine/prothrombin antibodies (aPS/PT) were investigated in obstetric antiphospholipid syndrome (OAPS).   
- Prevalence and titers of aPS/PT are lower in OAPS than in thrombotic APS.   
- aPS/PT prevalence and titers are higher in late pregnancy loss than in early pregnancy loss.   
- Added value of aPS/PT testing in OAPS seems limited compared with current diagnostic strategies.

# 2 MATERIALS AND METHODS

# 2.1 | Patient and control population

A retrospective case-control study with 653 women, including patients with OAPS, patients with TAPS, and controls, was performed. Samples were collected in 7 European medical centers, and patients were classified as APS or control by experts at each center, based on the Sydney criteria, as described before [20]. Patients with OAPS were excluded if there was a known history of thrombosis in order to retain a "pure" OAPS population. Control patients were divided into 4 categories: patients with an autoimmune disease (AID) without a history of thrombotic or obstetrical morbidity ("AID controls [AIDCs]"), patients who were referred for aPL testing without having clinical APS criteria ("hospital controls [HCs]"), patients who had a history of uncomplicated pregnancies ("pregnancy controls [PCs]"), and patients who experienced at least 1 complicated pregnancy either fulfilling clinical OAPS criteria, but negative for aPL or not fulfilling the Sydney clinical criteria for OAPS ("obstetric disease controls [ODCs]"). Pregnancy morbidity was registered as early pregnancy loss ( $\geq$ 3 miscarriages $<$ 10th week of gestation), late pregnancy loss ( $\geq$ 1 unexplained fetal death $\geq$ 10th week of gestation), or prematurity (<34th week of gestation due to preeclampsia or placental insufficiency) according to the Sydney criteria [2].

# 2.2 Laboratory assays

All samples were analyzed for aCL IgG/IgM and aβ2GPI IgG/IgM with the ACL AcuStar chemiluminescent immunoassay (Werfen), and aPS/PT IgG/IgM with the QUANTA Lite enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Werfen) and BEP III System plate reader (Siemens

<!-- page 3 -->
Healthcare). aCL and aβ2GPI IgG/IgM titers are expressed in U/mL and aPS/PT IgG/IgM titers in units. Manufacturers' cut-off values were 20 U/mL for aCL IgG/IgM, aβ2GPI IgG/IgM, and 30 units for aPS/PT IgG/IgM. The manufacturers' cut-off values were applied after verification in 20 healthy volunteers, according to the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) guidelines [6,21]. Furthermore, in-house aPS/PT cut-off values were calculated based on samples from 127 healthy female volunteers according to the 99th percentile method after outlier rejection by visual inspection of the box-and-whisker plot and Reed/Dixon test [6,21]. In-house cut-off values were 30 units for aPS/PT IgG and 42 units for aPS/PT IgM (Supplementary Figure S1 and Table S1). Calibrator, control, and patient samples were tested in duplicate with ELISA. Samples with aPL results above the upper limit of quantification were diluted manually (QUANTA Lite) or automatically (ACL AcuStar) according to the manufacturers' instructions. Samples with aPS/PT titers $>7000$ units were not further diluted. LAC was measured in the local participating centers according to the ISTH guidelines [7,22]. LAC positivity was defined as persistent positive LAC testing over $\geq 12$ weeks. aCL and aβ2GPI were remeasured at the coordinating laboratory (Ghent University Hospital) to obtain results for 1 analytical system (ACL AcuStar). Patients originally classified as APS based on local measurement of aCL/aβ2GPI but with negative results for aCL, or aβ2GPI by retesting in the central laboratory were not included in the study. aPS/PT were not measured by the local laboratories and was tested for this study only, without repeat measurement at a second timepoint. We determined the antibody profile for each subject and classified them as single positive with only one of the three criteria aPL being positive (LAC+, aCL+, or aβ2GPI+), double positive (LAC-, aCL+, and aβ2GPI+), or triple positive (LAC+, aCL+, and aβ2GPI+). We also specified the aPL profiles by their Ig isotypes: IgG triple-positive (LAC+, aCL IgG+, and aβ2GPI IgG+), IgM triple-positive (LAC+, aCL IgM+, and aβ2GPI IgM+), IgG double-positive (LAC-, aCL IgG+, and aβ2GPI IgG+), and IgM double-positive (LAC-, aCL IgM+, and aβ2GPI IgM+).

# 2.3 Statistical analysis

The Kruskal Wallis test followed by Mann-Whitney U-testing with Bonferroni correction was applied for comparison of continuous data. Association of categorical data between 2 groups was evaluated using the Fisher exact test. A significance level of 0.05 was considered for statistical evaluation. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was used to determine odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding $95\%$ CI adjusted for age and aPL test results. Furthermore, logistic regression models, including LAC, aCL, and $\alpha \beta 2GPI$ were compared with and without adding aPS/PT results. All regression analyses were performed to discriminate between the OAPS and control groups. Sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative likelihood ratios (LR+ and LR-), and interval-specific LR (IS-LR) with $95\%$ CI for OAPS were determined. IS-LRs were calculated to identify a high-titer threshold, which was defined as a titer with a corresponding IS-LR of 10 or

higher as previously described [15,23,24]. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics 28 (IBM) and MedCalc v15.6.1 (MedCalc Software). Visualization through Venn diagrams was performed with software available from https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/ (consulted on November 28, 2022).

# 3 | RESULTS

# 3.1 Patient characteristics

The studied patient and control population was initially described elsewhere [20]. We derived a subset of female patients for sex-matching purposes. aPS/PT have been investigated in a part of this population already, focusing solely on TAPS [15]. Patients with TAPS were included in this study to compare aPS/PT titers and positivity with patients with OAPS. Overall, 653 samples were analyzed and categorized as being from 84 patients with OAPS, 143 patients with TAPS, 168 HC, 137 AIDC, 98 PC, and 23 ODC. Clinical characteristics for HC, AIDC, and ODC are included in Supplementary Table S2. Patients with OAPS were younger than patients with TAPS and controls in the AIDC and HC groups $(p < .05)$ , and no significant difference in age distribution was observed between the OAPS, PC, and ODC groups. Patient characteristics and laboratory results are shown in Table 1. Early pregnancy loss, late pregnancy loss, and prematurity occurred in $67.9\%$ , $27.4\%$ , and $6.0\%$ of OAPS and $34.8\%$ , $39.1\%$ , and $13.0\%$ of ODC cases, respectively. Pregnancy morbidity other than described in the clinical OAPS criteria occurred in 5 $(21.7\%)$ of 23 of ODC cases. One ODC case with an obstetrical history of 1 early pregnancy loss and preeclampsia showed aCL IgM positivity $(24.3\mathrm{U}/\mathrm{mL})$ at measurement in the context of this study and was negative for other aPL.

# 3.5 Logistic regression analyses

Age-adjusted ORs were calculated for aPS/PT with and without adjustment for other aPL, and for antibody profiles with and without adding aPS/PT (Table 4). There was a significant association between OAPS and presence of aPS/PT IgG (OR, 4.32; 95% CI, 2.54-7.36), aPS/PT IgM (OR, 3.37; 95% CI, 1.93-5.89), and aPS/PT IgG and/or IgM (OR, 4.22; 95% CI, 2.54-7.00). The association did not remain significant when adjusting for LAC, meaning that no added value could be demonstrated for aPS/PT to diagnose OAPS compared with LAC. ORs for aPS/PT IgG and aPS/PT IgG and/or IgM remained significantly higher than 1 when adjusted for aCL IgG, aCL IgM, $\alpha\beta$ 2GPI IgG, or $\alpha\beta$ 2GPI IgM. Conversely, the association of aPS/PT IgM with OAPS remained significant when adjusting for IgM aCL and $\alpha\beta$ 2GPI, but not for IgG aCL and $\alpha\beta$ 2GPI. Moreover, the association remained significant only for aPS/PT IgG if adjusted for the combination of all solid-phase aPL as 1 diagnostic marker (aCL + $\alpha\beta$ 2GPI). This was also confirmed in an additional multivariable regression analysis considering the solid-phase aPL separately (Supplementary Table S4). Adding aPS/PT did not improve the association (ORs) with OAPS for single

LAC positivity or triple positivity, and aPS/PT positivity even excluded OAPS when the IgM double-positive aPL profile was observed.

# 4 | DISCUSSION

The presence of persistent circulating aPL is associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes. A recent Cochrane review showed that administration of heparin in combination with LDA during pregnancy might improve live birth rate in women with persistent aPL [25]. Furthermore, treatment with preconceptional LDA and prophylactic low-molecular weight heparin during pregnancy showed beneficial results on the live birth rate in patients with OAPS [26]. Currently, the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology, formerly the European League Against Rheumatism, recommends considering LDA for primary thromboprophylaxis in asymptomatic women with a high-risk aPL profile. Furthermore, in women with an OAPS history, LDA with or without prophylactic heparin treatment is recommended during pregnancy, and primary thromboprophylaxis with LDA might be considered [4]. aPS/PT IgG and IgM were measured with ELISA, of which the manufacturer's cut-off values were verified according to the CLSI EP28 and ISTH guidelines [6,21]. However, as recommended by the manufacturer, in-house cut-off values were calculated, including 127 healthy female volunteers based on a nonparametric 99th percentile approach. The in-house cut-off value for aPS/PT IgG was identical to the manufacturer's cutoff value (30 units), whereas the aPS/PT IgM in-house cut-off value was higher (42 units compared with 30 units). Sex-specific in-house cut-off values were determined in this study, although they were comparable with those based on an overall healthy population. Cut-offs had previously been calculated on the basis of a healthy volunteer population including women $(n = 127)$ and men $(n = 73)$ with comparable cut-off values of 28 and 43 units for aPS/PT IgG and IgM, respectively [15]. Selecting optimal cut-off values is a balance exercise between optimal sensitivity and specificity and is dependent on the anticipated use of a biomarker. A higher specificity might be desired as reduced sensitivity is acceptable because multiple aPLs are tested in the diagnostic evaluation of APS [20]. Therefore, we applied the in-house cut-off value for aPS/PT IgM, increasing the specificity for OAPS from $79.1\%$ to $85.2\%$ .

Studies have shown that aPS/PT IgG and IgM are associated with LAC and may be involved in prothrombotic processes [32-35]. Some groups have suggested the potential use of aPS/PT as a surrogate marker for LAC [13,34], but this is countered by the incomplete overlap of LAC and aPS/PT [14,15,36]. Replacing LAC by a marker determined by solid-phase assays such aPS/PT could improve the diagnostic process for APS as they are easier to perform and not prone to interference by anticoagulant therapy and acute phase proteins contrary to LAC assessment [11]. A recent meta-analysis reported a pooled aPS/PT prevalence of $84.3\%$ (95% CI, 73.3%-93.2%) in patients with LAC-positive APS, although these studies included only a minority of patients with APS with obstetrical complications or focused on TAPS only [14,37-41]. In a recent retrospective case-control study, only $15\%$ (25 of 170) of persistent LAC-positive patients with OAPS were aPS/PT positive, but this population might not be representative as 159 of 170 patients were single LAC-positive (aCL and aβ2GPI negative) [42]. In our study, aPS/PT positivity was observed in 36 (60%) of 60 LAC-positive patients with OAPS. In the single LAC-positive subpopulation (22 of 60, 37%), 10 (45%) patients were aPS/PT positive. These findings confirm that replacing LAC by aPS/PT on a one-by-one basis in the diagnostic evaluation of OAPS is not feasible.

with TAPS and OAPS, although the results are not consistent [43,44]. Evidence is increasing that different pathophysiological mechanisms are responsible for the obstetrical and thrombotic complications in APS [27,45,46], although the thrombogenic properties of aPL are a main concern in TAPS, obstetrical complications seem to depend more on a wide set of proinflammatory properties of aPL. Therefore, it might be interesting to investigate patients with OAPS and TAPS separately when studying noncriteria aPL, such as aPS/PT. In a retrospective cohort study, the prevalence and titers of aPS/PT IgG and IgM were found to be lower in primary OAPS patients without a thrombotic history (prevalence of $7.1\%$ and $25\%$ , respectively) than that in primary TAPS patients without a history of pregnancy complications ( $33\%$ and $56\%$ , respectively) and APS patients with a history of both obstetrical and thrombotic complications ( $60\%$ and $63\%$ , respectively) [47]. However, the prevalence of LAC positivity was also lower in patients with OAPS ( $8.9\%$ ) than that in the other 2 patient groups with thrombosis ( $90\%$ and $74\%$ , respectively) [47]. In another retrospective case-control study, Amengual et al. [12] included 51 women with OAPS without a thrombotic history and showed an aPS/PT IgG prevalence of 12 ( $41\%$ ) of 29 and 18 ( $82\%$ ) of 22 in patients without and with associated AID, respectively. In contrast, Shi et al. [48] showed a lower titer for aPS/PT IgM in patients with TAPS than that in patients with OAPS without thrombosis, but the prevalence was not reported. In general, aPS/PT prevalence seems to be higher in patients with TAPS than that in patients with OAPS, although this might be dependent on the presence of LAC, associated AID, or concomitant thrombotic history in OAPS.

In 2020, Radin et al. [49] published the results of a systematic review and meta-analysis in which they demonstrated that there was a significant association between pregnancy morbidity and aPS/PT IgG (mean OR, 6.7; 95% CI, 3.04-21.6) and aPS/PT IgG and/or IgM positivity (mean OR, 10.6; 95% CI, 3.54-35.4). However, a significant association with aPS/PT IgM could not be demonstrated [49]. The included studies had a very heterogeneous design with different patient groups (pregnancy morbidity according to clinical OAPS criteria [50,51], OAPS [12,37,48,52], or preeclampsia [53]) and control groups (AID cases [12,50], healthy volunteers [51], cases with a history of complicated [52] or uncomplicated pregnancies [53], or a combination [37,48]). In our study, we calculated ORs for aPS/PT, including a

patient group of OAPS cases without history of thrombotic events and a control group consisting of AIDC, HC, PC, and ODC. We observed similar ORs for aPS/PT IgG (4.32; 95% CI, 2.54-7.36) and aPS/PT IgG and/or IgM (4.22; 95% CI, 2.54-7.00), but also a significant association for aPS/PT IgM (OR, 3.37; 95% CI, 1.93-5.89). aPS/PT IgG remained significantly associated with OAPS when adjusting for the combined criteria solid-phase aPL (aCL and aβ2GPI) in a multivariable logistic regression analysis contrary to aPS/PT IgM. The association of aPS/PT was not independent from LAC. This suggests that adding aPS/PT to the diagnostic criteria for the first-line evaluation of OAPS is not helpful because aPS/PT is inferior to LAC. In our study, the "seronegative" OAPS group was small: 78% of the ODC (18 of 23) fulfilled the clinical criteria for OAPS without the presence of criteria aPL. In this group, 3 patients (16.7%) showed positivity for aPS/PT (=single aPS/PT). Another study demonstrated that seronegative APS patients with the presence of at least 1 of 8 noncriteria aPL (including aPS/PT IgG and IgM) had a decreased cumulative incidence of adverse pregnancy outcomes when treated with LDA with or without low-molecular weight heparin compared with no treatment, although prospective controlled interventional studies are needed to further confirm this association [54].

Recently, we showed that higher titers of aPS/PT IgG and IgM were associated with a higher likelihood of having TAPS, and we identified a high-titer threshold of 90 and 200 units for aPS/PT IgG and IgM, respectively [15]. In contrast, a clear association between higher titer and increased likelihood could not be demonstrated in patients with OAPS. Furthermore, we observed a lower median titer for aPS/PT IgG and IgM in patients with OAPS than that in patients with TAPS. A higher aPS/PT IgG titer distribution $(p = .046)$ was observed in patients with OAPS compared with AIDC cases, but not for aPS/PT IgM. LRs for OAPS did not increase with higher aPS/PT IgG and IgM titers, mostly due to the presence of patients with high aPS/PT titers in the AIDC group. Therefore, identifying a high-titer threshold in the workup of OAPS seems less appropriate. However, prospective cohort studies are warranted to investigate the clinical course of patients with AID and high values of aPS/PT.

Titers of aPS/PT IgG and IgM were higher in patients with OAPS with late pregnancy loss than that in patients with early pregnancy loss, in agreement with the study by Hoxha et al. [47]. The same

<!-- page 12 -->
pattern was observed for IgG aCL and $\alpha 2\mathrm{GP}$ , but not for IgM. aPS/PT IgG and IgM, aCL IgG, and a2GPI IgG were more prevalent in patients with late pregnancy loss than those with early pregnancy loss, unlike IgM aCL and a2GPI, and LAC. These findings might implicate that aPS/PT is a more relevant biomarker in patients who experienced late pregnancy loss. Insufficient patients with OAPS with prematurity were included in this study for evaluation of this clinical subgroup.

Twenty-five patients with OAPS had a triple-positive aPL profile $(\mathrm{LAC}+, \mathrm{aCL}+, \mathrm{and} \mathrm{a}\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}+)$ . Of these, 24 patients were also aPS/PT positive, confirming the high association of aPS/PT with triple positivity [15]. Evaluating aPL profiles, it has been suggested to add aPS/PT as a biomarker for double-positive cases when LAC measurement is unreliable during anticoagulant treatment [55] or when LAC might be false negative in direct oral anticoagulant [56-58] or vitamin-K antagonist treatment [59]. Seventeen patients with OAPS had a double-positive aPL profile $(\mathrm{LAC}-, \mathrm{aCL}+, \mathrm{and} \mathrm{a}\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}+)$ with 8 of 17 IgG double-positive, 7 of 17 IgM double-positive, and 2 of 17 combined IgG and IgM double-positive. Overall, 7 of 8 IgG double positives were also aPS/PT positive, but none of the IgM and IgG+IgM double positives (0 of 9) were aPS/PT positive. Although the number of double-positive aPL profiles in this study was limited, these findings suggest that aPS/PT could be useful for consolidation of the high-risk IgG double-positive profile, but not when IgM aCL and aβ2GPI are positive. In our study, a double-positive aPL profile indicated a high-risk profile irrespective of the isotype of aCL/aβ2GPI or aPS/PT positivity.

This study investigated a single aPS/PT assay, which limits its generalizability as aPS/PT testing is not standardized or harmonized. For instance, a study compared aPS/PT IgG testing with 2 commercial ELISAs reporting a moderate-to-substantial agreement in 2 study populations with Cohen's $\kappa$ values of 0.60 and 0.63 [12]. The lack of standardization additionally hampers the integration of aPS/PT testing in guidelines or diagnostic criteria.

In conclusion, aPS/PT IgG and IgM were associated with OAPS, but had no added value in OAPS diagnosis compared with LAC, and aPS/PT cannot be used as a surrogate marker for LAC in OAPS evaluation. The prevalence and titers of aPS/PT IgG and IgM were lower in patients with OAPS than those in patients with TAPS. Furthermore, higher aPS/PT values did not increase the likelihood of having OAPS contrary to this finding in TAPS. Within OAPS, higher aPS/PT positivity was observed in patients with late pregnancy loss compared with those with early pregnancy loss, implicating that aPS/PT might be useful as a biomarker in specific patient populations with pregnancy complications. We can summarize that it is currently not recommended to include routine aPS/PT testing in the diagnostic evaluation of OAPS without the presence of a thrombotic history as the added value to the laboratory criteria seems limited. However, after this exploration of the clinical value of aPL considering the current OAPS clinical criteria, further studies with focus on patient populations exhibiting the less frequent and most severe obstetrical manifestations are needed. These patient populations include those with early preeclampsia before the 34th gestational week classified according to maternal and fetal complications,

placental abruption, severe fetal growth restriction, near-term fetal deaths, and perinatal mortality, newborn with neurologic sequelae due to severe prematurity or growth restriction, and OAPS resistant to first-line antithrombotic therapy. These studies, preferably based on multicentric recruitment, should elucidate the risk of aPS/PT for the most devastating obstetrical complications.
