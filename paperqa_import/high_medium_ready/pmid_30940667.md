CASE REPORT

# Comparing APLS and oranges: caution with the use of direct-acting oral anticoagulants (DOACs) instead of warfarin in the treatment of antiphospholipid syndrome (APLS)

Matthew Benger, Jasper Vink, Luke Blagdon Snell, Karen Breen

Guy's and Saint Thomas' NHS Foundation Trust, London, UK

Correspondence to Dr Luke Blagdon Snell, luke-snell@nhs.net

Accepted 19 February 2019

# SUMMARY

A 63-year-old woman with known antiphospholipid syndrome (APLS) presented with catastrophic APLS and multiorgan dysfunction after a change in her anticoagulation from warfarin to rivaroxaban. Evidence suggests direct-acting oral anticoagulants (DOACs) like rivaroxaban may be less effective than warfarin in secondary prevention of thrombotic events in high-risk APLS patients.

# BACKGROUND

A new generation of direct-acting oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have revolutionised the management of venous thromboembolism (VTE). These medications, such as rivaroxaban, work via direct inhibition of factor Xa. They have predictable anticoagulation effects, negating the need for dose monitoring and adjustment in contrast to warfarin.

Various highly powered clinical trials have evidenced DOACs to be non-inferior to warfarin in many anticoagulation settings. For instance, rivaroxaban has proven efficacy in the treatment of non-valvular atrial fibrillation (AF), acute VTE and surgical VTE prophylaxis. Hence, DOACs are rapidly establishing themselves as the first-line treatment in such hypercoagulable states.

The 'normalisation' of DOAC use for such common conditions has naturally led to the consideration of DOAC use in rarer clotting disorders. One such example is antiphospholipid syndrome (APLS), an autoimmune disease in which various antiphospholipid antibodies (antibodies that recognise the anionic phospholipids on the cell membrane) effect clotting cascade activation and cause inappropriate thromboembolism. In APLS, anticoagulation is used as secondary prevention against thromboembolism. In recent years, there has been a surge in the use of DOACs in APLS, without any large-scale trials confirming their safety or efficacy in this condition. In large part, this is because of the relative scarcity of cases of APLS compared with other conditions requiring anticoagulation, such as non-valvular AF. Of particular concern is the use of DOACs as secondary prevention in so-called 'triple antibody positive' APLS, in which patients are seropositive for all three antiphospholipid antibodies and are known to have a far greater likelihood of

thromboembolic events than non-triple positive patients. This is highlighted in the following case study.

# CASE PRESENTATION

A 63-year-old patient was admitted generally unwell with multiorgan dysfunction involving her bowel, kidneys and heart. She had a previous diagnosis of triple antibody positive APLS in 1997. Her initial presentation at that time was one of altered cognition and tonic-clonic seizures, secondary to cerebral thrombosis. She was found to have positive antiphospholipid antibodies; specifically, IgG anticardiolipin antibodies, IgG anti- $\beta 2$ -glycoprotein-I antibody and lupus anticoagulant. These features retrospectively meet the Sydney criteria for APLS, which were set out in 2006.

Apart from hypertension, she had no other comorbidities. She had one uneventful pregnancy in 1983 with no history of miscarriage. She lived with her husband and was functionally independent.

She was maintained on warfarin for secondary prevention for two decades with a target international normalised ratio (INR) range of 3-4. Three months prior to her admission, her INR had become labile, reaching values above 7 with no cause identified. She had no thrombotic or haemorrhagic complications. In May 2018, her general practitioner switched her warfarin to rivaroxaban due to this labile INR. This was despite no recent complications from her APLS or anticoagulation.

Soon after, in June 2018, she was admitted to hospital in Spain with abdominal pain. The pain was severe, concentrated on the right side and not relieved with simple analgesia. She had no vomiting or diarrhoea. The patient underwent laparotomy and large bowel resection in Spain and the recorded macroscopic appearances were of visible clots on the bowel. She was discharged and returned home.

In early August 2018, she was admitted to hospital in the UK with small bowel ischaemia. She underwent emergency laparotomy, and ischaemia was confirmed on histopathology of the resected tissue. She subsequently developed acute kidney injury requiring renal replacement therapy in the form of continuous venovenous haemofiltration, and was transferred to our tertiary centre for further management.

# INVESTIGATIONS

Her full blood count identified a haemoglobin of $67~\mathrm{g / L}$ and platelets of $52\times 10^{9} / \mathrm{L}$ .

There was evidence of cardiac ischaemia and ventricular dysfunction, with a raised troponin $(365\mathrm{mg / L})$ and a raised brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) $(>35000\mathrm{ng / L})$ . An ECG showed sinus rhythm. Cardiac imaging in the form of echocardiogram and MRI was performed. The echocardiogram did not evidence a vegetation or a left ventricular thrombus. Her cardiac MRI showed myocardial oedema and a prior limited myocardial infarction, with an ejection fraction of $40\%$ .

Additionally, she had renal dysfunction, with a creatinine of $349\mathrm{mmol / L}$ , significantly raised off her baseline of $88\mathrm{mmol / L}$ . While this could be partly attributed to prerenal causes, the presence of microscopic haematuria and proteinuria suggested an intrinsic renal cause. An ultrasound of her renal tract showed increased echogenicity, further suggesting an intrinsic renal pathology. CT imaging of the renal tract also identified a likely adrenal infarct.

A chest X-ray showed a left pleural effusion with underlying collapse and consolidation. Her C-reactive protein (CRP) was $130\mathrm{mg / L}$ . As such, the patient was treated for a concurrent infection. Cultures of blood, urine and sputum did not isolate a pathogen.

# DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

This patient had multiorgan involvement with likely ischaemia of her heart, kidneys, adrenal glands and bowel. The diagnosis made was one of catastrophic APLS.

During the patient's work-up, a number of other differentials were considered. These included sepsis with multiorgan failure (however, lack of an identifiable organism made this unlikely), thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (later ruled out by a negative test for ADAMTS13 inhibitor and an elevated level of enzyme activity) and heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) (excluded by a negative ELISA screen).

Hence, based on the past medical history, histopathological findings, lack of identifiable organism and negative screening tests, catastrophic APLS was felt to be the most likely diagnosis.

# TREATMENT

Currently, no national guidelines for the treatment of catastrophic APLS exist, largely due to the lack of randomised control trials to inform management options. Treatment of catastrophic APLS consists primarily of giving unfractionated heparin and intravenous moderate-to-high dose steroids. There is also a growing evidence base for using plasma exchange (PLEX) or intravenous immunoglobulin in the initial management of organ-threatening APLS.

This patient was given immunosuppression in the form of pulsed methylprednisolone. To address presumed microvascular ischaemia, she was anticoagulated with an argatroban infusion (a direct factor II thrombin inhibitor). Treatment with heparin was considered and discussed, but given that HIT was initially considered as a differential for her presentation, it was decided it would be more appropriate to proceed with argatroban. Treatment with PLEX was also considered; however, after discussion between the rheumatology and haematology teams, it was decided to keep it as a reserve measure in the event of further deterioration.

She required several days of renal replacement therapy. Additionally, due to the presence of left ventricular systolic

dysfunction, she was discharged on cardioprotective medications for cardiac failure including a beta blocker and an ACE inhibitor.

# OUTCOME AND FOLLOW-UP

A decision was made to continue anticoagulation with warfarin, with a target INR of 3-4. Due to the suspicion of adrenal infarct, the patient was started on replacement steroid therapy, with a plan to investigate for adrenal insufficiency as an outpatient. She will be followed up in thrombosis and rheumatology clinic.

# DISCUSSION

DOACs, including rivaroxaban, are direct inhibitors of factor Xa, and as such act as anticoagulants to inhibit the clotting cascade. The major trials for rivaroxaban did not include thrombosis at unusual sites and were not specifically tested in those known to have thrombophilic disorders.[1-3] As such, their efficacy in treating thromboses associated with conditions such as APLS is not known.

APLS patients with previous arterial thrombosis have traditionally been managed with warfarin at a higher INR target of 3-4. This is in line with international recommendations for the secondary prophylaxis of arterial thrombosis in APLS. However, this 'high-intensity' anticoagulation has never been subjected to a randomised control trial against standard intensity anticoagulation in the setting of secondary prophylaxis against arterial thrombosis in APLS. Indeed, in the licensing trials rivaroxaban was compared with warfarin in patients suffering from a VTE where patients' thrombotic risk was not necessarily known, and when the standard anticoagulation target with warfarin is an INR of 2-3. Licensed doses of DOACs may therefore not achieve an anticoagulation effect equal to the aforementioned high-intensity anticoagulation recommended for secondary prophylaxis of arterial thrombosis in APLS.

Evidence on whether DOACs such as rivaroxaban are as effective as warfarin in preventing thrombosis in APLS is conflicting. Some data suggest that licensed doses of DOACs are not as effective as warfarin in the management of high-risk APLS patients. The Rivaroxaban in Antiphospholipid Syndrome (RAPS) trial looked at rivaroxaban versus warfarin in the management of APLS. The primary outcome was the difference in endogenous thrombin potential (ETP), a surrogate marker of thrombotic risk. Patients treated with rivaroxaban had a significantly higher ETP compared with those treated with warfarin, suggesting patients treated with rivaroxaban had a higher thrombotic risk. Similarly, the Trial on Rivaroxaban in Antiphospholipid Syndrome (TRAPS) trial investigated rivaroxaban versus warfarin in the management of high-risk triple antibody positive APLS. This study was terminated prematurely due to an excess of thrombotic events in the rivaroxaban arm of the trial.

# Learning points

Thrombosis in antiphospholipid syndrome (APLS) patients may present with multiorgan involvement.   
APLS patients have varying risk of thrombosis, related to the number of antibodies present.   
The effectiveness of direct-acting oral anticoagulants (DOACs) in secondary prevention of thrombosis in APLS is still unclear, with further research ongoing.   
DOACs may not be suitable anticoagulants for some patients with APLS, so changes to anticoagulation should be decided by a specialist with experience in the condition.

A meta-analysis of patients further suggested APLS patients treated with rivaroxaban were at a greater risk of thrombosis. Review of cases between 2000 and 2018 showed that high-risk APLS patients had a fourfold increased risk of thrombosis when treated with DOACs compared to warfarin.[10] Previous history of arterial thrombosis in APLS patients was also associated with failure of DOAC in comparison with warfarin. Further clinical trials are ongoing to assess the question of DOAC effectiveness in APLS.[11]
