# Primary Antiphospholipid Syndrome with Recurrent Transient Ischemic Attacks: Report of a Case and Its Successful Treatment

Masafumi IHARA, Haruo TANAKA and Yo NISHIMURA

A 35-year-old woman was admitted to our hospital with complaints of a two-year history of recurrent, daily episodes of transient ischemic attacks; the symptoms consisted of scotoma of her left eye, vertical diplopia, and paresthesia of her right arm. The presence of lupus anticoagulants and anticardiolipin antibodies led to the diagnosis of antiphospholipid syndrome (APS). After thrombotest values had decreased to $30\%$ (international normalized ratio: 1.5) with warfarin, her symptoms did not recur. This suggests that anticoagulant therapy is effective for the prevention of recurrence of ischemic events complicated by primary APS, even when they occur repeatedly. (Internal Medicine 37: 704-707, 1998)

Key words: lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin antibody, anticoagulant therapy

# Introduction

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is found in patients who have venous or arterial thrombosis in the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies (APLs), which include anticardiolipin antibodies (ACLs) and the lupus anticoagulants (LAs).

APLs are commonly seen in the presence of certain medical conditions such as autoimmune disorders (1), viral infections (2), lymphoproliferative disorders (3) and many others. APS in otherwise healthy individuals is classified as primary APS. We report here a case of primary APS with recurrent transient ischemic attacks (TIAs) successfully treated with warfarin.

# Case Report

A 35-year-old woman was admitted to our hospital with complaints of a two-year history of recurrent, daily episodes of scotoma of her left eye, vertical diplopia, and paresthesia of her right arm. The episodes were unrelated and lasted for a few minutes. She had also been suffering from Raynaud's phenomenon and intermittent tinnitus about once a week for two years. At 7 and 5 months before admission, she had experienced transient global amnesia. Those symptoms did not immediately precede her severe headaches which had been occurring twice a week for ten years. The patient had not been taking any medications including oral contraceptives. She had been sterile

since her first pregnancy at age 29, which resulted in a miscarriage in the third month, but at that time a thorough obstetrical examination was not performed. Her family history revealed that her mother had aborted spontaneously twice, and her older and younger sister once. There was a family history of migraine as well.

The results of general, neurological and ophthalmologic examinations performed at admission were noncontributory. Particularly noteworthy was a visual acuity of 20/20 in each eye, intact visual fields, and normal findings of the fundi. The patient was a well-nourished, right-handed woman without any noticeable skin lesions. Her blood pressure was $140 / 80\mathrm{mmHg}$ , pulse 90/min and regular, respiratory rate 14/min and temperature $37.0^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ . She described her episodes of scotoma of her left eye as follows: a sudden visual field defect over a small area which gradually expanded to occupy from a quarter to all of her visual fields, but a few minutes later completely disappeared.

Hematological values were normal except for a decreased platelet count at $10.2 \times 10^{4} / \mu l$ . Activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), measured using Thrombocheck APTT (International Reagent Corporation, Kobe) as an agent, was long at 40.6 seconds (control: 26-34), and had an activity of $66\%$ . Diluted prothrombin time (dPT) was also prolonged, which suggested the presence of LA. The patient's mother's and two sisters' plasma showed normal APTT and dPT, and blood chemistry findings were all within normal limits. The values of thrombin-antithrombin complex and D-dimer was $2.8 \mu g / l$

(control $< 3.2$ and $0.87~\mu \mathrm{g / ml}$ (control $< 0.90$ ), respectively, while platelet aggregability was within normal limits, and platelet-associated IgG was not detected. Results of serological tests for syphilis were negative. However, serologic and immunologic tests revealed the following abnormal values: 2,132 mg/dl of serum IgG, a titer of antinuclear antibody strongly positive at 1:10,240 with a speckled pattern, 24 phospholipid units of IgG ACLs (control $< 15$ ) $58~\mathrm{U / ml}$ of phospholipiddependent anti-beta 2-glycoprotein (control $< 3.5$ ). Searches for anti-single stranded DNA, anti-double stranded DNA, anti-Sm, and anti-ribonucleoprotein antibodies yielded negative results. Transthoracic echocardiography showed only a thickened mitral valve, but no evidence of vegetation or thrombus (Fig. 1). Observation via the transesophageal approach showed that the blood flow pattern in the left atrial appendix was normal and there was no sign of intracardiac right-to-left shunt, even with a Valsalva maneuver. B-mode and color flow imaging of the carotid arteries demonstrated no arteriosclerotic lesions on either side. Insonation of the ophthalmic arteries and carotid siphons via the transorbital approach also revealed normal bilateral flow patterns. Finally, magnetic resonance (MR) imaging and MR angiography demonstrated no intracranial abnormalities. In other words, no apparent embolus source could be detected.

The patient's symptoms and laboratory results did not meet the revised diagnostic criteria for systemic lupus erythematosus (4). Because it was suspected that all of her manifestations could be ascribed to complicated migraine, we started treatment with flunarizine hydrochloride to prevent migraine attacks, but this proved to be ineffective. Treatment with antiplatelet drugs also failed to reduce the frequency of the symptoms. Anticoagulant therapy was then started to alleviate her symptoms. After thrombotest values had decreased to $30\%$ [international normalized ratio (INR): 1.5], the daily episodes subsided completely without any recurrence. Interestingly, the headaches

never recurred, either. She has been treated as an outpatient for twenty months without any symptoms, and with thrombotest values maintained at between $20\%$ and $30\%$ (INR 1.5-1.8).

# Discussion

The process by which APS causes TIAs is thought to be either thrombotic or embolic. In the thrombotic process, in situ thrombus formation due to the hypercoagulable state associated with APS is presumed to lead to the TIAs. Levine et al (5) reported patients with lupus anticoagulants who developed visual disturbances, including transient visual loss, transient diplopia, or vertebrobasilar insufficiency due to thrombosis resulting from a hypercoagulable state. On the other hand, Fulham et al (6) reported seven patients with APLs whose cerebral ischemia was proven to be cardiogenic. They speculate that cardiac valvular lesions, in particular non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis, play an important role in the pathogenesis of cerebral ischemia in patients with APLs. In our case, echocardiography showed a mild thickening on the mitral valve, which sometimes accompanies APS (7), but showed no evidence of endocarditis. This means that we cannot attribute the pathogenesis of her frequent TIAs to only one process, but assume that hypercoagulable conditions either in the vessels or on the valves led to the TIAs (8).

Many cases of primary APS have been reported which caused a single episode of cerebral vascular disease (CVD). But only five cases of primary APS have been reported which developed frequent, stereotypic TIAs, without other apparent risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hypercholesterolemia, atrial fibrillation, and carotid or cardiac valvular lesions (5, 9-12, Table 1). Unlike the previously reported five cases, however, the frequency and variety of the ischemic events our patient experienced were noteworthy.

However, we now assume that her frequent episodes of TIAs arose from APS itself, not from migraine, because of the following three facts: absence of temporal correlation between her headaches and TIAs excludes complicated migraine; flunarizine hydrochloride, the drug of choice for the prevention of migraine, did not reduce the frequency of the headaches; the patient has been totally cured of headaches following anticoagulant therapy.

Hering et al measured ACL levels in a series of 94 migraine patients (13). A low positive ACL level was found in only one patient, and this became negative six months later. A comparison with positive rates for APLs in 267 healthy controls showed no significant differences. They conclude that APLs are unlikely to perform a pathogenetic role in migraine. In the present case, one possibility is that APLs activated platelet function or prostaglandin activity, and that the resulting metabolites stimulated the chemoreceptors of the vascular endothelium (14).

Primary APS should therefore be treated with anticoagulants, because it has a strong thrombotic tendency without accompanying vasculitic changes. Bick and Baker recommend that APS is best managed by long-term warfarin administration (15). To this regimen, they add low-dose aspirin in case of intracranial vascular thrombosis. In our case, too, symptoms abated with optimal warfarin and concomitant low-dose aspirin administration.

In cases of unexplained CVD, it should be determined whether coagulation abnormalities including LAs and ACLs

are present. Upon a diagnosis of primary APS, anticoagulant therapy should be initiated to alleviate and prevent ischemic manifestations resulting from hypercoagulable conditions. Positive results can be achieved, even when TIAs occur repeatedly as in the present case.
