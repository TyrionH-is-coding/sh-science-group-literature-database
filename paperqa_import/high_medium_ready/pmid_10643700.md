# INCREASED SOLUBLE VASCULAR CELL ADHESION MOLECULE 1 CONCENTRATIONS IN PATIENTS WITH PRIMARY OR SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS-RELATED ANTIPHOSPHOLIPID SYNDROME

Correlations with the Severity of Thrombosis

GILLES KAPLANSKI, PATRICE CACOUB, CATHERINE FARNARIER, VALÉRIE MARIN, REGINE GRÉGOIRE, ANNE GATEL, JEAN-MARC DURAND, JEAN-ROBERT HARLE, PIERRE BONGRAND, and JEAN-CHARLES PIETTE

Objective. Recent studies have shown that in vitro endothelial cells are activated by antiphospholipid antibodies and may support leukocyte adhesion. We studied levels of soluble intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (sICAM-1, sCD54), soluble vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (sVCAM-1, sCD106), and soluble E-selectin (soluble endothelial leukocyte adhesion molecule 1 [sELAM-1, sCD62E]) in sera from patients with primary antiphospholipid syndrome (primary APS), and compared them with those from patients with systemic lupus erythematosus-associated APS (SLE-APS) or pure SLE, as well as with those from 2 control groups composed of healthy volunteers and patients with thrombosis unrelated to autoimmune diseases.

Methods. Serum samples from 24 patients with primary APS, 15 patients with SLE-APS, 22 patients with pure SLE, 48 control patients with thrombosis, and 18 healthy volunteers were examined using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays specific for sICAM-1, sVCAM-1, and sELAM-1.

Results. Serum levels of sVCAM-1, but not sICAM-1 or sELAM-1, were significantly increased in all patient study groups compared with thrombosis control patients and healthy volunteers, but did not differ between the groups of patients with primary APS, SLE-APS, or pure SLE. Concentrations of sVCAM-1 were significantly higher in primary APS or SLE-APS patients with severe, recurrent thrombosis and were negatively correlated with platelet counts in primary APS patients. In patients with primary APS, sVCAM-1 levels were higher if there was thrombotic kidney involvement and correlated with creatinemia.

Conclusion. Serum sVCAM-1 concentrations are increased in patients with primary APS, especially those with repeated thrombotic events or kidney involvement. These findings suggest that endothelial/monocyte interaction may be important in the pathogenesis of primary APS.

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is defined by the association of arterial and/or venous thrombotic events, or recurrent fetal loss, with positive test results for antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL), consisting of the presence of lupus anticoagulant (LAC) and/or anticardiolipin antibodies (aCL) (1). The syndrome includes primary APS or APS associated with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE-APS) (1,2).

Antiphospholipid antibodies may also interfere with platelet activation (3). Alternatively, aPL may only be a marker for the presence of apoptotic cells during primary APS or SLE-APS, and may favor the clearance of apoptotic bodies by macrophages (5,6). Apoptotic blebs are known to express membrane phosphatidylserine (PS) extracellularly, even though PS is usually present only on the intracellular side of the cell membrane (7). PS has procoagulant properties which may contribute to the prothrombotic state associated with primary APS or SLE-APS (7).

Antiphospholipid antibodies are known to interfere with platelets, monocytes, or endothelial cells (EC) (8-10). Recently, experiments have suggested that in vitro activation of EC by aPL induces expression of E-selectin (endothelial leukocyte adhesion molecule 1 [ELAM-1, CD62E]), intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1, CD54), and vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM-1, CD106), and enhances leukocyte adhesion to EC (10,11). Leukocyte/endothelial adhesion is a major feature of endothelial activation and inflammation (12).

ELAM-1 is transiently expressed by inter

leukin-1 (IL-1), tumor necrosis factor $\alpha$ (TNF $\alpha$ ), or thrombin-activated EC, and mediates initial endothelial adhesion of neutrophils or memory lymphocytes (13-16). ICAM-1 is constitutively expressed by various cells including neutrophils, monocytes, lymphocytes, or EC, but its expression is increased by IL-1 or TNF $\alpha$ activation of EC (15). VCAM-1 is mainly present on EC activated by IL-1, TNF $\alpha$ , interferon- $\gamma$ , or IL-4 with a sustained kinetic (15). ICAM-1 supports neutrophil as well as mononuclear cell adhesion to EC, while VCAM-1 is mainly involved in mononuclear cell adhesion to EC (15).

These 3 molecules can be released from activated cell membranes and can be detected as soluble molecules (sELAM-1, sICAM-1, sVCAM-1) in serum or in body fluids (17,18). These soluble molecules may thus serve as useful markers of both leukocyte and EC activation in different diseases, including several autoimmune disorders such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA), scleroderma, vasculitis, or SLE (18-21). # PATIENTS AND METHODS

Patients. We retrospectively studied 61 patients with primary APS, SLE-APS, or SLE, as well as 48 control patients with vascular thromboses unrelated to APS and 18 healthy volunteers, for serum concentrations of the 3 sCAM: sVCAM-1, sELAM-1, and sICAM-1. The patients were placed in 3 diagnostic categories according to their symptoms.

Twenty-four patients (7 men and 17 women; mean $\pm$ SD age $40\pm 2$ years, range 26-45) had primary APS, defined by a history of at least 1 episode of venous or arterial thrombosis (with clinical and laboratory evidence) or 2 miscarriages, coupled with positive test results for LAC and/or aCL (IgG or IgM of $>20$ units) on 2 occasions $>8$ weeks apart, in the absence of other associated disorders (especially SLE), using previously reported strict exclusion criteria (22,23). Their clinical characteristics are shown in Table 1. Two patients in this group (patients 6 and 22) fulfilled the criteria for catastrophic APS (CAPS) (24).

Fifteen patients (3 men and 12 women; mean $\pm$ SD age $39\pm 3$ years, range 21-73) had both SLE (defined by the 1982 revised criteria of the American College of Rheumatology [ACR] [25]) and APS (as defined above). Clinical characteristics of these SLE-APS patients are shown in Table 2.

Twenty-two patients (3 men and 19 women; mean $\pm$ SD age $38\pm 4$ years, range 17-75) had SLE, meeting $\geq 4$ of the ACR 1982 revised criteria, without evidence of thrombosis or abortion, and had negative test results for both LAC and aCL (defined as "pure" SLE). Their clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 3.

Forty-eight patients (26 men and 22 women; mean $\pm$ SD age $49\pm 5$ years, range 30-75) had proven vascular thromboses—18 arterial (central nervous system [CNS], legs), 14 venous (deep vein thrombosis), and 16 both arterial and venous. All thromboses were unrelated to APS and SLE as

In addition, there were 18 healthy volunteers (9 men and 9 women; mean $\pm$ SD age $38\pm 6$ years, range 28-60).

We defined several subgroups among the 3 groups of patients with primary APS, SLE-APS, and pure SLE, depending on their clinical symptoms: Patients were defined as having a severe thrombotic profile if they had a clinical history of $\geq 3$ proven arterial and/or venous thrombotic events. Patients were defined as having severe obstetric involvement if they had a history of either $\geq 2$ spontaneous fetal deaths (at gestation of $>10$ weeks) or 1 fetal death associated with the loss of 1 neonate due to complications of severe preeclampsia or with

evidence of severe placental insufficiency (26). CNS involvement was defined by clinical manifestations of cerebral ischemia and abnormal findings on computerized tomodensitometry or magnetic resonance imaging. Renal involvement was defined by the presence of proteinuria $(>1\mathrm{~gm}/24$ hours) and/or creatinemia $(>120\mu \mathrm{moles} / \mathrm{liter})$ along with abnormal kidney biopsy findings (histologic features of thrombosis in patients with primary APS and SLE-APS, and evidence of autoimmune glomerulopathy in patients with SLE-APS and pure SLE).

Blood sampling and measurement of soluble adhesion molecules. Blood samples were obtained from each patient with primary APS, SLE-APS, or thrombosis unrelated to APS and SLE (at the time of, or in the days following, a thrombotic event). For patients with obstetric involvement, samples were obtained within 2 weeks of the event. For some of the patients with primary APS, another blood sample was obtained within 6 months after the initial measurement. Patients not diagnosed as having APS received no treatment at the time of sampling. Others might have received oral platelet antiaggregant or anticoagulant drugs. Patients known to have SLE received either steroids or hydroxychloroquine. None of the patients received immunosuppressive drugs at the time of the study. Blood was collected and serum was aliquoted and stored at $-80^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ until assayed. Soluble VCAM-1, sELAM-1, and sICAM-1 were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays specific for each molecule (R&D Systems, Abingdon, UK) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Interassay and intraassay coefficients of variation were $8\%$ and $6\%$ , respectively. Concentrations of sCAM from sera of 18 healthy volunteers in our laboratory (mean $\pm$ SD $543 \pm 30 \mathrm{ng} / \mathrm{ml}$ for sVCAM-1, $33 \pm 3 \mathrm{ng} / \mathrm{ml}$ for sELAM-1, and $233 \pm 18 \mathrm{ng} / \mathrm{ml}$ for sICAM-1) were not significantly different from control values provided by the manufacturer.

Statistical analysis. Analysis was done using the SPSS Base 7.5 statistical package (SPSS, Chicago, IL). Values of sCAM concentrations are presented as the mean $\pm$ SD. Differences between sCAM concentrations in the different groups were tested using Tukey's test. Correlations between variables were analyzed using Spearman's rank correlation. $P$ values less than 0.05 were considered significant. Tukey's box plot representations were used in some of the figures. Briefly, the horizontal line in the middle of a box marks the median of the samples, while the hinges of each box represent the 25th and 75th percentiles (thereby including $50\%$ of the data within a box). The vertical lines extending above and below each box show the range of values that fall within 1.5 SD of the hinges. Values between 1.5 and 3 SD outside the hinges are considered outside values.

# DISCUSSION

Soluble cell adhesion molecules are released from cells and are found in the circulation as well as in body fluids (17,18). These molecules have been studied in various diseases, notably chronic infectious diseases such as hepatitis B and C, in which sICAM-1 concentrations reflect liver inflammation (27,28). Other diseases studied include diabetes, ischemic strokes, and various autoimmune disorders, such as RA, SLE, scleroderma, or vasculitis (18-21). For SLE, elevated concentrations of sVCAM-1, but not of sICAM-1 or sELAM-1, have been found and may reflect disease exacerbation (19,21,29,30).

So far, no study findings have been reported concerning sCAM in primary APS or SLE-APS. However, experiments have shown that aPL in vitro can induce ELAM-1, ICAM-1, and VCAM-1 expression on EC in a $\beta_{2}$ GPI-dependent way (10,11). These data prompted us to investigate sCAM in sera from patients with primary APS or SLE-APS compared with that from healthy volunteers as well as patients with various thromboses or pure SLE. We observed that sVCAM-1 concentrations, but not those of sICAM-1 or sELAM-1, were elevated in sera from patients with primary APS or SLE-APS compared with that from healthy volunteers or thrombosis controls. However, although slightly higher in SLE-APS, sVCAM-1 concentrations did not differ significantly between primary APS, SLE-APS, and pure SLE.

For primary APS, sVCAM-1 concentrations were higher from patients with severe thrombotic profiles, including cerebral ischemic events, and correlated negatively with platelet counts. Concentrations of sVCAM-1 were also found to be higher for patients with renal thrombotic involvement, and were positively correlated with creatinemia. Similarly, among patients with SLE-APS, sVCAM-1 concentrations were higher for

patients with severe thrombosis, but not for those with renal involvement. For patients with SLE-APS and combined autoimmune and thrombotic renal involvement, however, sVCAM-1 concentrations were higher than those for patients with either isolated autoimmune involvement or no kidney disease. In addition, for patients with pure SLE, sVCAM-1 concentrations were almost significantly higher when there was renal involvement, compatible with previous findings (29,30).

Soluble VCAM-1, but not sICAM-1 or sELAM-1, appears to be an interesting molecule in primary APS. This may be surprising, considering the similarity of expression of the 3 molecules. These findings, however, did not result from a nonspecific elevation of sVCAM-1 due to thrombosis, since patients with various thromboses unrelated to aPL did not show significantly elevated concentrations of sCAM, including sVCAM-1. A nonspecific decreased clearance of sVCAM-1 due to renal failure can also be ruled out, since the 3 sCAM have almost the same molecular weight (90 kd for sICAM-1, 115 kd for sELAM-1, and 90–100 kd for sVCAM-1), and previous studies have shown that serum concentrations not only of sVCAM-1 but also of sELAM-1 and sICAM-1 are elevated in patients with chronic renal failure of various origins (17). Another explanation may be related to the relatively sustained endothelial expression of VCAM-1 (24–48 hours in vitro) compared with ELAM-1 (6–8 hours) and ICAM-1 (12–24 hours) (15), coupled with chronic endothelial activation related to autoimmune disorders. This hypothesis may be supported by the finding in this and previous studies of positive correlations between sVCAM-1 concentrations and the SLEDAI scores of SLE and SLE-APS patients.

Alternatively, these findings may suggest a potential role of VCAM-1 in the pathogenesis of thrombosis in primary APS and SLE-APS. VCAM-1 is known to be expressed on inflamed EC and to support the firm adhesion of mononuclear cells to endothelium through interaction with its ligand $\alpha 4\beta 1$ (very late activation antigen 4) (12,15). Monocytes appear to play a pivotal role in primary APS thrombogenesis. Indeed, studies have shown ex vivo that monocytes of primary APS patients strongly express tissue factor on their membranes and have procoagulant properties (31). Studies have also shown that in vitro activation of monocytes with aPL induces tissue factor messenger RNA transcription and protein synthesis (9). In addition, monocyte adhesion to EC in vitro induces tissue factor expression on monocytes in a mechanism that depends on ELAM-1-mediated adhesion (32). Furthermore,

<!-- page 9 -->
aPL-activated EC express VCAM-1 and support monocyte adhesion in vitro (11).

Therefore, aPL may induce endothelial VCAM-1 expression and monocyte adhesion as well as tissue factor production and expression by monocytes. Tissue factor expression may initiate the coagulation cascade, leading to thrombin synthesis and fibrin clot formation. Subsequently, thrombin is able to induce VCAM-1 endothelial expression, monocyte adhesion, and endothelial tissue factor synthesis, favoring a positive thrombogenic loop (33). Moreover, the association of sVCAM-1 with renal involvement may reflect local endothelial activation and the occurrence of thrombotic events in kidney tissue, where VCAM-1 is known to be highly expressed on microvessel endothelium as well as on tubular epithelium and smooth muscle cells (34,35).

In conclusion, this study shows that sVCAM-1 concentrations are increased among patients with primary APS and correlate with the severity of thrombotic and renal involvement. These findings suggest that in vivo endothelial activation occurs during primary APS, especially in kidney, and point to the possible role of monocyte/endothelial interaction in the prothrombotic state associated with this syndrome.
