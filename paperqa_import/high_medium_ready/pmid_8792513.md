# The Immunogenetics of the Antiphospholipid Syndrome, Anticardiolipin Antibodies, and Lupus Anticoagulant

Gian Domenico Sebastiani, Mauro Galeazzi, Gabriella Morozzi, and Roberto Marcolongo

Whether a genetic predisposition to develop the antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) and to produce anticardiolipin antibodies (aCL) and lupus anticoagulant (LAC) exists has been addressed by family studies and by population studies on primary APS and on aCL in diseases other than primary APS. Various studies suggest a familial occurrence of aCL and LAC, with or without clinical evidence of APS. This familial tendency could be genetically determined, because APS, aCL, and LAC occur in families carrying haplotypes which contain HLA-DR4, -DR7, and -DRw53. Population studies on primary APS also indicate that HLA genes have a role in conferring susceptibility to develop primary APS. Again, DR4, DR7, and DRw53 are the relevant loci. Population studies on aCL in diseases other than primary APS indicate that aCL are associated with DR4, DR7, and DRw53, at least when they are found in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus. Because HLA-DR4, -DR7, and -DRw53 are in linkage disequilibrium, the genetic association of aCL could be with DRw53 and, depending on the regional frequency of DR4 or DR7, it could be linked with either DR4 or DR7. HLA-DR4 seems to be more important in Anglo-Saxons, whereas DR7 emerges in populations of Latin origin. In this report we review our studies and the pertinent literature in this field.

GENETIC predisposition to autoimmune diseases has been documented, both in human and in animal models. Autoimmune disorders are characterized by abnormal B cell activation, autoantibody production, abnormal T cell regulation, and complement abnormalities. Investigations have therefore focused on the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) region, human leukocyte antigen (HLA) in humans, which is involved in the control of

immune recognition and response. Many autoimmune diseases are associated with genes in the MHC region. In some cases, for example, celiac disease, insulin-dependent diabetes, and narcolepsy, the degree of association with polymorphism at a given locus is so strong (relative risk of 10 or more, presence of the marker in more than $90\%$ of the patients) that the locus can plausibly be assumed to be involved in the pathogenesis. In other cases, the degree of association is much lower. For example in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), the HLA allele most significantly associated, DR3, shows a relative risk around 3 and is present in only $30\%$ to $40\%$ of the patients. In this case, the weak association may be attributable to linkage disequilibrium with an undetected polymorphism of a primarily involved linked locus or, alternatively, other alleles, not necessarily in the MHC, participate in conferring genetic susceptibility to the disease. Another hypothesis is that

<!-- page 2 -->
SLE is not a homogeneous disease, but can be divided into subgroups showing more consistent associations with HLA alleles. In some instances, MHC antigens seem to be associated with specific autoantibodies rather than with the disease itself.1-3 Thus, it appears that MHC genes may influence not only the expression of autoimmune diseases, but also the production of autoantibodies that can be found in these diseases.

Many researchers in the field of immunogenetics have investigated possible associations between the antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) or the various antibodies directed against negatively charged phospholipids and MHC genes or their products.

In this article, studies on the genetic predisposition to APS and to the production of anticardiolipin antibodies (aCL) and lupus anticoagulant (LAC) are summarized and discussed.

# HLA, ANTIPHOSPHOLIPID SYNDROME, AND ANTIPHOSPHOLIPID ANTIBODIES

The question of whether a genetic predisposition to develop APS and to produce aCL and LAC exists can be examined both in animal models and in humans. The presence of aCL has been reported in some, but not all, SLE-prone mice. They can be detected in MRL/MP/lpr/lpr (MRL/lpr) and $\mathrm{MRL} / + / +$ mice4 as well as in NZWx BXSB F1 mice, but NZB/NZW F1 mice, a classical murine model of SLE, do not develop these autoantibodies. These antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL) can also be spontaneously produced in normal C57BL/6J mice, and estrogen treatment of these mice augments the incidence as well as the levels of these autoantibodies. It thus appears that the genetic background of mice can influence the production of aCL, and this production can be modulated by hormones. Nevertheless, it has not been clarified whether aCL are constitutively expressed by mice or induced by antigenic stimulation. There is a preferential usage of certain Vh and Vk chains in aCL of NZW $\times$ BXSB F1 mice. This fact could indicate that in these mice aCL are not germline encoded but antigen driven.

In humans, the contribution of immunogenetics to the development of aCL, LAC, and APS

has been addressed mainly by family studies and by population studies looking at the HLA region. The APS may exist both as a primary condition as well as in the setting of another autoimmune disease (mainly SLE), and this implies possible differences in the association with HLA. Furthermore, aPL are a heterogeneous family of autoantibodies. Some aPL can be found in autoimmune diseases, but others appear during the course of infectious diseases, neoplasias, or are drug related; they can also be present as an isolated phenomenon in healthy individuals. Their presence is not always associated with the clinical manifestations of APS, and even in experimental animal models not all aPL are of pathogenetic significance. Some aPL bind preferentially to anionic phospholipids, whereas others react with zwitterionic phospholipids, and their binding can be either enhanced or depressed by $\beta_{2}$ -glycoprotein I, depending on the source of aPL. Therefore, what we call "antiphospholipid antibodies" may comprise a group of antibodies whose unique common feature is their reactivity against phospholipids, but with different specificity and different HLA associations.

HLA alleles can be detected by conventional serologic methods or by molecular methods, such as restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) analysis of polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-amplified HLA genes and DNA typing by oligonucleotide hybridization. Molecular methods allow better definition of the various polymorphisms.

# FAMILY STUDIES

Familial occurrence of aCL or LAC with or without clinical evidence of APS has been documented since 1980. Exner et al described three sets of siblings with LAC, two of which had more than one clinically affected member.7 Matthew et al described a family with primary APS, consisting of four affected members, and Jolidon et al reported a family with three cases of primary APS.9

Various studies[10-14] examined HLA by serological methods. Dagenais et al described an English Canadian family in which aCL were associated with a spectrum of clinical manifestations, from asymptomatic carriers to the typical

<!-- page 3 -->
thrombotic disease in association with SLE and autoimmune thyroid disease.10 They found the paternal haplotype A30; Cw3; B60; DR4; DRw53; DQw3 to be associated with aCL. This haplotype was present in a subject with primary APS, in another with SLE and aCL, and in a third case in association with aCL without clinical manifestations of either APS or SLE. The same haplotype was also present in a healthy subject without aCL and LAC, suggesting either variable penetrance in the expression of the gene responsible for the production of these antibodies, or that other genetic or environmental factors may play a role in the production of these antibodies and their expression as the APS.

The occurrence of LAC in families carrying haplotypes that contained either DR4 or DR7 also has been reported by others.11-13 May et al described a family, including identical twins and their mother, in which all members had SLE and presented with different manifestations of APS.14 The mother and the twins shared the HLA haplotype that included DR4, DRw53, and DQw7, whereas C4A or C4B deficiencies could not be implicated in the autoimmune process. Another 21-year-old son was HLA identical to the twins; he had lived with his mother and the twins since birth, and he did not have evidence of aCL, LAC, or any other clinical or serologic abnormality. A sister sharing the APS-associated haplotype also was clinically and serologically normal. Again, these findings indicate that HLA contributions are not the sole determinant of autoantibody production or disease expression in APS. Other non-MHC-linked genes or environmental factors could be implicated. Familial occurrence of aCL has been documented by others.15

In conclusion, family studies suggest a genetic predisposition to APS, either when it presents as a primary condition or when it is seen in the context of SLE. It appears that this genetic predisposition is in part accounted for by the HLA locus, the most consistent associations being those with DR4 and DRw53. Furthermore, it appears that LAC and aCL are both associated with the same HLA antigens, even if these two methods of detecting aPL do not overlap completely.

# POPULATION STUDIES ON PRIMARY ANTIPHOSPHOLIPID SYNDROME

In a study of primary APS and HLA associations, as detected by molecular methods, HLA-DQw7 (DQB1*0301 allele) was significantly associated with disease. All patients with DQw7 were HLA-DR4 or DR5 positive.[16] Asherson et al reported on 13 English patients with primary APS, in which both class II genes and class III genes were examined by molecular methods.[17] They found that significant differences were limited to the HLA class II region of the MHC. In fact, DR4 and DRw53 were found with increased frequency in patients compared with controls, whereas DR3 was absent in all patients. No significant associations between any DQB alleles or C4 or 21-hydroxylase gene polymorphisms and primary APS were found, although DQw7 was not significantly increased in patients. Because in their series DQB1*0301 (DQw7) and DQB1*0302 (DQw8) were only slightly raised in patients compared with controls, the authors suggest that the high incidence of DR4 is not caused by its linkage disequilibrium with these two genes. Instead, their data suggest that the increase of DQw7 could be attributable to linkage disequilibrium between DR4-DRw53 and DQw7, and that the DR locus is more strongly associated with primary APS than the DQB locus. The highest relative risk was conferred by the DRw53 allele, which was present in all aCL-positive patients with primary APS, suggesting that this allele may play a direct role in mediating an autoimmune response to phospholipids. Another interesting consideration that emanates from their work is that primary APS has a different immunogenetic predisposition when compared with SLE, because DR3, DRw52, and deletion of C4A gene, which are strongly associated with SLE in white and black populations, were decreased in patients with primary APS.

Other studies investigated HLA antigens by serological methods. Camps Garcia et al analyzed HLA class II antigens in 19 patients from the south of Spain.[18] They showed that primary APS is associated with DR4, DQw7, and DRw53. The association with DRw53 also has been reported by others.[19] Alarcon-Segovia and

<!-- page 4 -->
Granados described an increase of DR7 in Mexican patients with primary APS.[20] Trabace et al examined HLA-DR and DQ antigens in 49 Italian women with unexplained recurrent spontaneous abortions. Twenty-five were aCL positive, and there was an association with DR7.[21] Thus, population studies suggest that HLA genes have a role in conferring susceptibility to develop the primary APS. Again, DR4, DR7, and DRw53 appear to be the relevant loci. HLA-DR4 seems to be more important in Anglo-Saxons, whereas DR7 emerges in populations of Latin origin (Table 1). Results of those studies in which HLA polymorphisms have been investigated by molecular methods overlap those obtained by serological typing.

# POPULATION STUDIES ON ANTIPHOSPHOLIPID ANTIBODIES IN DISEASES OTHER THAN PRIMARY APS

Most of these studies deal with SLE and aCL, probably because aCL are more easily detectable than LAC. Reports can be divided into two groups: those in which HLA polymorphism was investigated by conventional serological methods (or by gel electrophoresis of plasma samples for C4 allotypes) and those in which molecular methods were used.

# HLA Typing by Molecular Methods

We studied 42 Italian SLE patients by locus-specific amplification followed by allele-specific

oligonucleotide hybridization, to analyze the frequency of HLA-DPB1 locus alleles and their correlations with the presence of aCL. Few studies have examined the association of HLA-DP antigens and disease, probably because of the difficulty in typing these antigens by cellular methods. The major problem of DP typing by these methods is the limited availability of cellular reagents, which also explains the large number of DP "blanks" found in the population. For this reason, HLA typing by molecular methods may be of particular interest in the study of the DP locus. We found a positive association of aCL with DPB1*1401 and DPB1*0301 alleles.[35] Remarkably, DPB1*0301 and DPB1*1401 share the same sequence at the hypervariable regions C, D, and F and differ slightly in region A. This could signify that an epitope shared by their membrane products and, possibly, by other HLA antigens, is of crucial importance for the DP-related susceptibility to aCL in SLE. Nevertheless, the cumulative frequency of DPB1*1401 and *0301 was only approximately 45% in aCL-positive patients. Thus, the biological relevance of this association is dubious, and it is possible that this genetic subgrouping possesses some clinical significance. For instance, we found that high levels of aCL correlated with thrombotic events and recurrent abortions in the same group of patients.[36] Moreover, this

association with HLA-DP could simply reflect linkage disequilibrium with HLA-DR or DQ, even if this hypothesis seems unlikely.

The finding of an association between DPB1*1401 and aCL is somewhat strengthened by the observation of the same association in lupus patients with antiendothelial cell antibodies, which correlate with aCL.37

Interestingly, Gulko et al found no association between aCL and either HLA-DR and DQ alleles in 46 white and 45 African Americans with SLE.[38]

Overall the results of these studies suggest a confusing picture. However, most of the reports on SLE seem to indicate that aCL are associated with DR4, DR7, or the closely linked antigen DRw53. The association of aCL with C4A or C4B null alleles is less evident and may be of some importance only in American blacks. In addition, it appears that the disease itself may influence aCL-HLA associations, because no association is found in diseases other than SLE (Table 2).

Molecular methods for the detection of HLA alleles will improve our knowledge, particularly when they are applied to the study of those loci that are difficult to investigate by conventional typing methods.

# CONCLUSIONS

Antiphospholipid syndrome can appear either as an isolated entity (primary APS) or in the context of another disease, most commonly SLE. The primary APS may be a variant of SLE or a distinct disease entity in its own right. Genetic studies may help to answer this question, by demonstrating that APS has a pattern of HLA associations distinct from SLE. Current studies on the primary APS indicate that it is genetically distinct from SLE. In fact, although DR3 is the class II allele of greatest importance in SLE, this allele seems to be decreased in

patients with the primary APS, where, by contrast, DR4, DR7, and DRw53 are the associated alleles. The picture is less clear if one looks at aCL and LAC. Of course, when these autoantibodies are found in patients with the primary APS, they show the same associations, but these HLA associations become less evident in SLE and disappear in other diseases, whether autoimmune or not. It seems that the association with DR4, DR7, and DRw53 in SLE is only evident when aCL and LAC are found in patients with secondary APS, thus supporting the conclusion that these HLA antigens are in fact associated with APS and not with the presence of aPL, which are a heterogeneous family of autoantibodies with possibly heterogeneous HLA associations. This latter hypothesis is reinforced by the observation that LAC and aCL, albeit representing probably different types of aPL, show identical HLA associations when found in the setting of APS.

Furthermore, the ethnic origin of the patients also influences the pattern of HLA associations. In some Latin populations such as Italians and Mexicans, aCL are associated with DR7, whereas in Anglo-Saxons aCL are associated with DR4. Both DR4 and DR7 are in strong linkage disequilibrium with DRw53.

In conclusion, immunogenetic studies suggest that APS is an entity distinct from SLE, even if it can appear in the course of this latter disease. The genetic predisposition to APS can be at least in part explained by the influence of certain HLA alleles. However, these alleles may be apparent only because of their linkage disequilibrium with an as-yet unidentified primarily involved HLA locus, or they could act in cooperation with other genes, possibly even outside the MHC. Thus, the search for a more strongly associated polymorphism is actively pursued whenever new loci are identified in the HLA region.
