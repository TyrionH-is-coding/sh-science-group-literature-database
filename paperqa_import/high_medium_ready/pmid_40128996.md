# Hepatocellular Carcinoma Arising Adjacent to Antiphospholipid Syndrome-associated Hepatic Infarction in a Non-cirrhotic Liver

## Abstract

A man in his 70s with antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) presented with a hepatic mass. Initial imaging suggested hepatic infarction, as the liver tumor markers were normal, and coagulation tests indicated a hypercoagulable state. Three years later, follow-up imaging revealed tumor enlargement, leading to a biopsy-confirmed hepatocellular carcinoma diagnosis. Transarterial embolization was performed followed by lenvatinib therapy. The patient achieved complete response with no recurrence for over eight months. This case highlights the potential of APS to contribute to liver carcinogenesis in non-cirrhotic patients and illustrates the need for individualized treatment strategies.

Keywords: antiphospholipid syndrome, hepatocellular carcinoma, hepatic infarction, non-cirrhotic liver, transarterial embolization, lenvatinib

## Introductions

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an autoimmune disorder characterized by recurrent thrombosis induced by antiphospholipid antibodies such as anti-cardiolipin (CL), anti-β2-glycoprotein I (β2-GPI) antibodies, and lupus anticoagulant ( 1 ). Although APS predominantly manifests with vascular events, such as deep vein thrombosis or stroke, it can also impair hepatic circulation, leading to hepatic infarction ( 2 ). Hepatic infarction, in turn, is an uncommon phenomenon due to the dual blood supply to the liver through both the hepatic artery and the portal vein.

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common primary liver cancer and is typically associated with chronic liver disease or cirrhosis. An analysis of autopsy cases has shown that the development rate of HCC in non-cirrhotic livers accounts for 10.7% of all HCC cases in Japan ( 3 ); HCC occurrence in APS is even more unusual.

We herein report a case of HCC that developed three years after the diagnosis of APS-induced hepatic infarction, providing a rare insight into the potential role of APS-related vascular dysfunction in liver tumorigenesis.

## Case Report

A man in his 70s was referred to our hospital by another hospital after a hepatic mass was detected during pacemaker implantation for sick sinus syndrome at that hospital in 202X. Prior to pacemaker implantation, the patient underwent dynamic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which revealed a 3-cm hypovascular mass in the posterior segment of the liver. MRI showed a tumor with a low intensity on the T1-weighted image and high intensity on the T2-weighted and diffusion-weighted images with no evidence of diffusion restriction on the apparent diffusion coefficient map. Dynamic imaging demonstrated continuous disruption of the portal venous flow and occlusion of the right hepatic vein, raising concerns about either a malignant tumor or hepatic infarction ( Fig. 1 ). His body mass index was 22.1, and he had never experienced a significant weight change. He had a history of asthma but experienced no exacerbations with a combination of bronchodilators and steroids, and no liver disease had ever been identified during routine health checkups. He had never smoked and had consumed an average of 22 g of alcohol per day for approximately 40 years before pacemaker implantation.

The tumor showed no contrast enhancement from the arterial phase through the delayed phase (E-G). Portal venous flow to the posterior segment was disrupted in the portal venous phase (F; arrow), and the right hepatic vein draining from the tumor to the inferior vena cava appeared occluded in the delayed phase (G; arrow).

Comprehensive liver tumor marker screening and coagulation tests, including D-dimer, fibrin and fibrinogen degradation products (FDP), were performed ( Table 1 ). Tumor markers were carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), and protein induced by vitamin K absence or antagonist-II (PIVKA-II), all of which were within normal limits. However, D-dimer and FDP levels were elevated, suggesting that the hepatic tumor was a consequence of hepatic infarction. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) further supported the diagnosis by showing disruption of the portal venous flow ( Fig. 2B ), cavernous transformation at the porta hepatis ( Fig. 2B ), and a disrupted hepatic vein ( Fig. 2C ). Early enhancement around the tumor, suggestive of a compensatory increase in arterial blood flow, was observed ( Fig. 2D ). These findings suggest chronic insufficiency of the posterior segment blood flow. A definitive pathological examination via a liver biopsy was not performed because the tumor's location posed a risk of lung puncture based on an abdominal ultrasonographic evaluation. Further investigation of the hypercoagulable state revealed the presence of anti-β2-GPI antibodies, confirming the diagnosis of APS. Based on these findings, the hepatic mass was attributed to a hepatic infarction caused by underlying APS. Warfarin therapy was initiated to maintain the patient's prothrombin time - international normalized ratio (PT-INR) at 2.0-3.0, with follow-up visits scheduled every 3 months to monitor both APS and the hepatic lesion.

The size of the hepatic mass remained stable for two years ( Fig. 3A, B ). Three years later, follow-up CT revealed enlargement of a 2.3-cm low-density area adjacent to the original lesion ( Fig. 3C, D ). CEA, CA19-9, and AFP levels remained low ( Table 2 ), and the imaging findings did not definitively indicate malignancy. The PIVKA-II level was not measured because it was induced by warfarin, leading to a marked increase. Given the possibility of further ischemic events, the patient was monitored for an additional three months. However, a new 2.8-cm low-density area expanded with faint contrast enhancement ( Fig. 3E, F ), prompting the need for a liver biopsy.

A tumor-targeted biopsy from the new tumor and a background liver biopsy were performed following the temporary discontinuation of warfarin. A pathologic examination revealed neoplastic changes in the tumor, which tested positive for HepPar-1 and Arginase-1 ( Fig. 4A-C ), leading to a diagnosis of HCC. A background liver biopsy showed minimal lymphocyte infiltration on hematoxylin-eosin staining and mild portal area fibrosis on trichrome staining, with no pericellular fibrosis on silver staining ( Fig. 4D-F ), consistent with the non-cirrhotic liver findings. Thus, a diagnosis of HCC arising in a non-cirrhotic liver was made.

Immunohistochemistry showed positive staining for HepPar-1 (B) and Arginase-1 (C), confirming the diagnosis of hepatocellular carcinoma. All microscopic images were captured using a 20× objective lens.

Following the diagnosis of HCC, surgical resection was considered; however, the patient declined due to concerns about the risk of complications, such as further hepatic infarction, and an interventional radiology procedure was performed. A CT scan taken 2 months after the biopsy and just before the interventional treatment showed that the mass had increased to 5.5 cm and exhibited intra-tumoral hemorrhaging ( Fig. 5A, B ). The patient experienced no abdominal pain or other subjective symptoms prior to treatment. Transarterial embolization (TAE) was performed to control the intra-tumoral hemorrhaging. Three weeks after TAE, follow-up CT revealed slight shrinkage of the embolized area and residual early enhancement at the tumor margin ( Fig. 5C ), suggesting viable tumor tissue. Given the patient's tendency toward thrombus formation, additional TAE or chemoembolization procedures were determined to pose a high risk of complications. Therefore, oral lenvatinib was initiated. The patient showed a favorable response, achieving a complete response (CR) based on the modified response evaluation criteria in solid tumors (RECIST) criteria, and has continued to be monitored, with no signs of recurrence for over eight months ( Fig. 5D ).

## Discussion

This case describes the development of HCC three years after the diagnosis of APS and hepatic infarction. APS is an autoimmune disorder characterized by recurrent thrombosis that often causes vascular complications in multiple organs ( 4 ). Hepatic infarction can occur asymptomatically and is difficult to distinguish from liver cancer, which may be diagnosed after resection ( 5 ). The progression from hepatic infarction to HCC, as seen in this case, is notable, given the absence of conventional HCC risk factors, such as cirrhosis or chronic viral hepatitis.

In cases of sudden and extensive hepatic infarction, this condition can be fatal ( 6 ). During the acute phase of severe hepatic infarction, the interruption of the portal blood flow is typically not compensated for by the hepatic artery, leading to necrosis ( 7 ). In contrast, chronic hepatic infarction caused by the occlusion of both peripheral portal vein branches and hepatic veins, as observed in the present case, is characterized by parenchymal atrophy due to an increase in arterial blood flow as a compensatory mechanism. This compensatory change may result in well-defined congested areas, referred to as Zahn's infarcts ( 8 ).

One limitation of this case is that a definitive pathological diagnosis of the infarcted area in the liver could not be made at the initial examination. Thus, the possibility of HCC lesions cannot be completely excluded. Given the inaccessibility of the lesion for a biopsy, it was monitored carefully by CT every three to six months. Over three years, the lesion showed no increase in size, and even following the development and treatment of HCC adjacent to the lesion, the infarcted area remained stable. These observations support the benign nature of the lesion, which is consistent with a liver infarction.

A PubMed literature search from 1994 to 2024 using the keywords “antiphospholipid syndrome” and “hepatocellular carcinoma” returned 10 results. However, these results include cases that described secondary APS occurring as a complication of pre-existing HCC ( 9 ) or cases of HCC in a patient with chronic hepatitis and positive anti-CL antibodies ( 10 ). No reports of HCC that developed against the background of APS without chronic hepatitis were found. Expanding the search to include cancers associated with APS yielded a review by Islam, which summarized 31 cases of malignancies arising in the context of APS and reviewed previous literature on cancers in patients with APS ( 11 ). While this review discusses the association of APS with hematologic and urologic malignancies, it provides no insight into the mechanisms linking APS to liver carcinogenesis. Notably, none of the cases in the review involved HCC, which emphasizes the novelty of our case. This highlights the need for a further investigation of the relationship between APS-related vascular dysfunction and liver cancer development.

As no studies have specifically examined the mechanisms linking APS to liver carcinogenesis, we referred to the pathophysiology of Budd-Chiari syndrome (BCS), which presents with similar vascular features. In BCS, hepatic venous outflow obstruction results in chronic ischemia, leading to inflammation, hepatocyte injury, and regenerative proliferation, all of which promote HCC development ( 12 ). Carcinogenesis associated with BCS is typically linked to liver fibrosis. However, liver carcinogenesis without fibrosis, as observed in this case, does not fully account for all pathological conditions. In contrast, the hypoxic environment caused by hepatic infarction can enhance the expression of hypoxia-inducible and vascular endothelial growth factors, both of which are known contributors to tumor progression in the liver ( 13 ). In the present case, carcinogenesis may reflect an ischemic response mediated by these molecules. These findings suggest that multiple mechanisms contribute to the liver carcinogenesis presented in this case.

Treatment for HCC was determined based on the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer staging system 2022 ( 14 ). This patient was classified as being at an early stage when a liver biopsy was performed; however, the tumor progressed to an intermediate stage, for which TAE or systemic chemotherapy is recommended while treatment options are considered. In Japan, the preferred systemic chemotherapy regimen is immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy ( 15 ). However, since this patient had APS, an autoimmune disease, there was a concern that administering an immune checkpoint inhibitor might exacerbate APS. Therefore, we selected TAE and the tyrosine kinase inhibitor lenvatinib. Lenvatinib, administered after TAE, successfully inhibited blood flow in areas inadequately embolized by TAE, and the patient's condition was well controlled, achieving CR according to the modified RECIST criteria.

This case highlights the importance of careful radiological monitoring, as no tumor marker elevations were detected throughout the disease course. This also illustrates the need for individualized treatment strategies, as the hypercoagulable state in the pathophysiology of APS complicates the use of standard interventional therapies. Further research is warranted to better understand the association between APS and liver tumorigenesis, particularly in non-cirrhotic livers.
