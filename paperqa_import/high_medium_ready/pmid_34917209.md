# ABSTRACT

Vascular surgery for chronic limb threatening ischemia (CLTI) in patients with antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) has a poor outcome with a high rate of postoperative thrombotic complications. However, there is little data regarding outcomes of endovascular treatment (EVT). This manuscript reports an interesting case of APS with CLTI in which timely EVT and continuous anticoagulant therapy not only prevented amputation but also thrombotic events over a long period of time. A 41-year-old man with CLTI in the left lower limb was seen and examined. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography revealed thrombotic occlusion in both iliac arteries and the left popliteal artery. Activated partial thromboplastin time was prolonged, and anti- $\beta2$ glycoprotein I antibodies and lupus anticoagulants were present. After starting antithrombotic therapy with warfarin, EVT was performed using self-expandable stents in both iliac arterial lesions, and a marked reduction in limb ischemia was observed. Furthermore, the patient was free from thrombotic events for six years until a change in the anticoagulant led to stent thrombosis. This case suggests that EVT using stents along with continuous antithrombotic therapy with warfarin in patients with APS and CLTI could be very effective for immediate relief of ischemia and long-term reduction in thrombotic events.

<Learning objective: APS is a rare but important cause of CLTI, and outcomes in patients with APS who receive vascular surgery for CLTI are poor because of a high rate of postoperative thrombotic complications. EVT using a stent can be a useful option if it is coupled with sustained antithrombotic therapy using a vitamin K antagonist, namely warfarin. In addition, administering direct oral anticoagulants as anticoagulant therapy following EVT in APS patients should be very carefully considered.>

# Introduction

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an autoimmune disorder characterized by the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies including anti- $\beta 2$ glycoprotein I antibodies (a $\beta 2\mathrm{GP}1$ ) and lupus anticoagulants (LA). APS also increases risk of stroke and myocardial infarction [1]. However, data regarding its relevance to arterial thrombosis and the outcome of the patients undergoing endovascular treatment (EVT) remain limited. This manuscript reports on an interesting case of APS and chronic limb threatening ischemia (CLTI) that was successfully treated by EVT followed by antithrombotic therapy with warfarin for six years. We obtained publication consent from the patient and institutional review board.

# Case report

A 41-year-old man with no known risk factors for atherosclerosis aside from being a smoker was referred to our hospital for resting pain and loss of sensation in his left toes for a month. He had been aware of intermittent claudication in both legs for several years. Physical examination revealed cyanosis, cold left toes, and absence of bilateral femoral artery pulsation. Consequently, the ankle brachial index (ABI) was very low for both feet (right, 0.75; left, 0.70). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CCT) revealed extensive thrombotic occlusion of the bilateral iliac arteries and a left popliteal arterial thrombus with decreased left leg perfusion (Fig. 1A,B). # Discussion

This manuscript presents a case of APS that overcame severe lower limb ischemia in response to EVT and sustained antithrombotic therapy, and remained eventless for six years. Arterial complications of APS occur mainly in the cerebrovascular and coronary arteries, with the prevalence of complications in the peripheral arteries at only $6\%$ [1]. Moreover, the prevalence of APS in patients with peripheral artery disease was reported to be less than $4\%$ [2]. Consequently, there is a paucity of data regarding the outcomes of patients with APS who undergo revascularization procedures. A common observation from a few clinical reports on this topic is the high rate of thrombotic events after vascular surgery. It has been reported that $90\%$ of patients with APS who underwent vascular surgery suffered graft thrombosis within one month after surgery, with $29\%$ of these patients requiring amputation [3,4]. The patient in this study avoided amputation by undergoing EVT and antithrombotic therapy with warfarin. This favorable outcome can be explained by the dramatic reduction of the risk of lower limb ischemia with EVT. EVT is a minimally invasive procedure that has recently been recognized as an alternative for revascularization in patients with CLTI. The efficacy of stenting the aorta

and major arteries has previously been demonstrated, even in patients with APS and limb ischemia [5]. However, long-term patency after EVT in patients with APS remains uncertain because of the absence of published data. The patient in this study suffered iliac stent thrombosis six years after EVT. Vascular endothelial damage by re-EVT was suspected as having initiated thrombus formation in the vessel. Several independent risk factors have been identified for long-term patency, including female gender, diabetes, renal failure, absence of aspirin, vessel diameter $< 8 \mathrm{~mm}$ , and outflow lesions, but none of these were present in the patient [6]. Therefore, the hypercoagulability associated with APS is thought to have contributed significantly to the recurrence of intravascular events. Another reason for the benign outcome in this patient is that continuous combined antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy not only prevented long-term stent thrombosis but also promoted steady regression of the obstructive thrombus in the left popliteal artery. There have been no prospective trials of secondary thromboprophylaxis of systemic arterial events in patients with APS. Based on the present data, patients with APS and arterial thrombosis are recommended to be treated with warfarin (PT-INR $>3.0$ ) or combined antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy (PT-INR 2.0-3.0) [7]. Moreover, one study reported the efficacy of sustained administration of anticoagulants after the implantation of artificial materials such as stent grafts [8]. There is no doubt that continuous combined therapy of aspirin and well-medicated warfarin played a critical role in the prevention of vascular occlusion in this case. On the other hand, limited clinical data prevented any conclusions regarding DOAC efficacy in APS. A cohort study reported that recurrence of arterial thrombosis was not suppressed in patients with APS treated with DOACs [9]. Unfortunately, symptomatic femoral arterial thrombotic events occurred twice in one and a half years after changing the anticoagulant from warfarin to a standard dosage of edoxaban. In my opinion, shifting the anticoagulant was unnecessary as there was no clinical evidence to support the change. The clinical course may indicate that DOAC is unsatisfactory in suppressing systemic thrombosis in patients with APS. Further research is necessary to establish the optimal anticoagulation regime following EVT in patients with APS. This report

highlights the usefulness of EVT for CLTI in patients with APS if it is coupled with continuous combined antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy using warfarin.
