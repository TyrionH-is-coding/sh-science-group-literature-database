# ABSTRACT

The antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), characterized by elevated serum levels of antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL) and thromboembolic complications, is a common cause of acquired hypercoagulability. The plasma protein $\beta 2$ -glycoprotein I ( $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ ) is the most clinically relevant antigenic target for aPL. Recent experimental evidence from our laboratory substantiated the concept that IgG anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ immune complexes containing oxidized LDL (oxLDL) not only facilitated the intracellular accumulation of oxLDL in macrophages but also allowed the presentation of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ epitopes to pathogenic autoreactive T cells. Both mechanisms required FcγRI-mediated uptake by macrophages/monocytes. Furthermore, several clinical studies demonstrated that the presence of circulating oxLDL/ $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ complexes and IgG autoantibodies to these complexes was significantly associated with vascular inflammation (i.e. autoimmune-mediated atherothrombosis) in autoimmune patients. In this article, we review recent findings concerning the biochemical and immunologic mechanisms involved in autoimmune-mediated atherothrombosis in patients with APS.

# 1. Introduction

The antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an autoimmune disorder of acquired hypercoagulability, characterized by high serum levels of antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL) associated with thromboembolic complications of both the venous and

<!-- page 2 -->
arterial blood vessels, or with pregnancy morbidity. Antiphospholipid antibodies, i.e., anticardiolipin antibodies (aCL) and lupus anticoagulants (LA), belong to a heterogeneous family of autoantibodies with reactivity to anionic phospholipids, to phospholipid/protein complexes, and to certain proteins displayed on appropriate surfaces (i.e. activated cell membranes, oxygenated polystyrene, etc.) [1]. However, the plasma protein, $\beta 2$ -glycoprotein I ( $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ ), is the most relevant antigenic target for antiphospholipid antibodies [2]. Antibodies to $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ have been reported as more specific for thrombosis and APS than aCL, and recent prospective clinical studies have shown that aCL antibodies, particularly those that are $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -dependent or those directed to $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ , were important predictors for arterial thrombosis (myocardial infarction and stroke) [3-5].

Oxidized LDL (oxLDL) has been found in atherosclerotic lesions in both humans and experimental animals [6]. OxLDL plays an important pathogenic role in early atherogenic events by stimulating the release of pro-inflammatory chemotactic agents for macrophages and T lymphocytes, by releasing several soluble inflammatory molecules, or by acting as a cytotoxic agent for endothelial cells. In particular, oxLDL is avidly internalized by macrophages of the arterial subendothelial space, which will lead to foam cell formation and development of fatty streaks during early stages of atherogenesis. OxLDL contains neoepitopes in both the lipid and protein moieties, and elevated levels of antibodies against these oxLDL neoepitopes have been detected in patients with angiographically verified coronary heart disease [7,8].

We have studied the interaction among oxLDL, $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ , and anti- $\beta 2$ GPI antibodies in order to clarify their role in the accelerated atherosclerosis observed in patients with SLE and APS [9-12]. $\beta 2$ GPI interacts with oxLDL to form stable oxLDL/ $\beta 2$ GPI complexes via oxidized cholesteryl ester(s) [11]. These oxLDL/ $\beta 2$ GPI complexes are the antigenic targets for anti- $\beta 2$ GPI antibodies in patients with APS [12-14]. Most recently, we reported in three separate papers [15-18] that IgG anti- $\beta 2$ GPI immune complexes containing oxLDL have important FcγRI-dedicated roles, one on antigen presentation and another on lipid accumulation in macrophages/monocytes. In this article, we review recent progress in our understanding of the autoimmune-mediated atherothrombotic mechanisms in APS.

# 2. APS and thromboembolic complications

APS is one of the most common causes of acquired hypercoagulability in the general population [19,20], and is frequently diagnosed in the context of a systemic autoimmune disorder such as SLE (secondary APS). Primary APS may occur in the absence of an obvious underlying disease [21]. APS is characterized by the presence of elevated serum levels of aPL with thromboembolic events of both the venous and arterial vasculature, or with pregnancy morbidity (miscarriages and fetal loss).

APL are members of a heterogeneous family of immunoglobulins that vary in specificity, isotype, subclass, titer, and mechanism of action. Most of these antibodies do not recognize phospholipids but instead recognize phospholipid-binding plasma proteins such as $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ or prothrombin. $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ is now identified as the most clinically relevant

antigenic target for aPL [2,22]. aPL from individuals with APS or other autoimmune diseases recognized $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ while aCL from patients with various infectious diseases seem to bind CL directly [23,24] and these patients rarely develop thromboembolic complications. Autoimmune aCL from patients with SLE and APS require $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ (as cofactor) to bind CL, an event frequently referred to as "β2GPI-dependent aCL" that is associated with vascular thrombosis. $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -dependent aCL are detected by ELISA using solid phase CL, in the presence of purified exogenous $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ . $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -dependent aCL also recognize $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ immobilized on oxygenated microtiter plates but not $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ immobilized on plain polystyrene plates. These findings suggested that $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -dependent aCL recognize an altered conformation of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ when bound to negatively charged phospholipids [1,24].

The origin of these autoimmune antibodies is largely unknown at present, however, some SLE patients seem to have an impaired ability to clear apoptotic material from tissues that could cause a breakdown of central and peripheral mechanism of tolerance against self-antigens [25]. $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -reactive $\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ T cells have been identified in the peripheral blood of patients with APS [26]. These $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ reactive T cells possess helper activity that induce the production of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -dependent aCL. Interestingly, these T cells react with $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ bound to phosphatidylserine liposomes, but not with $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ in its native form [15].

APL have also been associated with several forms of atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases such as myocardial infarction, stroke, and carotid stenosis [3]. Anti- $\beta 2$ GPI antibodies are the most specific for thrombosis and APS [27], and recent prospective studies have shown that $\beta 2$ GPI-dependent aCL or anti- $\beta 2$ GPI antibodies are strong predictors of myocardial infarction and stroke in humans [4]. Some aCL cross-react with oxLDL, suggesting that aPL may also participate in atherosclerosis. In addition, $\beta 2$ GPI, oxLDL and immunoglobulins have been localized in atherosclerotic lesions along with immunoreactive $\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ lymphocytes [28]. These findings suggested that $\beta 2$ GPI, oxLDL and anti- $\beta 2$ GPI antibodies play a pathogenic role in the development of thrombosis, particularly in arterial thrombosis ("atherothrombosis") in SLE and APS patients [29,30].

About $25\%$ of APS patients presented with arterial thrombosis (myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular accident, angina, etc.) and over $31\%$ developed both venous and arterial thrombosis during the follow-up [31]. Chronic vascular inflammation and oxidative stress are common in patients with systemic autoimmune diseases. These findings suggested an important interrelationship between inflammation, lipid peroxidation and clotting activation in patients with APS. This also indicates that APS patients are exposed to a strong chronic in vivo oxidative stress, likely resulting in excessive oxLDL formation.

Chemically modified LDL has been extensively used to study atherogenic mechanisms. This process leads to an oxidized LDL structure that shares many functional properties with the LDL oxidized by cells or to oxLDL extracted from arterial atherosclerotic plaques. There is general consensus that $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$ -oxidized LDL is a relevant autoantigen because the oxLDL found in atheromatous lesions and the oxLDL extracted from atherosclerotic lesions exhibited similar physicochemical and immunological properties to $\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}$ -oxLDL [6]. The precise pathways and reactive intermediates involved in in vivo oxidative modification remains to be elucidated, however, the leukocyte protein myeloperoxidase, may be one candidate for in vivo lipoprotein and lipid oxidation.

$\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ is a $50\mathrm{kDa}$ single-chain polypeptide composed of 326 amino acid residues, arranged in 5 homologous repeats.

β2GPI's fifth domain contains a patch of positively charged amino acids that likely represents the phospholipid binding region. β2GPI binds strongly to negatively-charged molecules, such as phospholipids, heparin, and certain lipoproteins. This binding may aid the clearance of apoptotic cells from circulation.

We recently studied the specific interaction between $\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}$ - oxLDL and $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ . Two chloroform-extractable lipids (oxLig-1 and oxLig-2) were identified as LDL-derived ligands responsible for the specific interaction between oxLDL and $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ . The chemical structure of the ligands were: 7-ketocholesteryl-9-carboxynonanoate [9-oxo-9-(7-ketocholest-5-en-3 $\beta$ -yloxy) nonanoic acid (IUPAC)] (oxLig-1), and 7-ketocholesteryl-12-carboxy (keto) dodecanoate (oxLig-2) (Fig. 1). Cholesteryl linoleate present in LDL is a major core lipid and represents the most probable candidate for a precursor of these ligands. The initial in vitro interaction of $\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}$ -oxLDL with $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ is due to electrostatic interactions between $\omega$ carboxyl functions and lysine residues of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ . Lipid peroxidation and oxLDL production are common in patients with some systemic autoimmune diseases, as well as the development of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [34,35] (Fig. 2). OxLDL, not native LDL, binds $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ in vitro initially forming dissociable electrostatic complexes, followed by more stable complexes bound by covalent interactions. High serum levels of stable oxLDL/ $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ complexes have been detected in most patients with SLE and systemic sclerosis (SSc) [5,36], as well as in patients with acute coronary syndrome, i.e., acute myocardial infarction (AMI) and unstable angina (UA). Patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) showed a slight increase of oxLDL/ $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ complexes compared to healthy controls. Unlike RA, both SLE and SSc are characterized by widespread vascular abnormalities. Serum levels of oxLDL/ $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ complexes were also significantly elevated in patients with secondary APS and in SLE patients without APS compared to healthy controls. However, these complexes were not associated with SLE disease activity or any major clinical manifestation of APS [5]. Although it can be hypothesized that this interaction might be related to chronic vascular inflammation that occurs in autoimmune patients, the exact in vivo mechanism(s) that oxidize LDL and forms oxLDL/ $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ complexes are not fully understood. It is possible that the interaction between $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ and oxLDL minimizes the inflammatory properties of oxLDL while promoting its clearance from circulation. In addition, the binding of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ to oxLDL is likely to occur inside the arterial wall as the intima microenvironment is conducive to further inflammation, oxidation, cell activation and macrophage uptake of oxLDL/ $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ complexes. Serum levels of oxLDL/ $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ complexes fluctuated widely when measured in samples obtained at different time intervals over a 12-

month follow-up from 6 SLE patients [5]. This suggests that oxidation and formation of complexes are very active processes under unknown regulatory mechanism(s). Stable oxLDL/β2GPI complexes may be clinically relevant, as they have been implicated as atherogenic autoantigens, and their presence may represent a risk factor or an indirect but significant contributor to thrombosis and atherosclerosis in patients with an autoimmune background.

# 4. New aspects of oxLDL/β2GPI complexes in the development of the APS

$\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ was preferentially taken up by macrophages when complexed with oxLDL or with phosphatidylserine containing vesicles via scavenger receptors. Once in the cytoplasm, $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ is finally transported into lysosomes (Fig. 3). As described above, $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ is a major autoantigen for antiphospholipid antibodies [1,23,24]. Our recent data show that binding of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ to oxLDL or to liposomes containing anionic phospholipids may facilitate the presentation of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ epitopes by macrophages/dendritic cells to autoreactive T cells [15]. In addition, the formation of IgG immune complexes enhanced the activation of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -specific T cells [16]. Thus, circulating IgG anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ antibodies facilitate the antigen presentation by macrophages to specific T cells.

Interestingly, in vitro stimulation of macrophages by oxLDL/β2GPI complexes together with anti-β2GPI antibodies led to the expression of membrane CD36 as well as FcγRI [17]. These observations suggest that IgG immune complexes containing oxLDL/β2GPI provide not only FcγRI- but also scavenger receptor-mediated uptake of oxLDL/β2GPI complexes by macrophages. Thus, oxLDL/β2GPI complexes represent a major atherogenic autoantigen, and IgG anti-β2GPI autoantibodies may facilitate antigen presentation and foam cell formation in APS.

# 5. Conclusion

Progress toward unraveling the role of newer elements and mechanisms that contribute to the initiation and progression of atherosclerotic/thromboembolic disease in

patients with antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), will move us closer to more effective diagnostic tools, therapeutic and preventive measures aimed to reduce, and optimistically, to eliminate the burden of vascular inflammation (atherothrombosis) in autoimmune disease. These new scientific developments will undoubtedly have significant impact in clinical fields outside autoimmunity.

# Take-home messages

- oxLDL interacts with $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ in atherosclerotic plaques and the complexes formed are released into the circulation. These complexes (oxLDL/ $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ ) are novel markers for predicting and monitoring atherothrombosis in APS.   
- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ can be preferentially bound/uptaken by macrophages as oxLDL complexes. IgG immune complexes containing oxLDL are transferred into macrophage lysosomes. The uptake of free $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ by macrophages is limited and extremely small amounts of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ can be occasionally stagnated in late endosomes, most probably due to its affinity to lysobisphosphatidic acid (LBPA), a negatively charged phospholipid and organelle marker of the late endosome.   
- In APS patients, IgG immune complexes containing oxLDL/ $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ facilitate autoantibody production through antigen presentation by macrophages/monocytes to $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ specific $\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ T and B cells.

# AECA and central nervous system in Behçet's disease

Anti-endothelial cells antibodies (AECA) have been described in several autoimmune diseases such as Wegener's granulomatosis, systemic sclerosis, systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, and Behcet's disease. However, there is no study that has assessed the clinical associations of AECA in this later disease. In an interesting paper, Souza et al. (Clinics 2007;62:685-90) investigated 50 Behcet's disease patients and found $38\%$ with AECA. Importantly, the authors found a positive association of AECA and a previous history of central nervous system involvement. This study was able to show that patients with Behcet's disease present anti-endothelial cells antibodies and these antibodies may be related toneurological findings.

# Autoantibodies and eating disorders

Antibodies directed to melanocortin peptides have been recently described. In a recent article, Fetissov et al. (Nutrition 2008;24:854-9) have reviewed the role of these antibodies in the context of altered appetite and eating disorders in human beings and rats. The authors found that autoantibodies against alpha-melanocyte-stimulating hormone, neuropeptide Y, agouti-related protein, ghrelin, leptin and other peptides are present in sera of healthy humans and rats. Furthermore, when these antibodies are injected to animal, they seem to be related to the regulation of feeding and anxiety. In conclusion, autoantibodies to appetite-regulating peptides may have a role in the regulation of their eating behavior.
