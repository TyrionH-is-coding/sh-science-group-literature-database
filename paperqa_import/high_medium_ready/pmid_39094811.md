# ABSTRACT

Objectives: This review aims to identify biological markers associated with the risk of recurrence of thrombotic and/or obstetric events in patients with antiphospholipid syndrome (APS).

Methods: A comprehensive review of literature was conducted to evaluate established and potential novel biological markers associated with thrombosis in APS. To this end, a PubMed literature search was conducted for the last twenty years using the following keywords or their combinations: thrombotic risk, recurrence of thrombosis, risk stratification, severity, predictive value.

Results: Previous studies showed that multiple aPL positivity correlates with an increased risk of thrombosis in APS. Moreover, the analysis of N-glycosylation of antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL) revealed that low levels of IgG sialylation, fucosylation or galactosylation increases the pro-inflammatory activity of aPL, predisposing to thrombosis. In addition, quantification of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) and antibodies directed against NETs (anti-NETs) in serum demonstrates promising prognostic utility in assessing APS severity. Oxidative stress plays a role in the pathogenicity of APS and paraoxonase 1 (PON1) activity emerges as a promising biomarker of thrombotic risk in APS. Furthermore, identification of novel antigenic targets involved in the pathophysiology of APS, such as lysobisphosphatidic acid (LBPA), had led to the discovery of unconventional aPL, antibodies directed against the LBPA (aLBPA), whose clinical value could make it possible to identify APS patients at high risk of thrombotic recurrence.

Conclusion: The immunological profile of aPL, N-glycosylation of aPL, quantification of NETs and anti-NETs, analysis of biomarkers of oxidative stress and the discovery of aLBPA offer potential prognostic tools for risk stratification in APS patients.

# 1. Introduction

The antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an acquired thrombophilia, causing any types of thrombosis. The reference treatment is based on curative anticoagulation, usually by Warfarin, a vitamin K antagonist [1]. Management of cardiovascular risk factors is imperative [2,3]. Despite significant progress in understanding its pathogenesis, the evolution of APS remains difficult to predict and recurrences constitute a leading cause of death of this disease [4]. That's why perspectives are based on a better definition of the high-risk antiphospholipid antibody profile and the identification of the biological factors that increase the risk of thrombosis. This literature review focuses on the most recent data regarding the biological factors involved in the risk of thrombotic

recurrence in APS.

# 2. Antiphospholipid syndrom

# 2.1. Classification criteria for the antiphospholipid syndrome

The most common clinical manifestations of APS is the occurrence of arterial, venous, and/or obstetric thrombotic events associated with the persistence, at least twelve weeks apart, of one or more conventional antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL) among lupus anticoagulant (LA), IgG and/or IgM anti-cardiolipin antibodies (aCL) and anti- $\beta 2$ -glycoprotein-I antibodies (aB2GP1).

The criteria for this auto-immune disease were defined in the

<!-- page 3 -->
Sapporo Conference in 1999 [5] and were updated in 2006 during a second consensus conference in Sydney [6]. This classification includes the presence of at least both one clinical and one laboratory criteria.

In 2023, an international group of experts supported by the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) and the European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) proposed new classification criteria [7]. These new classification criteria comprise several stages with weighted clinical and biological criteria. The first stage comprises an entry criterion. There must be at least one positive aPL test within three years associated with at least one clinical criterion among the following clinical criteria: macrovascular with venous thrombosis, macrovascular with arterial thrombosis, microvascular, obstetrical field, valvulopathy and haematology. The second stage includes clinical criteria and points are awarded according to existing clinical criteria. If a clinical sign is associated with another diagnosis more likely than APS, it will not be considered. If several clinical signs are present, the criterion with the most points will be considered. The third stage involves biological criteria. The points are awarded on the basis of the patient's biological profile (according to the type of aPL and the IgG or IgM isotype), with highest scoring for the IgG isotype and high levels of aPL. Finally, patients who accumulate at least 3 points in each of the clinical and biological domains are classified as having APS.

# 2.2. Pathophysiology

aPL primarily target phospholipid-binding proteins such as beta-2 glycoprotein I ( $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ ), altering its conformation and enhancing its binding to cell surfaces like endothelial cells, platelets, and monocytes [8]. These aPL- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ complexes interact with receptors on endothelial cells (annexin A2 and TLR4), triggering signaling pathways that activate endothelial cells and increase the expression of adhesion molecules and pro-inflammatory cytokines, promoting a prothrombotic state by enhancing leukocyte adhesion and activation. Additionally, aPL can directly activate platelets through binding to platelet receptors (GPI $\alpha$ and apolipoprotein E receptor 2), leading to platelet aggregation and the release of prothrombotic mediators such as thromboxane A2. In monocytes, aPL binding enhances tissue factor (TF) expression via receptors like TLR4, contributing to coagulation cascade initiation and proinflammatory cytokine (IL1, IL6, TNF alpha) release. Furthermore, aPL activate the complement system, particularly the classical pathway, leading to the generation of components (C5a) that promote inflammation and thrombosis. They also induce the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which are pro-thrombotic by providing a scaffold for platelet adhesion and activation, and contain TF to further enhance coagulation. Lastly, aPL disrupt the protective annexin A5 shields on cell membranes, exposing phospholipids to the coagulation cascade and promoting thrombosis [9]. Collectively, these mechanisms lead to the hypercoagulable state observed in antiphospholipid syndrome. However, the presence of aPL alone is not sufficient to cause thrombosis. According to the 'double hit' theory [10], thrombosis requires a 'second hit' to develop. Common triggering events, that increase the risk of clotting, include infections, neoplasms, inflammatory reactions, use of prothrombotic agents (such as tobacco or estrogen-progestin medications), genetic predispositions (thrombophilia), and situations like surgery or prolonged immobility [11].

# 2.3. Complications

The recurrence rate of thrombosis for patients with untreated APS is high, between 19 and $29\%$ per year [12]. In addition, an observational multicenter study of 177 patients with thrombotic APS with a median follow-up of 5 years showed that the recurrence rate of thrombosis was $7.5\%$ per year in the first five years after the first event, despite an appropriate anticoagulant treatment [13].

In APS, recurrence is the re-emergence of a clinical event in patients who already had a thrombotic or obstetric event. It may be related not

only to an imbalance in the anticoagulant treatment (discontinuation of treatment, under-dosing of the anticoagulant, drug interactions) but also to the presence of severity factors, whose some were elucidated and will be discussed in this review. These factors could allow a risk stratification of thrombotic recurrence by clinicians. Therefore, the identification of patients with a high thrombotic recurrence risk remains a major challenge in the management of the APS.

# 3. Evaluation of biological factors for high risk of thrombotic recurrence in patients with APS

# 3.1. Immunological profile

Numerous epidemiological studies demonstrated that the target, number of positive aPL, and titer of these autoantibodies are critical in risk stratification. That's why, the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) published in 2019 a thrombotic risk stratification for patients with aPL according to two levels represented in Table 1 [14]. Positivity for LA, triple positivity (TP) of aPL and the persistent positivity of medium to high aCL concentration are associated with an increased thrombotic risk [15].

Detection of aPL involves different methods including enzyme-like immunoassay, fluorescent enzyme immunoassay, chemiluminescence immunoassays. The lack of standardization makes difficult the comparison of titers between laboratories. It is therefore difficult to categorize aPL positivity as low, moderate, or high positivity as recommended by the new classification criteria [7]. Patients might be incorrectly classified due to inter-laboratory variations.

# 3.1.1. Target of antiphospholipid antibodies

Interpretation of the aPL profile depends on the antigenic target of theses aPL, a specific epitope, and the isotype of this antibody.

Among the diagnostic tests for APS, LA is a stronger risk factor [16] than aCL and aB2GP1 for thrombosis and obstetric complications [17] (Fig. 1). The LA may be mediated by aB2GP1 and anti-prothrombin autoantibodies (aPT). A meta-analysis of 1650 patients showed that the deep vein thrombosis (DVT) risk is increased $(\mathrm{OR} = 6.14, 95\% \mathrm{CI} 2.74 - 13.8)$ in LA-positive patients compared to LA-negative patients [18]. However, the clinical significance of isolated LA positivity is debated. Several studies have found that isolated LA positivity was not significantly associated with the occurrence of clinical manifestations of APS $(\mathrm{OR} = 1.3; 95\% \mathrm{CI} 0.30 - 6.0)$ compared with LA associated with aPT or aB2GP1 $(\mathrm{OR} = 10.1; 95\% \mathrm{CI} 1.3 - 79.8)$ [19]. Studies by De Laat et al. show that the presence of B2GP1-dependent LA is more strongly associated with a history of thromboembolic complications $(\mathrm{OR} = 42)$ than the presence of independent B2GP1 LA $(\mathrm{OR} = 1.6)$ [20]. In addition, studies have demonstrated that B2GP1 domain I-dependent LA has a greater risk factor for venous or arterial thrombosis and miscarriage than PT-dependent LA [21]. These results suggest that aB2GP1-dependent LA are important risk factors for thrombosis in APS.

# Thrombosis Risk

Fig. 1. Association between thrombosis risk and antibody target.

Regarding isolated aCL positivity, data in literature are controversial. aCL are not as strong risk factors for thrombosis as LA but isotypes and titers of aCL should be taken into account. Indeed, several significant associations with thrombosis risk, especially with the occurrence of stroke, myocardial infarction, or venous thrombosis, have been found for IgG aCL with a high titer, above 33 or 40 GPL units depending on the studies [16]. Schulman et al. showed that when Warfarin was discontinued after six months of treatment for DVT, patients with aCL compared to negative patients had a higher risk of recurrence (29% vs. 14%, $p = 0.0013$ ). Similarly, the 4-year mortality rate was 15% in patients with aCL versus 6% in those without aCL [22]. This suggests that the presence of high aCL titers six months after an episode of venous thromboembolism is predictive of an increased risk of recurrence and death, hence the value of long-term anticoagulation. However, the current debate about the place of aCL is related to the fact that laboratory tests do not distinguish between B2GP1-dependent aCL, with thrombogenic potential, and aCL associated with an infectious context known as B2GP1-independent. Thus, measurement of B2GP1-dependent aCL could enable a better weighting of aCL in the risk stratification of APS.

Concerning aB2GP1 carriers whose pathogenic role has been well demonstrated, Tektonidou et al. designed a 4-year prospective study of 842 patients with a first event suggestive of APS (livedo reticularis, thrombocytopenia, autoimmune hemolytic anemia, thrombosis, recurrent miscarriage). This follow-up showed that the risk of developing a second event was largest in patients with aB2GP1 and who had autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) as the first event $(\mathrm{HR} = 2.70, p = 0.018)$ [23]. For the same study, the Kaplan-Meier curves suggest that a second event occurred earlier in patients with aB2GP1 (HR 1.48, $p = 0.14$ ). This is especially true when the first clinical event suggestive of APS was an AIHA (0.93 years), compared to patients with recurrent miscarriages and no aB2GP1 (13,6 years). In addition, a subset of aB2GP1 IgG directed against domain 1 (epitope including Gly40 and Arg43) have lupus anticoagulant activity and strongly associate with thrombosis [24]. A large international, multicenter and retrospective study of 442 patients with positive aB2GP1 reported a $55\%$ prevalence of IgG directed against the first domain of $\beta 2\mathrm{GP1}$ . The presence of these antibodies was associated with an increased risk of thrombosis $(\mathrm{OR} = 3.5; 95\% \mathrm{CI} 2.3-5.4)$ and obstetric complication $(\mathrm{OR} = 2.4; 95\% \mathrm{CI} 1.4-4.3)$ [25].

The isotype of the aPL also seems to play a role, since a patient with IgG aCL and/or aB2GP1 isotype presents a higher risk than a patient with IgM aCL and/or aB2GP1 isotype [26]. In addition, isotype concordance between aCL and aB2GP1 could identify high-risk APS patients [27].

In clinical practice, some patients show a typical APS symptomatology despite a persistent negativity of criteria aPL. For these cases, the concept of 'seronegative APS' (SN-APS) has been proposed and its recognition is important for physicians to prescribe the most appropriate anti-thrombotic treatment and prevent recurrences. To this end, several

autoantibodies have been studied in SNAPS. Among them, the IgA isotype of aCL and aβ2-GPI have been extensively studied [28,29] but despite recommendation of testing in SNAPS by the task force of 2010 international congress [30] this marker is not included in the latest classification criteria for APS [31] nor have been selected as APS diagnosis criteria in the process of elaborating new international criteria of APS [32]. The epitopes they recognize are localized within domains III, IV and V of B2GP1 [33] and their positivity concomitant with LA negativity has been associated with recurrent unexplainable pregnancy losses [34] recurrent embryo implantation failure in women [35] or neurological manifestations compatible with small vessel brain lesions [36]. Among non-criteria aPL, IgG and IgM aPS/PT auto-antibodies have been described as thrombotic risk markers in patients with APS [37]. Another type of non-criteria aPL are those directed against prothrombin alone (aPT) that are more prevalent in venous than in arterial thrombosis and whose elevated levels of IgG isotype are associated with recurrent thrombosis [38]. Anti-phosphatidylethanolamine antibodies (aPE) have also been detected in SNAPS [39,40] where they decrease proliferation of endothelial cells, increase production of reactive oxygen species, and promote the expression of inflammatory molecules by binding PE of early endosomes [41,42].

# 3.1.2. Antiphospholipid antibodies associations

The number of aPL among LA, aCL and aB2GP1 defines triple or double positivity, regardless of the combination of these conventional aPL. Previous studies showed that multiple aPL positivity in symptomatic or asymptomatic APS patients correlates with a significantly increased risk of thrombosis [43] and obstetric complications [44], the highest risk corresponding to triple positivity (TP) (Fig. 2).

Several retrospective and prospective studies demonstrated that TP is more strongly correlated with thrombosis and pregnancy morbidity than the presence of single or double positivity. A large prospective multicenter study of triple-positive APS patients reported a cumulative incidence of thrombosis of $12.2\%$ (95% CI 9.6-14.8), $26.1\%$ (95% CI 22.3-29.9) and $44.2\%$ (95% CI 38.6-49.8) after 1, 5 and 10 years of follow-up respectively [4], demonstrating that triple-positive patients are exposed to the highest risk of recurrence. Regarding obstetric complications, Ruffatti et al. showed that TP in women with APS conferred an OR of 16.2 (95% CI 0.9-292) for late pregnancy loss and an OR of 34.4 (95% CI 3.5-335.1) for subsequent pregnancy loss [45].

In a study reported by Pengo et al., asymptomatic carriers with a TP profile have a higher incidence of thrombotic events, $5.3\%$ per year, classifying them as a high-risk profile [46]. Furthermore, recurrence remains frequent in TP patients despite the use of oral anticoagulants following a first clinical event, and the risk of recurrent thrombosis is even higher in TP patients who are not on oral anticoagulation (OAT) [47].

Actually, triple positivity is understood as a single aB2GP1 antibody that can reproduce positivity in all three conventional aPL tests. This

However, the classification of patients with a TP profile into a high-risk category may be questioned. In a retrospective study conducted in the Immunology Laboratory of the Marseille University Hospital [51], clinical and biological data of TP patients, considered to be at higher risk of clinical events, were compared with single positive (SP) patients expressing only aCL, considered to be at low risk of thrombosis according to the literature. Firstly, this study showed a high prevalence of healthy carriers $(43\%)$ regardless of triple or single aPL positivity, which calls into question the clinical relevance of TP. Furthermore, a so-called very high-risk profile for thrombosis was demonstrated for TP patients carrying a combination of IgG aCL and IgG aB2GP1 with two positive LA tests or a combination of IgG aCL and IgG aB2GP1 with a Rosner index $>27$ . This leads us to look for other factors of severity of APS in order to discriminate by other ways the patients at high risk of thrombotic recurrence.

# 3.1.3. Quantitative scoring systems for risk prediction using aPL profile

Several attempts at establishing quantitative scoring system for assessing the risk of thrombosis or pregnancy loss in APS have been made. Among them, the Global Anti-Phospholipid Syndrome Score (GAPSS) and antiphospholipid score (aPL-S) are the most used in literature. GAPSS is a clinic-biological score that has been proposed in 2013 [52] by combining cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension and hyperlipidemia) and aPL profile including both criteria (aCL, aB2GP1, LA) and non-criteria aPL antibodies (aPS/PT). Because aPS/PT antibodies are not included in the APS criteria and are not widely available an "adjusted" Global Anti-Phospholipid Syndrome Score (aGAPSS) and "partial" aPL-S without aPS/PT have been proposed. High levels of GAPSS or aGAPSS were associated with high risk of recurrences of clinical manifestations [53-55]. When analyzed based on the site of the recurrence, patients with recurrent arterial, but not venous thrombosis had higher aGAPSS [55]. Additionally, an increase in the GAPSS during the follow-up of SLE patients with aPL was associated with a higher risk of vascular events (relative risk 12.30; $95\%$ CI 1.43-106.13; $\mathrm{p} = 0.004$ ).

The antiphospholipid score (aPL-S) only computing biological data (LA, aCL, aB2GP1 and aPS/PT results) has been defined in 2012 [55]. During the follow-up, aPL-S and partial aPL-S were significantly higher in patients with recurrence of thrombosis than among those without new thrombotic events (median score 5.5 versus 0; $P = 0.012$ ). Both scores have shown a degree of accuracy at identifying high-risk APS patients, especially those at a high risk of thrombosis. However, before using these instruments clinicians should be aware that standardization of the aPL tests is still an issue [56].

# 3.2. Glycosylation of antiphospholipid antibodies

In line with the "double hit" theory, it's possible that other factors are involved in the development of thrombosis. Since immunoglobulins are glycoproteins, the pathogenicity of APS may depend on the glycosylation of the fragment crystallizable region (Fc) of aPL. This posttranslational modification, which takes place in the Golgi apparatus, may indeed influence the effector functions of IgG. Even though the changes in the glycosylation profile affect all classes of Ig, the CH2 domain in the Fc region of IgG remains the best characterized glycosylation site [57].

# 3.2.1. Impact of glycosylation on antibody effector functions

Glycosylation of IgG plays a major role in its effector functions mediated by IgG Fc-gamma receptors (FcγR). These receptors represent a family of membrane glycoproteins widely expressed on cells of the innate immune system (mast cells, basophils, neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes and macrophages) and thus provide a link between the humoral immunity of the adaptive immune system and the effector functions triggered by cells of the innate immunity. FcγR are distinguished by their affinity for IgG subclasses and by their transmission of activating (IA, IIA, IIIA receptors) or inhibitory (IIB receptors) signals [58]. Among the effector functions of IgG, FcγR are essential in phagocytosis, degranulation, proinflammatory mediators releasing and in antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC). Numerous studies showed that the N-glycan present on the asparagine nitrogen at position 297 (N297) in the heavy chain of the CH2 constant domain of IgG is crucial for its in vivo interaction with FcγR [58]. Structurally, this N-glycan is composed of a constant biantennary core heptasaccharide comprising $N$ -acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) and mannose residues with two linked arms in $\alpha1,6$ and $\alpha1,3$ , as illustrated in Figure 4 [59]. Arborisations of $\beta1,4N$ -acetyl-glucosamine (bisecting at position 4 of the mannose of the core structure), galactose (galactosylation), fucose (fucosylation) or even sialic acid (sialylation) are added in a variable manner, depending on age and clinical context. On average, total serum IgG N-glycans are composed of high levels of fucose $(95\%)$ , intermediate levels of galactose $(45\%)$ , low levels of bisecting $N$ -acetylglucosamine $(15\%)$ , attached to the primary mannose) and low levels of sialylation $(10\%)$ on the antennae [60]. Heterogeneity of the N-glycan carbohydrate units thereby alters the spatial conformation of IgG, its stability and affinity, and directly influences its binding to FcγR and the complement component C1q [61]. That's why, the IgG-attached sugar moiety is one of the most promising targets to modulating either the pro- or the anti-inflammatory activities of IgG.

Among these IgG glycovariants, some of them increase the pro-inflammatory activity while others are anti-inflammatory, as illustrated in Figure 3 [57]. The complete absence of IgG glycosylation leads to a conformational state that prevents binding to the FcγR and complement, which explains the inability of deglycosylated IgG to mediate inflammatory responses in vivo [62]. Besides, several studies suggest that the presence or absence of some sugar residues on IgG modulates their binding affinity with FcγR and thus the inflammation extent. Specifically, the core fucose in Fc N-glycans creates steric hindrance for the FcγRIIII, so afucosylated IgG has a higher affinity for activating FcγRIIIA in humans [63,64]. Furthermore, Malhotra et al. [65] suggested that the absence of terminal galactoses (IgG-G0 glycoforms) makes N-acetyl-glucosamine residues more accessible for binding to Mannose Binding Lectins (MBL), which increases pro-inflammatory activity through complement activation via the lectin pathway. Another biologically important modification of N-glycan is sialylation. Several studies highlighted that defective IgG sialylation directly increases ADCC by enhancing affinity for FcγRIIIA [66], thereby maintaining a pro-inflammatory state. Similarly, Kaneko et al. [62] showed that the presence of sialic acid is responsible for the anti-inflammatory activity of intravenous Ig in mice by reducing the affinity of IgG for FcγRIIII. # 3.2.2. Pathogenicity of antibody glycosylation in APS

It was as early as 1985 that Parekh et al. suggested that N-glycans are differentially expressed on serum IgG from patients with rheumatoid arthritis compared to healthy individuals and subjects with primary osteoarthritis. The CH2 domain of IgG is the most described glycosylation site in the literature to date. Indeed, Albert et al. [67] showed in autoimmune mouse models that in vivo cleavage of N-glycans, attached to the N297 residue on Fc, leads to the loss of the proinflammatory activity. This explains that N-glycosylation of autoantibodies play an important role in their interaction with FcγR of innate immune cells, which is responsible for the tissue damage observed in autoimmune diseases. Among the various IgG glycoforms, those enhancing proinflammatory activity are predominantly found in the serum of patients with autoimmune disease (AID). Among them, G0 glycans (without galactose residues) have a higher affinity for FcγRIII and are the most associated with AID [68]. Increased levels of G0 glycans also correlate with disease activity [69]. Similarly, IgG sialylation and fucosylation are often decreased in AID since hyposialylated and afucosylated IgG promotes a proinflammatory state by increasing IgG affinity for FcγR.

In APS, several works assert the role of IgG glycosylation in the pathogenic effects caused by aPL. Fickentscher et al. [70] demonstrated that the pathogenic factor of aB2GP1 is related to the sialylation of this autoantibody. For this purpose, they analyzed the sialylation of aB2GP1 by measuring the specific binding of Sambucus nigra lectin (SNA) to the sialic acid of aB2GP1. Calculation of the SNA/IgG ratio allowed comparison of aB2GP1 sialylation levels between sera from patients with symptomatic APS and asymptomatic subjects with aPL. A significantly higher sialylation is observed on aB2GP1 IgG isolated from the sera of healthy children and asymptomatic adults with aPL compared with that from symptomatic APS patients. Furthermore, sialylated aB2GP1 IgG is unable to bind to platelets and activate them via FcγRIIA. Thereby, hypersialylation of IgG aB2GP1 leads to low pathogenicity of these aPL and would prevent the development of clinical manifestations of APS. Conversely, hyposialylation of IgG would promote inflammation and precipitate thrombus formation in APS.

In addition, Alvarez et al. [71] recently noted that the amount of $\beta 1,4$ $N\cdot$ -acetylglucosamine galactose is significantly decreased on IgG from women with seronegative obstetric APS, carrying unconventional

aPL, compared with a control group of healthy women. Therefore, the obstetric manifestations of APS could be related to the specific glycan profile of unconventional aPL. However, the study of $\alpha 2,6$ sialylation showed no significant difference between the groups.

Moreover, a recent study observed that low galactosylation of all IgG aB2GP1 subclasses, an increased bisection and fucosylation of IgG1 and IgG2 aB2GP1 were strongly associated with catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (CAPS) and triple positivity of aPL [72].

Studies have focused on Fc glycosylation of antiphospholipid antibodies. By analogies with other auto-immune diseases [73], the glycosylation in the F(ab) region might affect autoantibodies interactions with their targets (such as $\beta 2$ -glycoprotein I) or might modulate their effects on coagulation processes.

To conclude, analysis of aPL N-glycosylation could be a biomarker associated with risk of thrombosis or obstetric complication. However, only a few studies have focused on aPL glycosylation. At this stage, it is therefore too preliminary to link aPL glycosylation to the risk of adverse events and to use the glycosylation of aPL in routine practice.

# 3.3. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) in the APS

# 3.3.1. Involvement of NETs in the immunothrombosis process

3.3.1.1. The role of neutrophil extracellular traps. The production of Neutrophil Extracellular Traps (NETs) is a cellular mechanism following the activation of neutrophils which results in the release into the extracellular space of a mesh of DNA (Deoxyribonucleic acid), histones and bactericidal proteins from their granulations such as elastase (NE), myeloperoxidase (MPO) or proteinase 3 (PR3). This NETosis plays a role in anti-infectious immunity by destroying extracellular pathogens through the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) by NADPH (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate) oxidase and antimicrobial peptides. However, NETs also contribute to the processes of inflammation and immunothrombosis, involving an innate immune response that causes vascular thrombosis, found in cancer, COVID-19[74] as well as APS. Indeed, aPL have the ability to bind to the surface of neutrophils and trigger the release of NETs, which have recently been incriminated in endothelial dysfunction, atherosclerotic plaque formation, and thrombosis occurrence [75]. Specifically, histones trigger platelet activation and aggregation, negatively charged extracellular DNA activates the intrinsic coagulation pathway via factor XII, and proteases inactivate some anticoagulant factors. In addition, NETs can be an important source of tissue factor (TF), generating thrombin through the extrinsic pathway of coagulation, and conferring resistance to fibrinolysis on the clot [76]. In serum, NETs are degraded by serum deoxyribonuclease I (DNase-I) [77].

3.3.1.2. NETs involvement in APS. In the case of APS, neutrophils show an activated phenotype compared to healthy control neutrophils, resulting in increased NETosis and an impaired clearance of NETs, which are involved in the pathophysiology of thrombosis. In addition, NETs are a source of autoantigens, which can be the target of autoantibodies directed against NETs, found in APS.

Indeed, Yalavarthi et al. [78] found that NETs, as measured by concentrations of MPO-DNA complexes, and cell-free DNA circulate at elevated levels in the plasma and serum of patients with primary APS compared with healthy volunteers. Similarly, neutrophils from patients with APS are more prone to NETosis than neutrophils from healthy volunteers. This study also demonstrated that plasmas from patients with APS stimulate neutrophils from healthy controls to release NETs and that NETs subsequently promote thrombin generation. In the same way, Meng et al. [79] demonstrated the in vivo role for NETs in aPL-mediated venous thrombosis. Indeed, thrombi from APS patients showed significant enrichment of citrullinated histone H3, quantified by western blot, compared with controls. To resume, this work reveals that

Lu et al. In addition, IgG aPL significantly stimulates the release of NETs by neutrophils from healthy subjects compared with IgG isolated from healthy controls. This study also provides evidence that IgG aPL accelerates NETosis through NADPH oxidase-mediated ROS production in a time-dependent manner and similarly to that of PMA (a known stimulus of NETosis). Finally, this study highlighted the detrimental effects of aPL IgG-induced NETs on trophoblast invasion and migration, which are essential for a normal placentation, as well as on the migration and tube-forming capacity of HUVECs (Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cells). Thus, NETs measurement by the quantification of extracellular DNA or MPO-DNA complexes in serum could serve as a predictive marker of thrombosis in obstetric APS.

# 3.3.2. Interest of NETs and anti-NETs autoantibodies in the assessment of the severity of APS

The association of NETs with disease severity was shown in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [81]. Indeed, plasma NETs levels identified patients with a severe disease phenotype, characterized by immune complex-induced nephritis. Furthermore, NETs levels were significantly associated with a future increase in SLE activity within 3 months $(\mathrm{OR} = 1.75; p = 0.01)$ as well as an overall increase in disease activity over one year $(p < 0.01)$ . Thus, NET levels offer significant clinical value in identifying patients at risk for severe disease in SLE. More recently, in COVID-19, several studies have testified that circulating NETs, quantified by assaying extracellular DNA, MPO-DNA complexes, and citrullinated histone H3 (H3Cit) [74] in serum samples, have a prognostic value because they are associated with disease severity [82].

As for APS, several studies have suggested that patients with the primary form have circulating IgG that binds to NETs themselves, apart from aPL that bind to neutrophils. However, the quantification of NETs and their autoantibodies, their function, and their prognostic relevance in APS have not been extensively studied.

# 3.4. Oxidative stress in APS

Oxidative stress is an imbalance between the generation of free radicals or reactive oxygen species (ROS) in mitochondria and cellular antioxidant capacity. ROS are largely involved in intracellular signaling pathways and contribute to maintaining homeostasis. Free radicals are highly reactive molecules that contain oxygen and can damage cells, proteins and DNA [85]. Excessive production of ROS or a decline in antioxidant capacity can contribute to various health problems, including inflammation, aging, neurodegenerative diseases, cardiovascular diseases, and cancer.

# 3.4.1. Oxidative stress and autoimmunity

There is growing evidence suggesting that oxidative stress plays a role in the development and progression of autoimmune diseases. Chronic stress, whether psychological or physiological (such as oxidative stress), can dysregulate the immune system, leading to increased inflammation and potentially triggering autoimmune responses in susceptible individuals. The ROS generated following oxidative stress trigger cell death, tissue damage, the formation of autoantigens and the release of interferon alpha [86]. Therefore, oxidative modifications of self-antigens may render them more immunogenic, leading to increased autoantibody production. Oxidative stress can exacerbate inflammation, which is a hallmark feature of autoimmune diseases leading to a vicious cycle of inflammation and tissue damage characteristic of autoimmune diseases. In APS, oxidative stress may exacerbate inflammation and endothelial damage, further promoting the prothrombotic state characteristic of the condition. Expression of cell surface proteins such as integrins and cell adhesion molecules (CAMs) and therefore the marginalization of immune cells in the organs are modulated by ROS. In particular, the liver plays a vital role in maintaining the body's homeostasis, as it serves both antioxidant and immunomodulatory functions. In a cohort of patients with SLE, Liu et al. [87] showed that liver dysfunction was present in almost $20\%$ of patients, and that it correlated with disease activity. Similarly, Patel et Perl [88] revealed that patients with SLE exhibited significantly elevated concentrations of CAMs than healthy individuals. Moreover, a meta-analysis conducted by Ambrosino et al. [89] indicated a significant association between liver disease and the presence of aPL.

Oxidative stress may directly influence the production of autoantibodies by altering the function of immune cells involved in the autoimmune response. Lai et al. [90] found that oxidative stress is increased in lupus patients with APS while regulatory T cells is depleted (Treg). Oaks et al. [91] demonstrated that mTOR (mammalian target of rapamycin)-dependent mitochondrial dysfunction contributes to aPL

<!-- page 8 -->
synthesis in the liver, preceding the onset of disease and responding to rapamycin treatment in lupus-prone mice. Oxidative stress triggers the activation of mTOR in patients with secondary APS with SLE. Indeed, Ji et al. [92] have elucidated the involvement of mTOR in the pathogenesis of APS by promoting the proliferation of endothelial cells, platelets and monocytes, thereby inducing a procoagulant state that predisposes to thrombosis. The activation of mTOR, reflecting oxidative stress, correlates with disease activity and precedes flare-ups in SLE patients [93,94]. Other oxidative stress-dependent markers have demonstrated a significant association with disease severity in SLE [88,95,96].

The beta-2 glycoprotein-1 ( $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ ), considered as the major auto antigen in APS, contains allosteric disulphide bonds that can exist in two redox states: free thiol or oxidised, oxidised form exposing neoepitopes. Therefore, quantitating the different redox forms of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ in serum may be used to stratify risk of thrombosis [97].

Moreover, a recent study [98] revealed for the first time an association between non-conventional aPLs, anti-phosphatidylethanolamine autoantibodies (aPE) and the production of ROS, suggesting that aPE contribute to the thrombotic process through signaling pathways linked to oxidative stress.

# 3.4.2. PON1, a promising biomarker of thrombosis in APS

Paraoxonase 1 (PON1), synthesized by the liver, is associated with high-density lipoproteins (HDL) in the plasma and protects circulating cells and lipoproteins against oxidative damage, thereby contributing to the antiatherosclerotic processes [99].

Several studies have shown that PON1 deficiency is associated with aPL synthesis [91,100]. Specifically, Delgado Alves et al. [101] demonstrated that the presence of aB2GP1 IgG was associated with a decrease in PON1 activity in patients with primary APS (Fig. 5).

Interestingly, studies have reported a significant decrease in PON1 activity among patients who developed arterial thrombosis in the context of APS [102]. All these results underscore the potential clinical

relevance of PON1 in predicting thrombotic risk in patients with APS. It would be interesting to investigate PON1 or oxidative stress-dependent markers in the context of increased risk of recurrence.

# 3.5. Towards new biomarkers of APS severity: anti-lysobisphosphatidic acid antibodies

# 3.5.1. Lysobisphosphatidic acid

The endothelial protein C receptor (EPCR) recognizing lipids such as lysobisphosphatidic acid (LBPA) was recently identified as a target of aPL, as illustrated in Fig. 6. As shown by Müller-Calleja et al. [103], binding of aPL to LBPA results in the recruitment of acid sphingomyelinase (ASM) to the cell surface of monocytes, followed by internalization of these aPL into late endosomes and dissociation of the FT-TPFI complex (Tissue Factor of Pathway Inhibitor), inducing activation of coagulation. This signaling pathway is responsible for the development of autoimmunity as it triggers TLR7-dependent secretion of interferon $\alpha$ in dendritic cells, which in turn promotes the expansion of aPL-producing B1a cells. Thus, EPCR signaling contributes to thrombotic events as well as to the development of autoimmunity in APS.

In vivo, ProcrC/S mouse models characterized by a mutation in the intracellular cysteine residue of EPCR showed abolition of endosomal trafficking of LBPA and proinflammatory signaling by IFN $\alpha$ in innate immune cells. Indeed, this mutation causes a defect in the ability to exchange phosphatidylcholine, which is structurally bound to EPCR, with LBPA. ProcrC/S mice or mice treated with an antibody blocking EPCR-LBPA are protected against aPL-induced thrombosis and fetal loss. In addition, an experimental model of APS represented by TLR7 $(- / - )$ and ProcrC/S mice with SLE showed that blocking EPCR-LBPA signaling is sufficient to prevent proliferation of B1a cells that cause aPL synthesis and other lupus-associated autoantibodies. These initial data indicate that anti-LBPA antibodies (aLBPA) are biomarkers that may enable a better assessment of thrombotic risk in APS.

# 3.5.2. Anti-lysobisphosphatidic acid autoantibodies

Alessandri et al. [104] showed for the first time that aLBPA levels are significantly higher in patients with APS compared with SLE patients and control subjects. Another study [105] assessing the diagnostic value of aLBPA in patients with primary APS showed a significant prevalence of IgG aLBPA in primary APS compared with a control group, with a sensitivity of $58.6\%$ and specificity of $92.9\%$ . Similarly, IgM aLBPA showed a significant association with the primary APS group of patients compared to the control group, although with a lower sensitivity of $28.6\%$ but a very high specificity of $97.1\%$ . As a result, aLBPA have a specificity for APS similar to aB2GP1 but lower than LA. On the opposite, their sensitivity is better than LA but lower than aCL and aB2GP1, limiting the use of aLBPA as an additional biological diagnostic tool in APS. Regarding the clinical usefulness of aLBPA detection in the prognosis of APS, this remains to be analyzed in large longitudinal studies. Indeed, a close association between LA positivity, constituting a high-risk profile for thrombosis, and aLBPA has been detected $(p = 0.0018)$ [104].

Recently, Hollerbach et al. [106] demonstrated a prevalence of IgG aLBPA in $45\%$ of patients hospitalized with COVID-19, a coagulopathy also associated with the presence of aPL, compared with healthy controls. Furthermore, they demonstrated that aLBPA produced by B1a cells were associated with COVID-19 severity and mortality, suggesting that this aPL is a marker of clinical severity in COVID-19-associated coagulopathy.

Overall, these data reflect that aLBPA represent a biological marker for APS with similar sensitivity and specificity to aB2GP1 and are strongly associated with the presence of LA. However, no studies have been carried out on the clinical value of aLBPA in antiphospholipid syndrome.

# 4. Conclusion

The heterogeneous nature, both clinical and biological, of APS makes its diagnosis difficult. aPL target, titer, isotype and number have much more levels of evidence as biologic markers of high risk of recurrence compared to glycosylation, NETs, oxidative stress and LBPA. Moreover, although most studies showed conventional APS biological profiles associated with a high risk of thrombotic recurrence, such as the presence of a LA, or high aPL titers, or the presence of multiple aPL positivity, not all studies are unanimous. Therefore, other proposals should be studied to routinely identify patients with APS at high risk of thrombosis. Among them, the study of the N-glycosylation of aPL is a promising test since several studies have shown that patients with APS had hyposialylated, hypogalactosylated or hypofucosylated aPLs compared with asymptomatic subjects with aPL. In addition, quantification of NETs and anti-NETs antibodies in serum would offer an additional prognostic value in the severity of APS. Oxidative stress-dependent markers are promising for predicting thrombosis in APS, particularly by measuring PON1 activity. Finally, the identification of new antigenic targets involved in the pathophysiology of APS has led to the discovery of other unconventional aPL, the aLBPA, whose clinical relevance could allow the identification of APS patients at high risk of thrombotic recurrence. Biological markers associated with the risk of recurrence described in this review are summarized in Fig. 7. They could be used as valuable tools for risk stratification of thrombosis and/or obstetric events in patients with APS.
