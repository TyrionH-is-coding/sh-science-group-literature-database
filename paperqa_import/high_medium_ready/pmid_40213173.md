## Abstract

Introduction and importance:: Hemorrhagic cholecystitis (HC) is a rare and serious complication of acute cholecystitis, characterized by intraluminal or extraluminal bleeding within the gallbladder. While commonly associated with gallstones, HC may also arise from coagulopathies, anticoagulant therapy, and autoimmune conditions such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and antiphospholipid syndrome (APS). However, HC in patients with SLE or APS is rarely reported in the literature.

Case presentation:: A 59-year-old female with a history of SLE and APS, on corticosteroids, hydroxychloroquine, methotrexate, and warfarin, presented with acute right upper quadrant pain, vomiting, hematemesis, and melena. Clinical examination revealed right upper quadrant tenderness and positive Murphy’s sign. Imaging studies, including ultrasound and computed tomography, confirmed a distended gallbladder with intraluminal hyperdense content and surrounding inflammation. An urgent open cholecystectomy was performed, revealing clotted blood within the gallbladder. The patient recovered uneventfully and was discharged in stable condition.

Clinical discussion:: HC is an uncommon but critical condition that requires prompt recognition and intervention. In this case, the coexistence of autoimmune diseases and anticoagulant therapy likely predisposed the patient to HC. Imaging played an essential role in diagnosis, and open cholecystectomy provided definitive management.

Conclusion:: HC should be considered in patients with SLE, APS, or coagulopathies presenting with acute abdominal symptoms. Early recognition and multidisciplinary management is essential to prevent life-threatening outcomes.

## Introduction

Hemorrhagic cholecystitis (HC) is a rare but serious complication of acute cholecystitis, characterized by hemorrhage within the gallbladder. It carries a high risk of morbidity and mortality, necessitating early diagnosis and management [ 1 ] . Epidemiological data on HC are scarce due to its rarity, but a recent study reported an incidence rate of 0.55 % [ 2 ] . However, the condition can arise from various underlying causes, including gallstones, which is the most common cause with over 50% of cases, biliary malignancies, trauma, coagulopathies, vascular abnormalities, chronic corticosteroid use, and anticoagulant therapy. Highlights Hemorrhagic cholecystitis is a rare complication of acute cholecystitis with high risk of morbidity and mortality. The association between hemorrhagic cholecystitis and autoimmune diseases, such as systemic lupus erythematosus and antiphospholipid syndrome, is rarely reported and need to be considered as a cause of acute abdominal pain in such patients. Early recognition and management are essential for patients with hemorrhagic cholecystitis.

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) are autoimmune conditions, with a prevalence of approximately 20–70 cases per 100 000 people and 40–50 cases per 100 000 individuals worldwide, respectively. They can manifest in multiple organ systems, including the cardiovascular, respiratory, gastrointestinal tract, liver, and gallbladder [ 3 , 4 ] . However, the coexistence of HC with SLE and APL is rarely reported and investigated, with only a few reported cases of hemorrhagic cholecystitis in patients with SLE or APS [ 4 - 6 ] .

The diagnosis of HC is challenging due to its nonspecific clinical presentation, including fever, acute abdominal pain, biliary obstruction, melena, hematemesis, or hemoperitoneum secondary to gallbladder perforation [ 7 ] . Imaging modalities such as ultrasound (US) and computed tomography (CT) are essential for early diagnosis [ 8 ] . Despite diagnostic challenges, HC is considered a surgical emergency, requiring emergent cholecystectomy as the treatment of choice to prevent life-threatening complications.

Here, we describe a rare case of hemorrhagic cholecystitis in a patient with a history of SLE and APS, who was on anticoagulant, corticosteroid, and immunosuppressant therapy. The patient was successfully managed with an urgent open cholecystectomy.

## Case presentation

A 59-year-old female, a known case of SLE and APS on corticosteroid, hydroxychloroquine, and methotrexate, presented to the emergency department complaining of acute right upper quadrant (RUQ) abdominal pain lasting for 2 days. The pain radiated to the right flank and back and was accompanied with vomiting, hematemesis, and black tarry stool. Her medical history was significant for hypertension, diabetes mellitus, peripheral arterial disease, aortic stenosis, and a history of upper gastrointestinal bleeding secondary to peptic ulcer disease. She had undergone left superficial femoral artery angioplasty 3 months prior, when she started on warfarin.

On examination, the patient’s vital signs revealed hypertension with blood pressure of 160/90, oxygen saturation of 98%, heart rate of 85 beats /minute, and no fever. Abdominal examination showed RUQ tenderness with positive Murphy’s sign. Per-rectal examination confirmed the presence of melena. Cardiovascular examination revealed a systolic murmur at the upper sternal border, consistent with her history of aortic stenosis. The respiratory, neurological, and integumentary systems were unremarkable.

Laboratory findings, shown in Table 1 , revealed abnormalities consistent with her clinical presentation. Given the patient’s signs and symptoms, an abdominal ultrasound was performed, showing an over-distended gallbladder with a thickened wall and heterogeneously hyperechoic intraluminal content without definitive vascularity (Fig. 1 A). Moreover, a rim of free fluid was noted in Morison’s pouch. Further evaluation with abdominal computed tomography (CT) revealed a distended gallbladder surrounded by fat stranding, non-enhancing intraluminal hyperdense contents suggestive of bloody contents and periportal edema, with no evidence of biliary tree dilatation (Fig. 1 B). A multidisciplinary team discussed the findings and recommended an urgent surgical intervention. Under general anesthesia, the patient underwent an open cholecystectomy through a Kocher incision. Intraoperatively, the gallbladder appeared dark in color and over-distended. After opening the gallbladder, it was filled with clotted blood (Fig. 1 C). The gallbladder was carefully dissected from its bed and removed uneventfully. The specimen was sent for histopathological analysis, which later confirmed acute-on-chronic cholecystitis with no evidence of malignancy.

Postoperatively, the patient was admitted to the intensive care unit for close monitoring. She had an uneventful recovery and was discharged in stable condition 5 days after surgery. Her discharge plan included continuation of her baseline medications, a short course of antibiotics, and outpatient follow-up.

## Discussion

HC is a rare and potentially life-threatening complication that requires prompt diagnosis and intervention. While acute cholecystitis is a more common condition in emergency departments (EDs), accounting for 3% to 9% of hospital admissions and 5% of ED visits [ 9 ] . HC is an uncommon variant with distinct pathophysiological and clinical features. A study conducted on 6458 patients who underwent cholecystectomy, 35 patients were identified with HC. The overall incidence of HC is 0.55% with a median age of 51 years, ranging from 36 to 83 years. While 89 % of patients were present as emergent cases with clinical presentation of cholecystitis or biliary colic, 11 % of patients were admitted for elective procedures [ 2 ] .

HC results from severe inflammation, which leads to ischemia and necrosis of the gallbladder wall, leading to subsequent intraluminal or extraluminal bleeding. The pathophysiology of HC involves a transmural inflammatory process that erodes the gallbladder mucosa, resulting in ischemia and breakdown of the mucosal barrier. Therefore, erosion develops in the gallbladder vasculature, leading to intraluminal bleeding, hematoma formation, and in severe cases, perforation or hemoperitoneum.

HC and hemobilia are rare but important to be considered as a cause of upper gastrointestinal bleeding. Although upper GI bleeding is more commonly caused by peptic ulcers and esophageal varices, which was ruled out by endoscopy in our case, HC can present with symptoms such as hematemesis and melena. The exact epidemiology of HC-induced GI bleeding is limited, but awareness of this condition is essential for accurate diagnosis and management.

SLE, a chronic autoimmune disease that can affect multiple organs, is associated with various gastrointestinal complications, including mesenteric arteritis, bowel perforation, and spontaneous peritonitis [ 10 ] . However, HC in the context of SLE has been rarely reported [ 4 - 6 ] . Rhoton et al reported a case of a 22-year-old woman with SLE who developed hemobilia and had high anticardiolipin antibody titers [ 5 ] . Similarly, Bojorge et al describe a case of a 17-year-old female presented with hemorrhagic cholecystitis after two years of SLE diagnosis. Her condition was successfully managed with open cholecystectomy [ 6 ] . In contrast to our case, these two cases have not reported anticoagulant use in their patients. Another reported case by Oshiro et al described a patient known case of SLE and APS, who presented with abdominal pain and melena. Following imaging and endoscopy, she was diagnosed with HC and hemobilia. Unlike our case, the initial management was conservative, with a subsequent elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy. This highlights the importance of using proper treatment strategies to the patient’s clinical presentation and risk factors [ 4 ] . These cases, in aligns with our case, highlight the potential link between autoimmune conditions and HC.

APS, which often coexists with SLE, further complicates the clinical picture. This autoimmune condition is characterized by recurrent thrombosis and elevated antiphospholipid antibodies, including lupus anticoagulants and anticardiolipin antibodies. APS is associated with a wide range of digestive manifestations, such as Budd-Chiari syndrome, hepatic infarction, portal hypertension, and intestinal infarction. However, thrombotic events in APS may lead to gallbladder ischemia, predisposing these patients to HC.

Our case describes a female patient with SLE and APS who was admitted with severe right upper quadrant abdominal pain and. Similarly, most HC cases are also presented with symptoms of abdominal pain, vomiting, and nausea. Diagnostic imaging modalities, such as abdominal ultrasound and CT scan, were useful to detect the hematoma inside the gallbladder and signs of inflammation. As in our case, abdominal ultrasound typically reveals gallbladder distension and heterogeneous echogenic material without acoustic shadows.

Coagulopathy is a significant inducing factor for HC, especially in patients on anticoagulant therapy or those with bleeding diathesis [ 11 , 12 ] . Anticoagulant therapy, such as warfarin, has been implicated in multiple cases of HC (Table 2 ). Although a definitive causal relationship between anticoagulation and hemorrhagic cholecystitis is not established, the ischemia and mucosal erosion associated with HC provide a plausible mechanism for intraluminal bleeding. This can lead to complications such as hematemesis, jaundice, and hemobilia. However, the relation between HC and anticoagulant therapy in coexisting of SLE and APS is rarely reported in the literature, with only one previous reported case [ 4 ] . The pathophysiology of HC in the context of autoimmune conditions like SLE and APS involves several potential mechanisms. In SLE, immune complexes and autoantibodies can cause vasculitis and inflammation of the gallbladder wall, leading to hemorrhage within the gallbladder. APS is characterized by hypercoagulability, resulting in thrombotic events within the gallbladder vasculature, further contributing to hemorrhage and necrosis. These autoimmune processes create a predisposition to HC, highlighting the importance of considering underlying autoimmune conditions in patients presenting with HC.

Management of HC requires a multidisciplinary approach, considering the patient’s comorbidities and hemodynamic stability. In this case, surgical intervention was warranted due to the patient’s high-risk profile, including SLE, APS, and anticoagulant use. Open cholecystectomy is preferred in complex cases to allow thorough exploration and optimal bleeding control. While laparoscopic cholecystectomy is preferred in stable patients, open surgery remains the standard for complex or unstable cases. Additional therapeutic options include cholecystostomy or endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) in cases involving biliary leakage [ 13 ] .

Given the patient’s use of anticoagulants and immunosuppressive therapy, perioperative management focused on reversing anticoagulation to minimize the risk of intraoperative bleeding, strict aseptic techniques were employed during the surgical procedure to reduce the risk of infection, considering the patient’s immunosuppressed status, and careful attention was given to achieving optimal hemostasis during surgery to address the increased bleeding risk associated with anticoagulation and APS. Moreover, postoperative management involved close monitoring in the intensive care unit to ensure hemodynamic stability and prevent complications. Anticoagulation therapy was gradually reintroduced under close INR monitoring. Additionally, patient education included guidance on recognizing symptoms of recurrent abdominal pain, bleeding, or infection, with an emphasis on the importance of regular follow-ups to manage underlying autoimmune conditions, adjust treatment regimens, and monitor for long-term complications.

## Conclusion

HC should be considered in patients with SLE, APS, or other conditions predisposing to bleeding diathesis, especially those presenting with biliary colic, hematemesis, or jaundice. Early recognition and prompt intervention is very important to reduce mortality in this life-threatening condition. This case confirms the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in managing patients with complex autoimmune and thrombotic disorders, highlight the role of imaging in diagnosing HC and guiding timely surgical intervention.
