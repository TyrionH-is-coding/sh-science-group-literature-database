# Concise report

# Clinical value of amyloid-beta1-40 as a marker of thrombo-inflammation in antiphospholipid syndrome

Maria G. Tektonidou $①$ , Evrydiki Kravvariti $①$ , Nikolaos I. Vlachogiannis $①$ , Georgios Georgiopoulos $3$ , Aimilia Mantzou $3$ , Petros P. Sfikakis $①$ , Konstantinos Stellos $^{2,4,\star}$ and Kimon Stamatelopoulos $^{2,3,\star}$

# Abstract

Objective. Amyloid-beta1-40 (A $\beta$ 40) is a pro-inflammatory peptide under investigation as a novel biomarker of vascular inflammation, endothelial dysfunction and atherothrombosis in the general population. Herein we tested the hypothesis that A $\beta$ 40 is deregulated in APS, a systemic autoimmune disease characterized by a thrombo-inflammatory state.

Methods. Between January 2016 and July 2017, we consecutively recruited 80 regularly followed thrombotic APS patients (44 primary, 36 SLE/APS) and 80 age- and sex-matched controls. Plasma $\mathsf{A}\beta 40$ levels were measured using ELISA and APS-related clinical and laboratory characteristics were recorded. The adjusted Global Anti-Phospholipid Syndrome Score (aGAPSS), a validated risk score in APS, was calculated as a comparator to $\mathsf{A}\beta 40$ performance to detect arterial thrombotic APS-related events.

Results. Higher $\mathsf{A}\beta 40$ levels were significantly associated with the presence of APS [odds ratio (OR) 1.024 per $1\mathrm{pg}/$ ml (95% CI 1.007, 1.041)] after adjustment for cardiovascular risk factors (CVRFs), including smoking, arterial hypertension, dyslipidaemia and BMI, and for estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Among APS patients, increased high-sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP) serum levels was the only independent determinant of $\mathsf{A}\beta 40$ levels. Importantly, $\mathsf{A}\beta 40$ levels above the optimal receiver operating characteristics (ROC)-derived cut-off value were independently associated with recurrent arterial events [OR 4.93 (95% CI 1.31, 18.51)] after adjustment for age, sex, CVRFs, hs-CRP and high anti- $\beta 2$ glycoprotein I IgG titres. Finally, by ROC curve analysis, $\mathsf{A}\beta 40$ provided incremental additive value over the aGAPSS by significantly improving its discrimination ability for recurrent arterial thromboses.

Conclusion. In APS, Aβ40 plasma levels are elevated and associated with an adverse thrombo-inflammatory profile. The pathophysiological and prognostic role of Aβ40 in APS merits further investigation.

Key words: antiphospholipid syndrome, amyloid beta, arterial thrombosis, vascular inflammation, cardiovascular disease

# Rheumatology key messages

- Amyloid-beta1-40 (A $\beta$ 40) was significantly associated with the presence of APS, adjusting for smoking, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, BMI and eGFR.   
- A $\beta$ 40 was independently associated with recurrent arterial thromboses and hs-CRP in APS.   
- Aβ40 provided incremental discriminative value over the aGAPSS score to detect recurrent arterial thrombosis in APS.

# Introduction

APS is a systemic autoimmune disorder with often devastating outcomes, characterized by arterial or venous thrombosis and/or pregnancy morbidity in association with persistently positive aPL antibodies [1, 2]. Both primary APS (PAPS) and APS associated with SLE (SLE-APS) have increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality risk [3], rising as high as $40\%$ in the rare catastrophic form of APS [4]. Their gravest complications involve thrombosis in the arterial vasculature, such as myocardial infarction and stroke [3]. However, the pathophysiology of the entire spectrum of macro- and microvascular manifestations of APS is not completely understood, as can be inferred from the significant residual morbidity and mortality rates despite antithrombotic treatment [2, 3]. Thus identification of novel pathways and associated prognostic markers and therapeutic targets is imperative to improve risk stratification and prevention of recurrent thrombotic complications in APS.

Accumulating evidence suggests that the pathogenesis of cardiovascular events in APS encompasses a thrombo-inflammatory pattern. This novel concept is based on evidence supporting that aPL antibodies can activate platelets, monocytes and endothelial cells, leading to increased expression of vascular cell adhesion molecules and pro-inflammatory cytokines as well as complement and subsequent neutrophil activation and tissue factor expression, ultimately promoting thrombus formation [5-8]. Amyloid-beta1-40 (A $\beta$ 40), an oligopeptide derived from the breakdown of amyloid precursor protein with a predilection for the arterial wall, has an increasingly recognized role in atherothrombotic disease [9]. Interestingly, A $\beta$ 40 plays a central role in many thrombo-inflammatory pathways that have also been implicated in APS pathophysiology [9, 10]. Its proinflammatory and pro-atherogenic effects [9] support its prognostic value in patients with coronary artery disease with risk biomarker characteristics [9, 11, 12]. Thus we tested the hypothesis that A $\beta$ 40 may be deregulated in APS, contributing to the autoimmune pro-thrombotic phenotype of the disease.

# Methods

This is a cross-sectional, case-control study comparing Aβ40 plasma levels between patients with thrombotic APS with or without obstetric complications and 1:1 age- and sex-matched healthy controls (HCs). Blood samples were collected during the APS patients' visit to our cardiovascular research laboratory for planned vascular assessment [13, 14].

We enrolled all eligible patients with APS (PAPS and SLE-APS) $>18$ years of age regularly followed in our Rheumatology Unit between January 2016 and July 2017 who agreed to participate in the study by providing written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. We excluded patients with

established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (CVD), isolated obstetric APS, diabetes mellitus, pregnancy, active infection or malignancy. Detailed recorded patient characteristics and disease features are described in Supplementary Methods, available at Rheumatology online. The adjusted Global Anti-Phospholipid Syndrome Score (aGAPSS), a validated risk score for recurrent arterial events in APS [15], was also calculated as a comparator to Aβ40 performance to detect thrombotic APS-related events. The outcomes of the study included history of the first and recurrent arterial or venous thrombotic episodes and APS-related obstetric complications. Aβ40 was measured in the plasma of patients and HCs by a well-characterized ELISA (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). The study was approved by our local institutional review board (Laiko Hospital Scientific Council; IRB number 1758).

# Statistical analysis

To evaluate the association of $\mathsf{A}\beta 40$ plasma levels with the diagnosis of APS, we applied a binary logistic regression model controlling for a pre-specified set of cardiometabolic risk factors. We used univariable and then multivariable linear regression to identify independent determinants of Aβ40 levels in APS patients. Ordinal logistic regression analysis was used to investigate associations between Aβ40 levels and recurrent arterial thromboses. Non-parametric receiver operator characteristics (ROC) curve analysis and area under the curve (AUC) comparisons between nested models were used to assess the discriminative and additive value over the aGAPSS [15] of Aβ40 for recurrent arterial thromboses, respectively. Statistical analysis was conducted with Stata version 12.1 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Methods and statistical analysis are described in detail in Supplementary Methods, available at Rheumatology online.

# Results

We enrolled 80 patients with APS [69% female, median age 46.5 years (interquartile range 37-53), all Caucasian, all on anticoagulation treatment] and 80 age- and sex-matched HCs. One SLE-APS patient and his control match were excluded due to an outlier value of Aβ40 (>3 s.d. above the mean), which we attributed to chronic renal transplant rejection status. No difference was observed in Aβ40 plasma levels between the APS subgroups, but Aβ40 in the HCs was lower from both subgroups (mean Aβ40 60.6 pg/ml in PAPS vs 51.5 in SLE-APS) (Fig. 1B). # Sensitivity analyses

In order to explore the potential confounding effect of renal insufficiency $(\mathrm{GFR} < 60 \, \mathrm{ml/min} / 1.73 \, \mathrm{m}^2)$ on Aβ40 levels [9], we performed sensitivity analyses after excluding these patients and their respective case-matched HCs. Aβ40 levels remained significantly higher in the remaining APS patients vs HCs [APS $(n = 75)$ : mean 54.7 pg/ml (s.d. 27.6) vs HC $(n = 75)$ : 42.6 (19.5), $P = 0.003$ ] while association of high Aβ40 levels with recurrent arterial thromboses did not lose significance [OR 4.87 (95% CI 1.32, 17.94), $P = 0.017$ ]. Similarly, because Aβ40 may be affected by antihypertensive treatment [9], we analysed the data after exclusion of all patients on antihypertensive treatment $(n = 25)$ or those on

angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors $(n = 10)$ and their respective age- and sex-matched HCs. Accordingly, Aβ40 remained significantly higher in APS patients vs HCs [APS $(n = 54)$ : 50.1 pg/ml (s.d. 23.1) vs HCs $(n = 54)$ : 39.8 (19.0), $P = 0.01$ and APS $(n = 69)$ : 54.5 pg/ml (s.d. 27.4) vs HCs $(n = 69)$ : 40.9 (18.6), $P = 0.001$ , respectively]. Moreover, high Aβ40 levels remained significantly associated with the primary clinical endpoint of the study (recurrent arterial thromboses) after exclusion of the patients on ACE inhibitors [OR 4.52 (95% CI 1.13, 18.14), $P = 0.033$ ]. Of note, high Aβ40 levels remained significantly associated with recurrent arterial thromboses after additional adjustment for antihypertensive treatment in the core multivariable model [OR 4.41 (95% CI 1.17, 16.57), $P = 0.028$ ]. Finally, we performed an additional analysis comparing Aβ40 levels between APS patients with neurological involvement due to APS and/or SLE (including stroke, transient ischaemic attack, epilepsy, dural sinus thrombosis or severe cognitive impairment/ dementia on drug therapy) and the rest of the patients, because increased circulating Aβ40 has been reported in several neurological diseases [9]. This analysis revealed that patients with neurological manifestations $(n = 25)$ did not have increased Aβ40 levels compared with those without [mean Aβ40 levels 48.9 pg/ml (s.d. 23.1) vs 60.9 (30.9), respectively; $P = 0.089$ ].

# Discussion

The main novel findings of this study are that circulating A $\beta$ 40 levels are increased in APS patients irrespective of subgroup type, hs-CRP levels were the only independent determinant of A $\beta$ 40 and high A $\beta$ 40 plasma levels are independently associated with recurrent arterial events with incremental discriminative value over the aGAPSS that has been used to identify APS patients at risk of recurrence [15]. Overall, these findings support a clinical role of A $\beta$ 40 in thrombo-inflammatory pathways of APS.

Arterial thrombosis is a predominant feature of APS related with its most severe disease phenotypes and outcomes, including macrovascular thrombotic disease [1, 3, 4] or small vessel vasculopathy [2, 3, 16, 17]. The pathogenesis of arterial events in APS involves the interplay between aPL-mediated thrombotic and proinflammatory mechanisms. Specifically, endothelial activation, upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, platelet activation and aggregation, as well as complement activation and neutrophil extracellular traps release and tissue factor formation, synergistically promote thrombus formation [5-8]. Interestingly, the main source of Aβ40 in the peripheral circulation is activated platelets and its production is greatly enhanced by pro-inflammatory cytokines [9]. In turn, Aβ40 directly promotes vascular damage and thrombosis through further endothelial activation and pro-inflammatory gene expression and platelet activation and aggregation,

which ultimately enhance systemic inflammation [9]. Interestingly, we found that Aβ40 in APS patients was independently associated with hs-CRP, an established marker of systemic inflammation. Along this line, elevated hs-CRP levels have been independently associated with microvascular involvement in some APS cohorts [18]. Thus there are common pathways between the detrimental thrombo-inflammatory milieu in APS and Aβ40 metabolism.

The elevated Aβ40 levels that we found in patients with APS vs HCs could either be a mere marker of aPL-related endothelial damage or it could be an indication that Aβ40 is a pathogenic mediator of APS. In support of the latter concept, Aβ40 was associated with recurrent arterial thromboses after adjustment for traditional cardiovascular and APS-specific risk factors, including anti-β2GPI IgG antibodies, considered as the primary pathogenic antibodies in APS [1, 2, 5, 6]. Importantly, Aβ40 provided incremental discriminatory ability over the aGAPSS. We have previously shown that Aβ40 blood levels concentrates characteristics of a novel risk biomarker [9] in patients with coronary artery disease [11, 12]. The high residual risk for thrombosis in spite of optimal anticoagulation treatment in APS patients makes antithrombotic treatment in APS a matter of controversy and highlights the imperative need to better understand underlying mechanisms and develop new risk stratification strategies and therapeutic targets [5-8, 19, 20].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, a relatively small sample size was included in the study, owing to the rarity of APS. However, post hoc analysis showed that our study was adequately powered ( $>90\%$ power) to detect the observed difference [Aβ40 mean difference: 13.8 pg/ml (S.E. 3.9)] in circulating levels of Aβ40 between APS patients and controls. Second, there was a cross-sectional rather than prospective timeline, with variable time intervals between thrombotic events and blood collection. Third, despite the multivariable case-control analysis, a few potential confounders in the association between APS presence and Aβ40 levels remain unexplored [anticoagulation status for example, since there were no anticoagulated participants in our control group; however, Aβ40 levels did not correlate with the international normalized ratio in patients taking acenocoumarol ( $n = 61/79$ , $r = 0.016$ ). For these reasons, the notion that a single measurement of Aβ40 could improve the prognostic ability of currently available risk markers in APS warrants further investigation to determine its clinical utility as a potential risk biomarker of recurrent arterial events [9].

In conclusion, the current study provides new evidence suggesting that Aβ40 may be implicated in proinflammatory thrombotic pathways in APS. Furthermore, our findings demonstrate that Aβ40 plasma levels provide clinically relevant incremental discriminative value over established risk factors of the disease. Prospective studies are needed to validate these results and further
