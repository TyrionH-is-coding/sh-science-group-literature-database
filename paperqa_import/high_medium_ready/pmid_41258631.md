# Avascular necrosis in antiphospholipid syndrome: pathophysiology, risk factors, and management

Reine Zankar<sup>1</sup> $\cdot$ Reem El Jamal<sup>1</sup> $\cdot$ Georges El Hasbani<sup>2</sup> $\cdot$ Imad Uthman<sup>3</sup> $\cdot$

Received: 22 June 2025 / Revised: 5 November 2025 / Accepted: 10 November 2025 / Published online: 19 November 2025

# Abstract

Background Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an autoimmune disorder characterized by thrombosis and pregnancy morbidity in the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies, which include lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin, and anti- $\beta 2$ glycoprotein I. The updated 2023 ACR/EULAR classification criteria recognize microvascular features of APS but not orthopedic complications. Among these, avascular necrosis (AVN) is an uncommon and underrecognized manifestation, characterized by ischemic death of bone tissue, most often affecting the femoral head, knee, talus, and humeral head. The link between APS and AVN remains unclear and warrants further review.

Objectives To explore the association between APS and AVN, focusing on its pathophysiology, prevalence, risk factors, clinical presentation, and management strategies.

Methods A comprehensive search was performed across medical databases, including PubMed, Medline, and Google Scholar, using relevant keywords.

Results AVN is a rare but significant complication of APS, with prevalence ranging from 0.9 to $20\%$ , depending on the study design and population analyzed. The pathogenesis is primarily attributed to microvascular thrombosis and hypercoagulability caused by antiphospholipid antibodies. Identified risk factors include prolonged glucocorticoid use, thrombocytopenia, pregnancy, and underlying microvascular abnormalities. Early detection via MRI and timely interventions, such as anticoagulation therapy, lifestyle modifications, and surgical procedures, improve patient outcomes and prevent disease progression.

Conclusions Understanding the multifaceted nature of AVN in APS is crucial for timely diagnosis and effective management. Further research is needed to establish clear preventative strategies and optimize long-term treatment protocols, ultimately improving patient care and clinical outcomes.

Keywords Antiphospholipid syndrome $\cdot$ APS-related complications $\cdot$ Avascular necrosis $\cdot$ Osteonecrosis $\cdot$ Pathogenesis

# Introduction

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) was first documented by Graham RV Hughes in 1983 [1]. It is an autoimmune systemic disorder characterized by arterial, venous, or small vessel thrombosis and/or obstetric complications such as eclampsia or severe preeclampsia that result in

premature birth, as well as fetal loss in the setting of persistent antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL) [2]. aPLs are autoantibodies that bind to phospholipid-bound proteins, and they include lupus anticoagulant (LA), anticardiolipin (aCL), and anti- $\beta 2$ -glycoprotein I antibodies (aB2GPI) [3]. APS may present as a primary disorder or as secondary to other autoimmune diseases, specifically systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [3]. A minority of patients may develop catastrophic APS (CAPS), which is a life-threatening condition characterized by diffuse thrombosis in multiple vascular beds, resulting in multiorgan dysfunction over a short period of time [4].

The 2023 ACR/EULAR APS classification criteria, which is intended for research purposes, include a criterion of at least one positive aPL within 3 years of the presence of an aPL-associated clinical criterion proceeded with other

<!-- page 2 -->
weighted criteria grouped into six clinical domains and two laboratory domains [5]. The clinical domains have been updated to include microvascular complications of APS, but avascular necrosis (AVN) is not a part of this new classification criteria [5]. The orthopedic manifestations associated with APS remain rare and underrecognized, with AVN being among these manifestations [6, 7]. AVN has been unusually associated with APS [8]. AVN is also known as osteonecrosis, ischemic necrosis of bone, aseptic necrosis, and subchondral avascular necrosis [9]. It is a degenerative bone condition characterized by the death of bone marrow cells and trabecular bone due to the disruption of the subchondral blood supply [10]. It most commonly involves the femoral head, knee, talus, and humeral head [10].

Although scattered case reports and small series have described AVN in APS, there is no comprehensive synthesis of its prevalence, risk factors, pathophysiology, and management. The objective of this review is therefore to summarize current evidence regarding AVN in APS, highlight the limitations of existing literature, and identify areas requiring further research to guide clinical practice.

# Materials and methods

A comprehensive literature search was performed to explore the association between APS and AVN, focusing on pathophysiology, prevalence, risk factors, clinical presentation, and management. PubMed, Medline, and Google Scholar databases were systematically searched using a combination of MeSH terms and keywords, including "antiphospholipid syndrome," "antiphospholipid antibodies," "avascular necrosis," "osteonecrosis," "ischemic necrosis of bone," and

"bone infarction." Boolean operators such as AND/OR were applied to optimize the search.

Filters were applied for studies published in English and non-English between January 2000 and March 2025. We included human studies published in English that specifically reported AVN or osteonecrosis in patients with APS or in association with antiphospholipid antibodies, regardless of study design (case reports, cohort studies, cross-sectional studies, or reviews). Exclusion criteria were animal or in vitro studies and articles not distinguishing APS-related AVN from other causes. Titles and abstracts were screened for eligibility, followed by full-text review, and relevant data were extracted on prevalence, mechanisms, clinical features, and treatment outcomes. Reference lists of relevant articles were also screened to identify additional studies. Although this was a narrative review, methodological quality was considered: observational studies were appraised using elements of the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, and case reports were reviewed against CARE guideline criteria, with findings interpreted in light of the limitations inherent to single case descriptions (Fig. 1).

# Risk factors

# General AVN

Glucocorticoid use is a well-established risk factor for AVN [11]. However, many case reports describe cases of osteonecrosis that occurred in the absence of corticosteroids or other typical risk factors, but with isolated thrombocytopenia and a triple positivity for aPL (6), or with high titer IgG and IgM anticardiolipin antibody (aCL) [15, 17]. Thrombocytopenia was found in $66.7\%$ of cases with APS and AVN compared to only $7.9\%$ of APS with no AVN [11]. It also occurred in $13.2\%$ of females and $18.2\%$ of males, indicating approximately similar risk distribution across genders for this APS-associated clinical complication [18]. In addition, pregnancy, a secondary factor of hypercoagulability [19], acts as a major exacerbating factor for osteonecrosis, with symptoms worsening in the third trimester and postpartum [20]. Multifocal AVN has been reported after liver transplantation in patients with antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), highlighting the role of atypical triggers and multifactorial risk in its clinical presentation [21].

Age was also found to be a risk factor. A cohort study showed that AVN was significantly more common in younger patients (mean age: $31.2 \pm 7.3$ years) compared to those without AVN (mean age: $42.4 \pm 11.9$ years) $(P = 0.036)$ [8]. The study emphasized that AVN might be linked to thrombosis in subchondral arteries and impaired

blood supply, which could be more impactful in younger patients whose collateral circulation may not yet be fully developed.

# AVN in APS

Patients with PAPS who use glucocorticoids are at a higher risk of developing AVN compared to those who do not. A study demonstrated that patients with PAPS who developed AVN were more likely to have used glucocorticoids $(\mathrm{P} = 0.038)$ [11]. All of the patients with PAPS who developed AVN had a history of glucocorticoid use [11]. However, despite the strong association between AVN and glucocorticoid use, AVN can still develop in the absence of corticosteroids in PAPS. aCL IgG antibodies have been the most commonly associated aPL with AVN. Elevated aCL IgG and IgM antibodies were consistently linked to AVN in studies connecting them with APS [22]. $\beta 2$ -glycoprotein 1 antibodies were also elevated in some cases, though not as frequently. High titers of aPL, particularly aCL, and lupus anticoagulant, were identified as significant risk factors for osteonecrosis, even in the absence of corticosteroids or trauma [19]. Despite osteonecrosis associated with aPL being rare, most cases were linked to high-titer aPL levels, even without meeting APS classification criteria [16]. This revision emphasizes aCL IgG as the key antibody associated with AVN and highlights the role of high titer aPL in predisposing to AVN.

Thrombocytopenia, which is a risk factor for developing AVN, is a common complication associated with APS, and it is observed in 20 to $40\%$ of patients with APS [23]. Livedo reticularis, a common skin manifestation in APS, was significantly associated with AVN $(P = 0.041)$ [8].

# Pathophysiology

# General AVN

The main pathogenetic mechanism that is thought to contribute to AVN is intravascular coagulation and thrombosis [19, 23, 24]. The primary factors that contribute to hypercoagulability include hypofibrinolysis (low tissue plasminogen activator, high plasminogen activator inhibitor) and

<!-- page 4 -->
increased levels of von Willebrand factor, both of which disrupt blood flow in bone vasculature [19]. In addition, protein C and protein S deficiencies, as well as activated protein C resistance, have been associated with an increased tendency for venous thrombosis and extended thromboembolic events [19]. Homocystinuria, with additional factors such as the presence or absence of factor V Leiden, has also been associated with thromboembolic events [19]. Additional contributing factors include lipid metabolism changes, increased intraosseous pressure, endothelial injury [11], endothelial and platelet activation, and upregulation of adhesion molecules [22, 25]. Other possible secondary causes of hypercoagulability include glucocorticoids and pregnancy [19]. Glucocorticoid-induced apoptosis of osteocytes leads to structural collapse [14]. Pregnancy may induce multifocal osteonecrosis through hypercoagulability, venous congestion, and hormonal changes like increased estrogen and progesterone levels during the third trimester. These changes lead to alterations in lipid metabolism and increased intraosseous pressure, contributing to bone ischemia and infarction [20].

# AVN in APS

AVN in PAPS is also thought to arise from hypercoagulability and vascular thrombosis. Specific mechanisms,

as outlined in Fig. 2, include microthrombosis of bone vessels, including subchondral arterial vessels, leading to tissue ischemia and infarction [6, 15, 16, 22]. Additional mechanisms that fall under the general AVN mechanisms and contribute to thrombosis in PAPS are endothelial injury and/or pathological activation, compromised fibrinolytic activity, and platelet activation [11]. aPL target the coagulation cascade proteins, endothelial cells, and platelets. This prevalence of coagulation abnormalities, including aPL, in patients with osteonecrosis, underscores its under-recognized association [19]. Also, anti-aB2GPI antibodies activate complement and contribute to thrombosis [26]. Besides, a prior history of cerebral infarction and thrombocytopenia further supports the link between APS and vascular ischemic events [25, 27]. Livedo reticularis, a common skin manifestation in APS, was more frequent in patients with AVN, suggesting an association with diminished blood flow [8].

Furthermore, although we previously mentioned that there is no difference in gender contribution to APS/AVN, a study showed a potential gender difference in immune response, with females showing higher levels of IgM aCL (76.3% in females vs. 36.4% in males, $P = 0.025$ ), which aligns with the broader understanding of heightened immunoreactivity and antibody production in females due to hormonal influences [11] and venous congestion [19].

# Clinical presentation and diagnosis

Patients usually present with severe joint pain interfering with daily activities, and limited mobility, typically affecting weight-bearing joints (Table 2) [19].

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the diagnostic modality used for confirmation, particularly in high-risk groups [11]. MRI detected $20\%$ of AVN in patients with primary APS, and asymptomatic AVN can be detected by MRI in approximately one-fifth of patients with primary APS [8]. This emphasizes the superiority of MRI over radiography for early detection, suggesting that clinicians may consider MRI screening in high-risk APS patients even in the absence of symptoms. MRI has also been recommended in atypical affected sites like the humerus [6] or knee, showing typical signal changes such as subcortical necrosis, sclerotic changes [17], and subchondral collapse, bone marrow edema, and articular incongruity in advanced cases [22], before radiographic changes appear. It can detect early ischemic changes and confirm the extent of necrosis [19].

To further support the effectiveness of MRI, a tibial biopsy confirmed osteonecrosis in a case of a 15-year-old girl with osteonecrosis in the distal femur and proximal tibia [16]. Another case of a patient who presented with inflammatory arthralgia, hip stiffness, and motion limitations due to multifocal AVN detected by MRI was later confirmed by bone scintigraphy and other radiographic imaging identifying multiple infarcts, including atypical sites like the semilunar bone (wrist) [15], "piano keyboard" spine lesions [28], and ribs [29].

# AVN in APS

Hydration, use of anticoagulants, and glucocorticoid tapering for at-risk patients resulted in a favorable outcome with no recurrence over 3 years [6-13, 34]. Patients with APS are at high risk of recurrent thrombosis and require lifelong antithrombotic prophylaxis [32, 35]. Moreover, treatment with low-dose aspirin led to significant improvement, with follow-up MRI showing a reduction in bone lesions over 1 year [16]. Long-term follow-up further demonstrated no additional APS-related complications with continued low-dose aspirin therapy, underscoring the role of anticoagulation in preventing recurrence [17].

Routine imaging is recommended in APS patients with risk factors like livedo reticularis [8]. In advanced cases, surgical interventions like core decompression, arthroplasty, or joint replacement may be necessary [1, 17, 19].

As for asymptomatic aPL carriers with a high-risk profile (identified by any of the following: multiple aPL positivity, LAC, or persistently high aPL titres), primary prophylaxis with low-dose aspirin is recommended [36].

Early diagnosis and prophylactic anticoagulation therapy during pregnancy are recommended to prevent severe complications like MFON [14]. While timely diagnosis and tailored treatments are critical for preventing disease progression and maintaining joint function, caution is needed when recommending aspirin or anticoagulants for all patients with APS who develop AVN. The ALIWA-PAS trial found no significant difference in the number of thrombotic events between low-dose aspirin (LDA) versus low-dose aspirin combined with low-intensity warfarin $(\mathrm{LDA} + \mathrm{W})$ [37]. However, $\mathrm{LDA} + \mathrm{W}$ was associated with a higher risk of bleeding [35]. Moreover, some studies support the fact that anticoagulation may not always prevent AVN in APS [38]. Therefore, treatment strategies should be individualized, balancing the benefits of aspirin and analgesics against the potential risks, to optimize outcomes without compromising patient safety [31, 39, 40].

# Limitations of the review

It should be emphasized that most of the available data regarding the treatment of APS-related AVN, including the reported benefits of aspirin and anticoagulation, are derived from isolated case reports or small series. These findings, while informative, lack the robustness of large prospective cohorts or randomized controlled trials and must therefore be interpreted with caution.

# Conclusion

AVN is a rare but impactful complication of APS, linked to hypercoagulability and microvascular ischemia which might be caused by aPL. Its multifactorial etiology underscores the importance of early diagnosis and tailored management strategies. Timely interventions, including anticoagulation and surgical options, can mitigate disease progression and improve patient outcomes. Moreover, proactive imaging screening for AVN in APS may need to focus on high-risk patients due to the potential severity and impact of delayed diagnosis. These include patients undergoing prolonged glucocorticoid therapy, especially those on high doses or long-term treatment, as well as those with thrombocytopenia, elevated titers of aPL, pregnancy, SLE, chronic alcohol use, or smoking. Additionally, asymptomatic patients in high-risk scenarios, such as post-thrombotic events (history of deep vein thrombosis or arterial thrombosis), and pediatric and adolescent APS patients, who are particularly vulnerable due to rapid bone growth and vascular changes, are included.
