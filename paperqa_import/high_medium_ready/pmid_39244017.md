Hypercoagulable states, also called thrombophilia, can either be congenital or acquired. Congenital thrombophilia, associated mainly with venous thrombosis, is either secondary to coagulation-inhibitor deficiencies, i.e., antithrombin, protein C and Protein S, or gain of function mutations, i.e., factor V Leiden and prothrombin G20210A mutations. Despite the relative frequency of these two mutations, they have not been associated with venous thrombosis recurrence. Most prevalent thrombophilia have a limited impact and usually does not change indications for duration of antithrombotic treatment or prophylaxis compared to decisions based on clinical factors. However, rare inherited thrombophilia such as antithrombin deficiency could justify a long-term anticoagulation. The main acquired thrombophilia, the Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), is associated with both arterial and venous thrombosis. Its impact on patient management is significant: choice of the anticoagulant (DOAC vs. warfarin), duration of anticoagulation, screening of any organ involvement and systemic autoimmune disease, introduction of immunosuppressive therapy.

# 1. Introduction

Venous thrombosis, which includes deep-vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), is a frequent disease with an incidence of approximately 1 in 1,000 individuals in industrialized countries (review in 1). Thrombophilia is used to define constitutional or acquired conditions that are known to be associated with hyperactivity of the coagulation system and/or the development of thromboembolic events (2). Congenital thrombophilia is mainly associated with the occurrence of venous thrombosis, whereas acquired thrombophilia (mainly antiphospholipid syndrome) can be associated with either venous or arterial thrombosis.

After Egeberg published the first case of inherited antithrombin deficiency, it opened the field of thrombophilia, with the description of protein C (PC) and protein S (PS) deficiencies (3-6). Thrombophilia was initially considered a rare monogenic disorder highly associated with venous thrombosis, but this view was challenged in the mid 1980s when more frequent genetic risk factors of thrombophilia emerged, demonstrating that venous thrombosis is a multicausal disease (7,8). Furthermore and concomitantly, acquired conditions such as the antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) were described with the occurrence of venous but also arterial thrombosis (9). We will describe here the main congenital and acquired thrombophilias and their implication in clinical practice according to current evidences and guidelines.

Screening for thrombophilia has a clinical significance only if a positive test could modify management in terms of type, dosage or duration of anticoagulation in a patient with a history of venous thromboembolism (VTE) or if screening in the family of an index case could lead to counseling or prophylaxis measures to prevent occurrence of VTE. Within the recent years the role of the most frequent genetic thrombophilia has been shown to be of limited utility

<!-- page 7 -->
in clinical practice, whereas identification of the antiphospholipid syndrome has much more relevant clinical implications.

# 2.1. Antithrombin Deficiency

Antithrombin (AT) is a 58-kDa plasma protein that regulates coagulation by inhibiting procoagulant serine proteases such as thrombin, activated (a) factor X, and factor IXa (review in 15). AT is synthesized by the liver and circulates at a concentration of approximately $2.5\mu \mathrm{M}$ ; levels are decreased by estrogen and heparin therapy.

AT deficiency is transmitted as an autosomal dominant disease (0.02% prevalence in the general population). Among congenital thrombophilias, AT deficiency is undoubtedly the one bearing the highest risk of VTE (approximately 1% per year) (16). There are two types of hereditary AT deficiency. Type I or quantitative deficiency is the most frequent and is characterized by decreased activity and a decreased protein concentration. Type II or qualitative deficiencies are caused by functional defects, protein concentrations being normal or near normal in immunoassays. The dysfunction may affect the reactive site (type $\mathrm{II}_{\mathrm{RS}}$ ) or the heparin-binding site (type $\mathrm{II}_{\mathrm{HBS}}$ ) or both (type $\mathrm{II}_{\mathrm{PE}}$ - pleiotropic effect). Homozygosity is very rare, mostly present in patients with type $\mathrm{II}_{\mathrm{HBS}}$ , with the exception of one patient with type $\mathrm{II}_{\mathrm{PE}}$ deficiency. Venous and arterial thrombosis may occur during infancy in such patients (17,18).

# 2.2. Protein C and Protein S Deficiencies

PC is a vitamin K-dependent protein that is activated at the endothelial surface by thrombin bound to thrombomodulin. In the presence of its cofactor PS, activated PC (APC) degrades activated FV (FVa) and FVIIIa, thereby impeding further thrombin generation (20). FV inactivation occurs in a biphasic reaction, with rapid cleavage at Arg506, followed by a slower cleavage at Arg306 (see below factor V Leiden). The first cleavage only partially affects FV activity, whereas full inactivation occurs after the second cleavage at Arg306. PS markedly stimulates the second phase of the inactivation process, by a 20-fold enhancement of Arg306 hydrolysis (21). The mechanism of FVIIIa inhibition by APC is also biphasic, with cleavage at Arg562 and then at Arg336. FVIIIa inactivation by APC is increased by PS and FV, which act synergistically as cofactors for the reaction (22).

PC is synthesized by hepatocytes and circulates at a concentration of approximately 70 nM, with a half-life of approximately 8 hours. APC forms inactive complexes with serine protease inhibitors, mainly protein C inhibitor (PCI), but also protease nexin 3, $\alpha_{1}$ antitrypsin, and $\alpha_{2}$ macroglobulin (23). Although PS is mainly produced by hepatocytes, it is also detected in endothelial cells and platelets. In the circulation, PS forms inactive complexes with C4b-binding protein (C4b-BP). Free PS represents approximately $40\%$ of the total circulating level, and only this fraction has APC cofactor activity. PS plasma levels are lower in women younger than 45 years and in those who are pregnant or are using oral contraceptives (24).

PC and PS deficiencies are transmitted, like AT, as autosomal dominant traits. Hereditary PC deficiency was first identified in subjects who had about half the normal PC concentration and a family history of thrombosis. In prospectively studied asymptomatic members of thrombophilic families, the incidence of VTE was approximately $0.5\%$ per patient-year in

<!-- page 9 -->
patients with PC deficiency and between $0.5\%$ and $1.65\%$ in patients with PS deficiency (10- 15).

The thrombotic risk associated with PC levels less than $67\%$ was confirmed in a case-control study of unselected patients who developed DVT before 70 years of age, with a relative risk (RR) of approximately 3 (25,26). Free PS of less than the 90th percentile of control values are also associated with a risk of developing VTE (27,28). Heterozygous subjects belonging to families with PS deficiencies are at risk of recurrent thromboembolic disease in adulthood. In PC or PS heterozygous subjects, the probability of being free of thrombotic events at 45 years of age is approximately $50\%$ (28,29). Homozygous PS deficiency, or homozygous PC deficiency, are rare thrombophilia phenotypes associated with severe thrombosis, including neonatal skin necrosis and purpura fulminans (30-32).

# 2.3.Factor VLEIDEN

# 2.3.1. Physiopathology

In 1993, Dahlbäck described three families in whom APC did not yield the expected prolongation of the clotting time in an aPTT assay (33,34); this defined a new phenotype, called APCR (Activated PC Resistance). APCR was found in more than $15\%$ of patients with DVT and in $2\%$ to $10\%$ of control subjects (35,36). The study by Bertina et al. showed that APCR cosegregated with the $FV$ gene and with a single mutation $\mathrm{(FV_{Leiden}}$ , Arg506 Gln) affecting one of the APC cleavage sites (37). Most cases of APCR are caused by $\mathrm{FV_{Leiden}}$ . A single base mutation, guanine to adenine at position 1,691 of the $FV$ gene, is responsible for the Arg506 Gln mutation known as $\mathrm{FV_{Leiden}}$ (37-40). This mutation results in a substantially reduced anticoagulant response to APC, because $\mathrm{FV_{Leiden}}$ is inactivated about 10 times more slowly than

<!-- page 10 -->
normal FV. This impairment of FVa inactivation increases thrombin generation and explains more than $90\%$ of clinical APCR phenotypes.

# 2.3.2. Epidemiology

The prevalence of $\mathrm{FV}_{\mathrm{Leiden}}$ is high in populations of white descent, but low in native populations of Asia, Africa, and Australia (41). All $\mathrm{FV}_{\mathrm{Leiden}}$ alleles are carried by the same haplotype, leading to the inference that the mutation spreads by a founder effect. The estimated occurrence time of the mutation is approximately 30,000 years ago, implying that it took place after the out-of-Africa divergence that occurred approximately 100,000 years ago (42). Its spread among Whites and its high prevalence suggests that $\mathrm{FV}_{\mathrm{Leiden}}$ is associated with a survival advantage, such as a decrease in severe bleeding after delivery (43,44).

The frequency of $\mathrm{FV}_{\mathrm{Leiden}}$ in white populations is around $5\%$ in caucasians, with subpopulation with higher prevalence (35,36,45-50) (Table I). $\mathrm{FV}_{\mathrm{Leiden}}$ affects $15\%$ to $25\%$ of caucasians' patients with DVT, and the risk of a first DVT in heterozygous carriers is approximately fivefold higher than in a control population. The association with PE is weaker than with deep vein thrombosis (51-53) also called as the $\mathrm{FV}_{\mathrm{Leiden}}$ paradox (reference).

Familial and case-control studies show that $\mathrm{FV}_{\mathrm{Leiden}}$ is clearly a milder thrombophilic state than heterozygous AT, PC, or PS deficiency. The absolute risks of VTE events in subjects with AT, PC, and PS deficiency are between $0.5\%$ and $1.5\%$ per year, compared to only $0.1\%$ to $0.3\%$ per year in heterozygous carriers of $\mathrm{FV}_{\mathrm{Leiden}}$ . The risk is higher in homozygous $\mathrm{FV}_{\mathrm{Leiden}}$ (54-56). Finally, the risk of recurrence associated with $\mathrm{FV}_{\mathrm{Leiden}}$ is low as demonstrated in numerous studies (57-69). Indeed, in a patient with a history of VTE and based on epidemiologic studies,

the first episode of VTE carries a significant higher weight for a reccurrent event than the $\mathrm{FV}_{\mathrm{Leiden}}$ itself.

# 2.4. The FII (Prothrombin) G20210A mutation

# 2.4.1. Pathophysiology

Prothrombin has procoagulant, anticoagulant, and antifibrinolytic activities after its activation into thrombin by the prothrombinase complex. Thrombin acts by activating factors XIII, XI and VIII, V, PC, and the thrombin-activatable fibrinolysis inhibitor (TAFI) and by cleaving fibrinogen to fibrin. Prothrombin activation is mediated by FXa, which cleaves prothrombin at Arg271-Thr272 and Arg320-Ile321 to release the catalytic domain from the carboxy-terminal domain.

By extensively screening the prothrombin genes of 28 families with unexplained venous thrombosis, Poort et al. found one heterozygous nucleotide transition (G to A) at position 20210 in the $3^{\prime}$ untranslated region in 5 probands $(18\%)$ (70). This mutation is associated with significantly higher prothrombin levels in heterozygotes than in non-carriers and is an independent risk factor for thrombosis, leading to increased thrombin generation (71,72). However, on an individual basis, prothrombin levels cannot discriminate if patients are carriers of the FII mutation.

# 2.4.2. Epidemiology

The prevalence of the FII G20210A mutation is high in populations of white descent but low or nil in Asians, American Indians, and African Americans (73,74). As in the case of $\mathrm{FV}_{\mathrm{Leiden}}$ , a founder effect explains the high prevalence of the factor II G20210A mutation in whites (75).

<!-- page 12 -->
The estimated frequency of FII G20210A in white populations is around $2 - 3\%$ (Table I). This mutation is more common in southern than in northern Europe, a gradient opposite to that of $\mathrm{FV}_{\mathrm{Leiden}}$ (73). The risk of DVT is increased by threefold to fourfold in heterozygous carriers of FII G20210A compared to noncarriers. In the rare FII G20210A homozygotes, the risk of thrombosis is moderately increased (72,76,77), often associated with other genetic or acquired risk factors (78). As for $\mathrm{FV}_{\mathrm{Leiden}}$ , the risk of recurrence associated with heterozygous FII G20210A is low (79-82) for the same epidemiologic reason.

# 3. Clinical consequences of congenital thrombophilia

There is no consensus on the subsets of subjects who qualify for thrombophilia screening and no guidelines based on strong evidence concerning the consequence of thrombophilia on prophylaxis or treatment duration after VTE events, as there are no randomized studies to answer these questions. The most salient recommendations are summarized below (1,83-92). When to screen for thrombophilia depends mainly on the knowledge concerning the associations between thrombophilia and its clinical consequences. Congenital thrombophilia is mainly associated with venous thrombosis and thrombophilia screening is recommended for proximal DVT or PE, but

not in case of superficial venous thrombosis or distal DVT. Concerning the question what to screen in clinical practice there is a consensus to make a complete thrombophilia screening as clinical events can be associated with any of the thrombophilic factors. For this reason, when thrombophilia screening is indicated after a VTE event, it is recommended to perform:

Coagulation inhibitor deficiency (AT-, PC-, PS-deficiency)   
- Genetic testing for $\mathrm{FV}_{\text {Leiden }}$ and FII G20210 mutations   
- Screening for APS (IgG and IgM anticardiolipin, IgG and IgM anti beta-2GPI and lupus anticoagulant).

Several guidelines have been issued since 2010 concerning who should be tested and the impact of testing (89-93). It must be emphasized that all these guidelines are based on very low certainty in the evidence. That is particularly clear in the most recent guidelines issued in 2023 by the American Society of Hematology (93); the reason is that no prospective randomized studies were performed to demonstrate the usefulness of thrombophilia screening. It is also due to the fact that thrombophilia concerns heterogeneous situations such as low risk of thrombophilia (i.e. frequent heterozygous FV Leiden or prothrombin mutations) or higher risk such as antithrombin deficiency (Table II). The predictive value of a family history for identifying patients with a thrombophilic state is unreliable, and the absence of a family history does not preclude the need to screen for $\mathrm{FV}_{\mathrm{Leiden}}$ , FII G20210A, and coagulation inhibitor deficiencies (94-97). # 4. Antiphospholipid syndrome

# 4.1.Relevance of new classification criteria for definite antiphospholipid syndrome.

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an autoimmune disease associating thrombosis and obstetric morbidity and the presence of antibodies directed against phospholipids or certain proteins associated with phospholipids. This disease is rare in adults and exceptional in children. Since 1999, the identification of patients with APS has been facilitated by the use of the Sapporo classification criteria updated in Sydney (101). Although these criteria have been of great help so far, our understanding of APS has changed and evolved since 2006 and some limitations to the classification have emerged. Indeed, the symptoms of APS are not limited to thromboses and obstetric complications. The Sapporo-Sydney classification does not take into account other symptoms or complications potentially linked to APS ("non-criteria" manifestations such as livedo, cardiac valve damage, renal damage, thrombocytopenia) (101). Furthermore, these criteria did not enable a stratification based on the biological profile or cardiovascular risk factors, while it is recognized that "triple positive" patients (presence of the 3 antiphospholipid tests concomitantly) present the greater risk of thrombosis. Finally, these criteria highlighted a need for optimization of definitions including those of obstetric manifestations and the positivity of an antiphospholipid test among others.

Thus, in 2015, work began to overhaul the APS classification criteria under the aegis of the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) and the European Alliance of Associations For Rheumatology (EULAR). These new criteria aimed to be more precise and, above all, more

<!-- page 20 -->
specific. End of August 2023, the new ACR/EULAR classification criteria for antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) are officially published and can now be used (Figure 1)(102,103).

# 4.2.Contribution of the new 2023 ACR/EULAR APS classification criteria

The objective was to develop new classification criteria for APS with high specificity for research use exclusively. Thanks to a rigorous methodology already proven by previous classification criteria (lupus, scleroderma, etc.) and a major international multidisciplinary contribution, these new criteria are weighted and stratified according to vascular risk factors and biological profile.

They reflect the current state of knowledge on APS, providing high specificity and a solid basis for future research into this syndrome. As a reminder, these criteria are classification criteria, as were the Sapporo-Sydney criteria, allowing the homogeneous inclusion of patients in scientific studies only. This differs from criteria made for diagnosis. However, in the absence of diagnostic criteria for autoimmune diseases, the classification criteria are used in clinical practice. Thus, it is necessary to know the limits of the classification criteria: no inclusion of manifestations that are too rare and little studied during APS (chorea, myelitis, etc.), very specific definitions which may lead to the exclusion of certain patients (thresholds revised upwards of antiphospholipid tests, less specificity of the IgM isotype or certain obstetric manifestations).

Thus, for patients who cannot be classified according to the new classification and in all cases, the clinical judgment of the practitioner prevails and remains essential to decide on the therapeutic management of the patient based international guidelines (104). As these new criteria

<!-- page 21 -->
are intended to be more specific, they may also have the benefit of reducing overclassification ("overdiagnosis") and questionable management of APS patients.

# 4.3.New 2023 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for APS

To classify a patient with APS, 4 steps are now mandatory: a) The patient must meet the presence of entry criteria; b) the number of clinical manifestations must be counted to obtain a total clinical score; c) the number of biological manifestations must be counted to obtain a total biological score; d) the patient is classified as APS if the clinical score $\geq 3$ AND the biological score $\geq 3$ (Figure 1).

Patients must have at least one positive antiphospholipid test (at moderate or high rate) with a maximum delay of three years between clinical manifestations and biological results. This makes impossible to classify patients with no information relative to APS serology or with negative APS serology (seronegative APS).

Clinical criteria are divided into 6 domains and take into account stratification according to cardiovascular risk factors and according to the number of manifestations. Similarly, biological criteria are divided into 2 domains and take into account stratification according to persistence, types of tests, titers, and isotypes. A number of points is assigned to each criterion allowing a final score (clinical and biological) to be calculated (Figure 1; from ref. 102-103).

Of note, it is possible to count only one criterion per domain, the one earning the higher number of points. For example, if a patient has a history of two episodes of pulmonary embolism, one caused by a major risk factor (1 point) and the other unprovoked (3 points), thus only the second event should be counted (3 points for domain 1 "venous thrombosis").

<!-- page 22 -->
Furthermore, it is possible to count a criterion if not another cause is identified. For example, a thrombocytopenia occurs following a chemotherapy in a patient; thus the criterion "thrombocytopenia" cannot be taken into account because it is not linked to APS but rather as a result of chemotherapy. This analysis requires therefore the clinical judgment of the investigator.

# 4.4. Physiopathology and epidemiology

The exact pathophysiology of APS is still not fully understood, but it is thought to involve a combination of genetic, environmental, and hormonal factors. One of the key mechanisms in APS is the production of aPL, which are a group of autoantibodies that target various phospholipid-binding proteins, such as beta-2-glycoprotein I (β2GPI) (105). One major pathway implicated in thrombotic events in aPL positive carriers is the APCR which can be evaluated by thrombin generation assay (106). aPL could impair APC effect on FV activation by FXa a direct interaction with FV from the endothelial cell surface. Proteins C and S are phospholipid-binding proteins, and anti-PC and anti-PS antibodies have also been described as potentially involved in APC resistance. Platelets might play a crucial role in APC resistance mediated by aPL as they are a pivotal actor in APC sensitivity and an important cellular target of aPL (106). Immune system components seem to interact with APC and vice versa. Some studies suggested that neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) components could interact with APC sensitivity concentration and APC resistance in aPL + subjects (107). More studies are needed to understand the full pathophysiology of APS.

# 4.5. Testing for APS

<!-- page 23 -->
ASH guidelines published in 2023 (108) stated that for patients with unprovoked VTE who have completed primary short-term treatment, the ASH guideline panel suggests not to perform thrombophilia (both hereditary and acquired) testing to guide the duration of anticoagulant treatment. However, in the case of APS, by missing an APS diagnosis, this would decrease the chance of arterial thrombosis and obstetrical complication prevention.

The BSH guidelines published in 2022 stated that (109):

- Screening for antiphospholipid antibodies is recommended following unprovoked VTE because this may alter management including choice of antithrombotic therapy (Grade 1B).   
- Screening for antiphospholipid antibodies is suggested in patients with VTE provoked by a minor risk factor as this may alter management including choice of antithrombotic therapy (Grade 2C).   
- Patients with acute multiple thrombotic events and evidence of organ failure suggestive of catastrophic APS should be tested for antiphospholipid antibodies (Grade 1A).   
- As APS is an acquired thrombophilia, screening for antiphospholipid antibodies is not recommended in family members of patients with thrombosis (Grade 1A)

The NICE guidelines updated in 2020 consider testing for aPL in people who have had unprovoked DVT or PE if it is planned to stop anticoagulation treatment, but with the risk that these tests can be affected by anticoagulants and specialist advice may be needed (110).

Taken together, BSH guidelines may represent a reasonable option to decrease the misdiagnosis of APS offering to patients a higher chance to decrease the risk of thrombosis overtime.

# 4.6. Thromboprophylaxis in APS.

<!-- page 24 -->
Based on evidence from a systematic literature review and expert opinion (high-quality evidence is limited, indicating a need for more high-quality research), the EULAR guidelines published in 2019 aim to guide thromboprophylaxis in adults with APS (104).

# To summarize:

- high-risk aPL profile (i.e. lupus anticoagulant, double, triple aPL positivity, persistently high aPL titres) is associated with greater risk for thrombotic and obstetric APS.   
- Risk modification includes screening for and management of cardiovascular and venous thrombosis risk factors, patient education about treatment adherence, and lifestyle counselling.   
- Low-dose aspirin (LDA) is recommended for asymptomatic aPL carriers, patients with systemic lupus erythematosus without prior thrombotic or obstetric APS, and non-pregnant women with a history of obstetric APS only, all with high-risk aPL profiles.   
- Patients with APS and first unprovoked venous thrombosis should receive long-term treatment with vitamin K antagonists (VKA) with a target international normalised ratio (INR) of 2-3.   
- In patients with APS with first arterial thrombosis, treatment with VKA with INR 2–3 or INR 3–4 is recommended, considering the individual's bleeding/thrombosis risk.   
- For patients with recurrent arterial or venous thrombosis despite adequate treatment, addition of LDA, increase of INR target to 3-4 or switch to low molecular weight heparin may be considered.

Regarding the use of direct oral anticoagulants in APS patients, ISTH guidelines have been published in 2020 (111) and recommended the following:

- APS patients are considered as "high-risk" if they present any of the following: triple positivity, arterial thrombosis, small vessel thrombosis or organ involvement, heart valve disease according to Sydney criteria

- In "high risk" APS patients, VKA should be used instead of DOACs   
- DOACs should not be used in APS patients with recurrent thrombosis while on therapeutic intensity VKA. In this circumstance, other therapeutic options may include an increased target INR range, treatment dose LMWH, or the addition of antiplatelet therapy.   
- DOACs should not be used in APS patients who are non-adherent to VKA. In this circumstance, other options may include education on adherence to VKA treatment along with frequent INR testing.   
- In single or double positive non-"high risk" APS patients who have been on DOACs with good adherence for several months for a first episode of VTE, we recommend a discussion with the patient of options including perceived risks and uncertainties, in the spirit of shared decision-making and review of whether continued treatment with a DOAC is appropriate.   
- In single- or double-positive non-"high-risk" APS patients with a single prior VTE requiring standard-intensity VKA, with allergy or intolerance to VKA or erratic INRs despite patient adherence, we suggest that alternative VKAs, if available, should be considered prior to consideration of a DOAC.   
Importantly, if a DOAC is used, only a full dose must be prescribed and no reduced dose should be considered since negative results of the ASTRO-APS trial comparing reduced dose apixaban vs. warfarin (112).

<!-- page 25 -->
To conclude, aspirin should be prescribed in primary thromboprophylaxis in APS patients. Regarding the secondary thromboprophylaxis of APS patients, Warfarin remains the cornerstone while DOACs could be a reasonable option if prerequisites proposed by ISTH guidelines are fulfilled (see above).
