## Abstract

Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT) represents a severe variant of immune-mediated thrombocytopenias (IMTs) in which anti-platelet factor 4 (PF4) antibodies activate platelets independently of heparin, leading to both thrombosis and thrombocytopenia. Despite its clinical significance, aHIT remains poorly understood and lacks evidence-based immunomodulatory treatments. This narrative translational review integrates mechanistic and therapeutic insights from immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) and antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) to identify shared pathogenic pathways relevant to aHIT. Literature from 2015 to 2025 was analyzed across PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and ClinicalTrials.gov, focusing on FcγRIIa–Syk–BTK signaling and complement activation as central drivers of platelet activation and clearance. Preclinical and clinical data indicate that targeting these axes with Syk inhibitors (fostamatinib), BTK inhibitors (rilzabrutinib, zanubrutinib), and complement inhibitors (sutimlimab) can restore platelet counts and mitigate immune-driven thrombosis. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of pathway-specific interventions in aHIT, highlighting the need for biomarker-guided, translational trials to validate their efficacy and safety. Bridging mechanistic evidence from ITP and APS provides a framework for precision immunotherapy in autoimmune HIT.

Keywords: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, FcγRIIa–Syk–BTK pathway, Complement inhibition, Immune-mediated thrombocytopenia, Translational immunotherapy

## Full Text

## Introduction

IMTs are a group of disorders characterized by autoantibody-driven platelet destruction or activation, leading to low platelet counts and clinically significant bleeding and/or thrombosis [ 1 ]. Major examples include primary immunethrombocytopenia (ITP), APS, and heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). These conditions carry a substantial clinical burden. For instance, primary ITP has an incidence of roughly 3.3 new cases per 100,000 adults per year (making it an orphan disease) and a prevalence of ∼8% [ 2 ]. Although ITP predominantly predisposes to bleeding, with intracranial hemorrhage occurring in ~ 1.4% of patients and other major bleeding in 9.6% [ 3 , 4 ], paradoxically, ITP patients also exhibit a modest increase in thromboembolism risk compared to the general population [ 2 ]. APS, defined by thrombosis and persistent antiphospholipid antibodies, often features mild thrombocytopenia (in ~ 20–30% of cases), which itself is an independent risk factor for recurrent thrombosis [ 5 ]. Likewise, HIT is a prototypical IMT whereby anti-platelet factor 4 (PF4)/heparin IgG antibodies activate platelets via FcγRIIa, causing platelet consumption and a highly prothrombotic state [ 1 , 6 ]. Without prompt recognition and therapy, up to half of HIT patients develop thromboses, sometimes with fatal outcomes. Thus, across IMTs, patients face the dual hazards of hemorrhage and thrombosis, underscoring the importance of advancing our understanding and management of these disorders [ 5 , 7 – 9 ].

aHIT is a distinct, emerging subtype of HIT that exemplifies the overlap among IMTs. In classical (heparin-dependent) HIT, thrombocytopenia occurs 5–10 days after heparin exposure and is driven by antibodies requiring heparin to form immune complexes. In aHIT, by contrast, patients harbor pathogenic anti-PF4 antibodies that can activate platelets even in the absence of heparin [ 10 ]. In other words, aHIT can occur spontaneously (or persist after heparin is stopped), placing it within the broader category of IMTs as an autoimmune process independent of exogenous drug triggers [ 11 , 12 ]. Clinically, aHIT shares key features with both ITP and APS. Like ITP, it involves immune-mediated platelet destruction (often with severe thrombocytopenia), and like APS (and HIT), it precipitates a marked thrombotic tendency despite low platelet counts [ 6 ]. Recent analyses of anti-PF4 disorders indicate that the less common subtypes (aHIT, spontaneous HIT, and vaccine-induced thrombotic thrombocytopenia) tend to follow more aggressive courses than classic HIT, with more profound thrombocytopenia and a higher frequency of both bleeding and thromboses at unusual sites (e.g., cerebral or splanchnic veins) [ 6 ]. These observations underscore why aHIT merits particular attention alongside better-known IMTs such as ITP and APS.

The current management of IMTs, including aHIT, remains suboptimal, prompting a need for novel therapeutic strategies. Standard treatments in ITP rely on broad immunosuppression (corticosteroids, IVIG) or splenectomy and newer thrombopoietin-receptor agonists, yet many patients relapse or become refractory, requiring multiple lines of therapy [ 2 , 13 ]. Similarly, in APS, indefinite anticoagulation (vitamin K antagonists or heparinoids) is the mainstay, but a significant proportion of patients still experience recurrent thromboses despite therapeutic anticoagulation [ 14 , 15 ]. Notably, APS-related thrombotic events can be resistant to warfarin, suggesting that non-coagulation mechanisms (such as complement-mediated injury) contribute to disease persistence [ 15 , 16 ]. In the context of HIT and aHIT, approved therapy has centered on non-heparin anticoagulants to prevent thrombosis; however, there are no approved immunomodulatory treatments for the underlying autoimmune process. Clinicians have attempted off-label interventions – for example, high-dose IVIG has shown efficacy in accelerating platelet count recovery in severe aHIT [ 10 , 12 ] – but robust evidence is limited. Indeed, despite increasing use of direct oral anticoagulants and IVIG in refractory HIT, high-quality data on these interventions remain scarce [ 12 ]. This therapeutic stagnation across IMTs has galvanized interest in immune-targeted therapies . In particular, signaling pathways that mediate platelet destruction/activation, such as the FcγRIIa–Syk–BTK axis, and effector mechanisms like complement activation have emerged as promising targets for next-generation treatments [ 15 , 17 ]. Agents tapping into these pathways are already being explored in ITP and APS; for example, the Syk inhibitor fostamatinib was recently approved for chronic ITP after demonstrating improved platelet counts in patients refractory to steroids, splenectomy, rituximab, or thrombopoietic agents [ 13 ]. Likewise, early-phase trials of Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors (e.g., rilzabrutinib) have shown they can suppress immune platelet destruction and raise platelet counts in heavily pretreated ITP [ 18 , 19 ]. Complement inhibitors are also under investigation, particularly for APS, given compelling data implicating complement activation in APS-related thrombosis and obstetric complications [ 15 , 20 ]. These advances suggest that targeting immune signaling in thrombocytopenic conditions can translate into tangible clinical benefit.

Direct clinical evidence in autoimmune HIT remains limited due to its rarity and under-recognition [ 12 , 21 , 22 ]. Consequently, a translational approach – leveraging insights from more extensively studied IMTs – is invaluable to inform aHIT management. Both ITP and APS provide instructive analogues: they illustrate how interrupting key immune pathways can ameliorate thrombocytopenia or prevent thrombosis. For example, successes with Syk or BTK inhibition in ITP, and with complement inhibition in refractory APS raise the question of whether similar strategies could modulate the pathogenic PF4 autoimmunity in aHIT. Extrapolating carefully from these related disorders can help identify which immune mechanisms are most relevant to aHIT’s unique combination of coagulopathy and thrombocytopenia, and which targeted therapies might be repurposed or prioritized for testing in aHIT [ 2 , 15 ]. In essence, what has been learned in ITP and APS sets the stage for innovative , mechanism-driven interventions in aHIT , where evidence-based treatment options are currently lacking.

This narrative review adopts a translational viewpoint to bridge current knowledge gaps in aHIT. We begin by outlining the principal immune pathways involved in platelet destruction and thrombosis, focusing on the FcγRIIa–Syk–BTK signaling axis and the complement system, and their roles across IMTs. We then review the preclinical and clinical evidence for therapies targeting these pathways, drawing on data from ITP, APS, HIT, and related conditions. Finally, we discuss the implications of these findings for aHIT, highlighting how emerging immune-axis inhibitors might be applied to aHIT and identifying key priorities for future research and clinical trials. Through this approach, we aim to illuminate potential avenues to improve outcomes in aHIT by translating lessons learned from parallel IMTs.

## Methods

This study was designed as a narrative translational review focusing on the shared immunopathogenic mechanisms and therapeutic implications across immune-mediated thrombocytopenias (IMTs), with special emphasis on aHIT. The objective was to bridge mechanistic evidence from immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) and antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) to potential therapeutic applications in aHIT. A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and ClinicalTrials.gov databases for studies published between January 2015 and October 2025. The search combined disease-related, mechanistic, and therapeutic terms to ensure comprehensive retrieval of translational evidence. The main keywords included:

“autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia,” “aHIT,” “immune thrombocytopenia,” “ITP,” “antiphospholipid syndrome,” “APS,” “FcγRIIa,” “Fc gamma receptor IIA,” “Syk,” “BTK,” “Bruton tyrosine kinase,” “complement,” “C1q,” “C5a,” “fostamatinib,” “rilzabrutinib,” “zanubrutinib,” “orelabrutinib,” “sutimlimab,” “immunothrombosis,” and “thromboinflammation.” Eligible publications included peer-reviewed experimental studies (in vitro or in vivo), phase I–III clinical trials, and high-quality translational reviews focusing on the FcγRIIa–Syk–BTK axis or complement pathways in immune platelet activation, destruction, or thrombosis. Non-English papers, non-peer-reviewed preprints, and studies without mechanistic or therapeutic relevance were excluded. Extracted data were qualitatively synthesized and categorized into three domains: [ 1 ] FcγRIIa signaling [ 2 ], Syk/BTK inhibition, and [ 3 ] complement modulation. Each study was appraised based on design quality, translational validity, and clinical applicability to maintain scientific rigor while preserving the flexibility of a narrative review framework.

## Immune pathways in thrombocytopenia

### Immunopathogenic framework of thrombocytopenia

IMTs arise from autoantibodies or immune complexes targeting platelet antigens (e.g., glycoproteins) or platelet factor 4 (PF4)/polyanion complexes [ 29 – 31 ]. These immune triggers bind to platelets and engage Fcγ receptors, particularly FcγRIIA on human platelets, leading to platelet activation and the release of procoagulant microparticles [ 32 ]. Activated platelets aggregate via integrin αIIbβ3 and expose phosphatidylserine, contributing to thrombus formation [ 30 ]. Simultaneously, IgG-opsonized platelets are recognized by splenic and hepatic macrophages through Fc receptors, leading to their phagocytic clearance (ADCP) and, consequently, thrombocytopenia [ 33 – 36 ]. Complement pathways can be triggered by immune complexes on platelets, which deposit C3b and mark the platelets for removal by macrophage complement receptors (CR1) [ 37 ]. Immune complexes and complement activation also recruit neutrophils; C5a chemoattracts and primes neutrophils to release neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) rich in DNA, histones, and TF [ 38 ]. NETs form scaffolds that further promote platelet adhesion and coagulation. Endothelial cells respond to these inflammatory stimuli (immune complexes, C5a, cytokines) by upregulating adhesion molecules and TF, shifting to a procoagulant phenotype [ 38 ]. In summary, IMTs involve a network of interactions: autoantibodies and immune complexes (“inputs”) activate platelets, monocytes/macrophages, neutrophils, and complement (“pathways”), culminating in accelerated platelet destruction and paradoxical platelet activation/thrombosis (“outputs”). This interplay explains why patients can experience both thrombocytopenia and thrombotic complications in these conditions [ 30 , 39 ].

### FcγRIIa ITAM signaling on platelets

Human platelets uniquely express the low-affinity IgG receptor FcγRIIA (CD32a), which contains an immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motif (ITAM) in its cytoplasmic tail [ 40 – 42 ]. Clustering of FcγRIIA by multivalent IgG immune complexes induces Src family kinase–mediated phosphorylation of ITAM tyrosines [ 35 ], leading to recruitment and activation of spleen tyrosine kinase (Syk) [ 31 ]. Activated Syk phosphorylates downstream adapters and enzymes, notably phospholipase Cγ2 (PLCγ2) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K), leading to second messenger generation (IP₃, DAG) and a rise in intracellular Ca²⁺ [ 34 , 43 ]. In parallel, Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK) is recruited to the PI3K product PIP₃ and amplifies PLCγ2 signaling and calcium flux [ 34 , 35 ]. These signals drive platelet activation, characterized by the activation of integrin αIIbβ3, granule secretion, and shape change [ 42 ]. FcγRIIA cross-linking induces α-granule exocytosis with surface P-selectin (CD62P) expression and release of dense-granule agonists, reinforcing aggregation [ 30 , 44 , 45 ], and promotes the generation of procoagulant platelets exposing phosphatidylserine and shedding microparticles [ 42 ]. A subset of activated platelets acquires a “coated” phenotype with high P-selectin expression and prothrombinase activity, supporting coagulation rather than aggregate formation [ 44 , 45 ]. Genetic modulation of this pathway is influenced by the FCGR2A H131R polymorphism. The FcγRIIa-H131 variant exhibits higher affinity for IgG2, whereas R131 binds IgG2 poorly [ 46 ], favoring IgG1-mediated receptor clustering and enhanced platelet activation [ 29 ]. This polymorphism has been associated with differential responses in disorders such as HIT, where FcγRIIA signaling is central [ 29 ]. ### Role of Syk and BTK in platelet activation & destruction

Syk and BTK are critical signaling hubs that connect platelet activation to platelet destruction in IMTs. Upon FcγRIIA engagement on platelets, Syk activation is the pivotal step that initiates downstream events leading to platelet activation (aggregation, secretion, procoagulant activity) [ 42 , 43 , 47 ]. BTK, recruited via PI3K, amplifies these signals; in platelets, BTK activity is required for full granule secretion and integrin αIIbβ3 activation, as evidenced by BTK inhibition blocking FcγRIIA-induced aggregation [ 35 ]. These kinases also mediate crosstalk with the GPVI/FcRγ pathway – platelets’ collagen receptor complex. Both FcγRIIA and GPVI signals converge on Syk and PLCγ2, raising cytosolic Ca²⁺ and activating protein kinase C, thereby reinforcing each other [ 34 , 40 ]. For example, simultaneous engagement of GPVI (by collagen) and FcγRIIA (by immune complexes) can synergize to produce “superactivated” platelets that expose phosphatidylserine and shed microparticles [ 42 , 48 – 50 ].

Importantly, Syk and BTK also function in immune effector cells that destroy platelets. In splenic macrophages, cross-linking of Fcγ receptors by platelet-bound autoantibodies triggers the Lyn→ITAM→Syk cascade, analogous to platelets [ 35 ]. Syk activation in these phagocytes initiates the actin remodeling and “phagocytic synapse” formation needed for engulfing opsonized platelets [ 35 , 51 ]. Among the molecules recruited downstream, BTK plays a key role by activating Rho-family GTPases (Rac/Rho) to drive actin polymerization; BTK is required for efficient phagocytosis of antibody-coated platelets [ 35 ]. Thus, Syk/BTK signaling in macrophages leads to platelet internalization and also cytokine release that can exacerbate inflammation [ 35 ]. In essence, there are two phases to the Syk–BTK mediated pathology: an early platelet-activation phase and a subsequent platelet-clearance phase . In the activation-driven phase, robust ITAM signaling (via Syk/BTK) causes platelets to aggregate and form microthrombi; platelets may be consumed within clots, contributing to thrombosis [ 39 ]. Consistently, co-activation of platelet FcγRIIA and thrombin receptors produces highly prothrombotic, coated platelets that support fibrin formation [ 30 ]. In the clearance-driven phase, the same Syk/BTK pathway marks platelets for opsonization and ingestion by phagocytes, causing thrombocytopenia [ 45 , 52 ]. For instance, Syk-mediated signaling in monocytes upregulates phagocytic machinery and can induce TF, linking platelet opsonization to coagulation activation [ 53 ]. The balance of these processes may vary by disease, but both contribute: platelets are not only destroyed by phagocytes but also hyperactivated to promote clotting. Syk and BTK sit at this intersection, making them attractive therapeutic targets to interrupt both platelet activation and immune-mediated destruction [ 54 ].

### Monocyte/Macrophage Fcγ receptors & reticuloendothelial clearance

IgG autoantibodies bound to platelets are recognized by Fcγ receptors (FcγRs) on phagocytes, especially splenic macrophages (and to some extent hepatic Kupffer cells), leading to ADCP of the platelets [ 34 , 55 – 57 ]. This FcγR-mediated reticuloendothelial clearance causes a reduction in circulating platelets and the formation of platelet–antibody immune complexes [ 58 , 59 ]. Such immune complexes can circulate (as ultralarge complexes in HIT, for example) and further activate effector cells [ 59 , 60 ]. Engagement of monocyte FcγRIIA by opsonized platelets triggers an inflammatory procoagulant response: monocytes upregulate TF expression and release TF-positive microparticles upon FcγR activation [ 30 , 44 , 45 , 61 ]. The induced TF drives thrombin generation, linking the autoimmune platelet destruction to thrombosis [ 30 , 59 ]. Monocyte-derived TF can also feed back on platelets; thrombin and TF released from monocytes activate platelets via PAR-1, promoting the formation of prothrombotic “coated” platelets [ 62 – 65 ]. Notably, while the spleen is the primary site of platelet clearance, hepatic involvement can modulate disease severity [ 34 , 55 ]. In certain cases, antibody-opsonized platelets are sequestered in the liver (by Kupffer cells or hepatocytes), and predominant hepatic clearance has been associated with more rapid, refractory thrombocytopenia unresponsive to splenectomy [ 55 ]. This dichotomy between splenic versus hepatic clearance may influence the extent of platelet count reduction and the clinical response to therapies in ITP. ### Complement–Platelet crosstalk

Immune complexes formed in ITP can robustly activate the complement system. In particular, platelet factor 4 (PF4)/polyanion–IgG complexes (such as those in HIT or VITT) potently trigger the classical complement pathway via C1q binding [ 59 , 66 – 68 ]. Complement activation leads to C3 and C5 cleavage, generating the opsonin C3b and the anaphylatoxins C3a and C5a [ 59 , 65 ]. C5a recruits neutrophils and monocytes to sites of immune complex deposition and amplifies inflammation by upregulating monocyte tissue factor (TF) expression and enhancing neutrophil activation and NETosis [ 62 – 65 , 69 ]. Activated platelets further propagate complement activation: surface P-selectin can initiate the alternative pathway, while platelet-exposed C1q receptors engage the classical pathway [ 65 ]. Excessive activation results in C5b-9 (membrane attack complex, MAC) formation. Sublytic C5b-9 deposition induces platelet and endothelial activation rather than lysis, promoting granule secretion, shedding of procoagulant microparticles, phosphatidylserine exposure, and enhanced thrombotic potential [ 70 ]. Complement activation also promotes platelet clearance. C3b and iC3b deposited on platelets mediate interactions with complement receptor 3 (CR3; CD11b/CD18) and CR4 on macrophages and neutrophils, acting synergistically with FcγRs to accelerate phagocytosis [ 59 , 65 , 70 ]. In some patients, complement-fixing autoantibodies directly induce platelet injury or lysis through MAC formation, contributing to thrombocytopenia beyond FcγR-mediated mechanisms [ 34 , 71 ]. Physiologically, platelets and endothelial cells are protected by complement regulators, including CD55, CD59, and Factor H binding to GPIIb-IIIa, limiting autologous complement attack [ 65 , 72 ]. If these regulators are absent or overwhelmed, complement damage goes unchecked: for example, in paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (CD55/CD59 deficiency), unbridled MAC formation on blood cells leads to intravascular cell lysis and a high risk of thrombosis [ 65 ]. Consistently, clinical studies show that platelet complement activation correlates with ITP severity and that inhibition of the classical pathway, such as anti-C1s monoclonal antibody therapy, can reduce platelet destruction in refractory ITP [ 68 ]. ### Biomarkers & functional readouts (Bridging Box)

Anti-PF4 antibody tests and platelet activation assays are key in diagnosing and studying HIT/VITT. Anti-PF4 ELISAs detect IgG antibodies against PF4 complexes [ 73 ]. This immunoassay is very sensitive but not fully specific [ 74 ], so a positive ELISA supports the diagnosis but usually requires confirmatory testing. Functional assays (e.g., the Serotonin Release Assay, SRA, or heparin-induced platelet activation test, HIPA) demonstrate the ability of patient antibodies to activate platelets [ 75 ]. The radioactive ^14 C-SRA is the gold-standard confirmatory test for HIT [ 74 ]. Newer flow cytometry-based functional assays, such as the PF4-dependent P-selectin expression assay (PEA), have shown high accuracy (AUC ≈ 0.94) comparable to SRA [ 74 ], providing more rapid and convenient results in practice [ 74 ].

Platelet activation markers are measured by flow cytometry to gauge platelet degranulation and aggregation propensity. Upon activation, platelets translocate P-selectin (CD62P) from α-granules to the surface [ 76 ]. Concomitantly, the fibrinogen receptor GPIIb/IIIa undergoes a conformational change to a high-affinity state that can be detected by PAC-1 monoclonal antibody binding [ 77 ]. Elevated P-selectin exposure and PAC-1 binding on platelets indicate robust platelet activation [ 75 , 77 ]. Procoagulant platelets – a subset of highly activated platelets – expose negatively charged phospholipids (phosphatidylserine) on their outer membrane. These are identified by annexin V binding with high affinity to phosphatidylserine [ 75 ]. HIT/VITT patient sera can induce the formation of abundant procoagulant platelets in vitro [ 78 ]. This procoagulant platelet generation is a hallmark of strong FcγRIIa-mediated platelet activation [ 26 , 79 – 81 ], contributing to thrombin generation and clot propagation in patients.

Complement and NETosis markers provide additional insight into the downstream inflammatory-thrombotic cascade. Severe PF4-antibody reactions often trigger complement activation, measurable by elevated plasma C3a, C5a, and sC5b-9 (terminal complement complex) levels [ 82 ]. In VITT, for example, patients’ acute-phase plasma shows high sC5b-9 as a sign of complement system activation (with levels tapering at follow-up) [ 82 ]. Concurrently, intense neutrophil activation, accompanied by NETosis, is observed. Markers of NET formation, such as circulating cell-free DNA, citrullinated histone H3, and myeloperoxidase–DNA complexes, are significantly elevated in patients with HIT and VITT [ 79 ]. These NETs not only indicate fulminant neutrophil activation [ 73 , 79 ] but also amplify thrombosis: PF4–IgG immune complexes induce NET release [ 82 , 83 ], and the DNA released by NETosis can, in turn, activate complement pathways [ 73 ], creating a self-amplifying prothrombotic cycle. All of these laboratory biomarkers – anti-PF4 antibodies, platelet activation markers (P-selectin, PAC-1), procoagulant platelet measurements (annexin V binding), complement split products, and NETosis markers – form an integrated toolkit to bridge clinical observations with the underlying immunothrombotic mechanisms [ 73 , 74 , 79 ]. Each readout provides a piece of the puzzle, helping researchers and clinicians characterize the immune-mediated platelet activation and thrombo-inflammatory milieu in conditions like HIT and VITT.

In this context, VITT represents an informative human model of anti-PF4–mediated immunothrombosis, sharing key pathogenic mechanisms with aHIT, including FcγRIIa-dependent platelet activation and thromboinflammatory amplification. Importantly, this mechanistic overlap helps contextualize several biomarker readouts discussed in this section. The PF4-dependent P-selectin expression assay shows diagnostic performance comparable to SRA (AUC ≈ 0.94). In VITT, anti-PF4 IgG drives a PF4-enhanced procoagulant platelet phenotype (P-selectin upregulation with phosphatidylserine exposure), and patient IgG can trigger FcγRIIa-dependent NETosis and thrombus formation in vivo, reinforcing the value of integrating platelet activation, procoagulant markers, and NET metrics when interpreting severe anti-PF4 syndromes [ 74 ].

In HIT/VITT, anti-PF4 immunoassays (ELISA or rapid tests) are used as quick screening tools: they have high sensitivity but low specificity (many false positives) [ 75 , 84 , 85 ]. Notably, in VITT cases, ELISA remains sensitive, whereas rapid immunoassays often miss the pathogenic antibodies [ 86 ]. Conversely, functional platelet-activation assays (including serotonin-release or HIPA) confirm true pathogenic antibodies with very high specificity (the clinical gold standard) but require specialized laboratories and often take days to run [ 75 , 87 ]. Flow-cytometry tests of platelet activation markers (such as P‐selectin, annexin V) can detect activation more rapidly [ 87 , 88 ], but they still demand specialized equipment and validation. Complement or NETosis biomarkers (including C3 fragments or citrullinated histones) correlate with severe anti-PF4 thrombotic events [ 89 ] but remain investigational. Thus, no single assay is definitive; in practice, a stepwise strategy (screen by immunoassay, then confirm positives by functional or flow assays) is used to balance diagnostic accuracy, turnaround time, and feasibility [ 75 , 88 ].

## Preclinical and early-phase studies

### Overview of preclinical evidence supporting FcγRIIa, BTK, Syk, and complement inhibitors

Recent studies indicate that targeting specific biological pathways with axis-targeted therapies can minimize treatment side effects and enhance outcomes [ 90 – 92 ]. BTK inhibitors are categorized into two types: irreversible and reversible. A study indicated that applying irreversible BTKis, such as Zanubrutinib, resulted in mild bleeding, whereas reversible BTKis, like Rilzabrutinib, did not show this effect in clinical trials [ 93 ]. Zanubrutinib (Brukinsa) is a potent, highly selective irreversible BTK inhibitor first approved for MCL (Mantle cell lymphoma) in 2019, and has been shown to inhibit platelet aggregation stimulated by FcγRIIa in blood [ 94 ]. Currently, Zanubrutinib is under investigation in Phase 2 clinical trials for primary ITP. Orelabrutinib is another highly selective irreversible BTK inhibitor with potential value for ITP [ 95 ], which is ongoing in phase 2 for primary/refractory ITP patients. In preclinical research [ 96 ], Orelabrutinib has demonstrated impressive suppression of B-cell activation and alleviates thrombocytopenia in active ITP murine models. Rilzabrutinib, a reversible BTK inhibitor, is under investigation in phase 2 and 3 trials for ITP. Rilzabrutinib has demonstrated high affinity and selectivity for the BTK [ 97 , 98 ]. Moreover, no reports of bruising or any effects on platelet counts in the normal range [ 99 ].

Fostamatinib is the primary SyK inhibitor and the only approved one [ 100 ]. This molecule converts to its active metabolite, R406 [ 101 ]. Generally, patients suffering from ITP are susceptible to thromboembolic occurrences; however, cases receiving Fostamatinib displayed a lower incidence rate of thromboembolic events, compared to traditional therapies [ 102 ]. Due to Fostamatinib blocking only three major pathways to aggregate platelets (FcγRIIA, GPVI, and CLEC-2 receptors), and has no effects on other hemostasis platelet receptors, it prevents undesirable bleeding [ 103 ]. Moreover, Sovleplenib and cevidoplenib are new SyKis under investigation for ITP.

FcγRIIa(CD32) receptors have a crucial role in immune issues [ 33 ]. BTKis and SyKis, via blocking the FcγRIIa signaling pathways, could alleviate the condition. The irreversible and reversible BTKis, Zanubrutinib and Fenebrutinib, inhibit platelet aggregation in blood triggered by FcγRIIa in HIT [ 94 ]. Also, R406 demonstrated in in-vitro that it prevented platelet aggregation, FcγRIIa-stimulated, induced by HIT [ 104 ].

The complement system is a key link between innate and adaptive immune systems. Potential autoantibodies activate the complement system via the classical pathway, resulting in immunopathological disorders like ITP [ 105 ]. Sutimlimab, a humanized monoclonal antibody that avoids classical pathway activation, enhances platelet count in refractory ITP patients [ 68 ]. Notably, the side effects have been reported as mild. Additionally, the long-term safety profile of Sutimlimab in patients with chronic ITP was satisfactory [ 106 ].

### Key animal model studies and findings

There are animal studies that report the importance and functional influence of BTK, SyK, and complement inhibitors in autoimmune-mediated diseases. Ringheim et al. had a detailed description of BTKis animal models for autoimmune conditions like RA (Rheumatoid Arthritis), SLE (Systemic Lupus Erythematosus), MS (Multiple Sclerosis), and ITP [ 107 ]. For instance, Orelabrutinib administration in mice demonstrated that it remarkably affected the platelet count increase [ 108 ]. Additionally, research has elucidated that pretreating the ITP-induced via antibodies in mouse models with Rilzabrutinib brought about a prompt decrease in platelet obliteration, contingent upon dosage, which constructively prevents the mice from ITP [ 98 ]. It is worthwhile to mention that in an animal study, mice without FcγRIIa did not respond to HIT immune complexes and did not develop thrombocytopenia; whereas, transgenic mice with FcγRIIa were affected by the condition [ 41 , 109 ]. This study emphasizes the FcγRIIa role in ITP, and also implicitly hints that FcγRIIa inhibitors could be analyzed in in vivo situation.

SyKis, like BTKis, also have an acceptable effect range in autoimmune-mediated disease [ 110 , 111 ]. SyKis prevent platelet activation in a mouse model of HIT through the FcγRIIa pathway [ 112 ]. In a study, it is described that induced ITP by antibodies in mice illustrated that those who received Fostamatinib, pretreated or treated, were noticeably resistant to developing thrombocytopenia [ 113 ]. In another study, a novel selective SyKis, Bl1002494, in a mouse model demonstrates a remarkable prevention of arterial thrombosis and cerebral infarct through GPVI and CLEC-2 signaling pathways without interfering with bleeding time [ 114 ]. Although this study was not specifically directed towards ITP, it is essential to consider the importance of inhibited pathways in the context of ITP.

Animal models have elucidated key mechanisms of aHIT. For example, FoxP3 mice spontaneously produce PF4/heparin–specific IgG that activate FcγRIIA-expressing platelets in a PF4-dependent manner [ 115 ]. In another model, mice immunized with human HIT IgG generate anti–anti-idiotypic antibodies that function like HIT antibodies; when these mice receive heparin, their platelet counts drop significantly [ 116 ]. These data confirm that dysregulated IgG production and FcγRIIA signaling drive aHIT-like thrombocytopenia. Translating to humans, early trials of FcRn inhibitors (which increase IgG catabolism) in refractory ITP showed promise: in a phase II study of efgartigimod, ~ 38.5% of treated patients achieved an IWG-defined platelet response (versus 16.7% on placebo) [ 117 ]. These approaches target pathogenic anti-PF4 IgG and platelet activation directly. Preliminary ITP data support efficacy, but no studies have tested these agents in aHIT, so their benefit in aHIT remains uncertain.

### Initial phase I human trials: safety profiles and initial efficacy indicators

In a study [ 118 ], the safety and efficacy of Zanubrutinib in CLL (Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia) were evaluated. The study comprised two parts: dose escalation and cohort expansion. In the first part, the most seen events were URT infection, contusion, cough and diarrhea, which also presented in the latter. The majority of the adverse events (AEs) were in grade 1 or 2. The grade 3/4 events occurred only in the cohort part and were low, such as anemia, neutropenia. Additionally, the study showcases the encouraging activity of Zanubrutinib in both treatment-naive and relapsed/refractory patients. Also, in an 11-month study [ 119 ], Zanubrutinib was assessed regarding safety and efficacy in corticosteroid-resistant/relapsed ITP. 22 patients out of the 40 enrolled reached acceptable results. A total of 12 patients have received a medication before. The most common AEs were mild, including URT infection, diarrhea, nausea, and abdominal distention. Furthermore, another investigation [ 120 ], looked at four patients with relapsed/refractory ITP who had not responded to earlier treatments or splenectomy before starting Zanubrutinib. The data showed that platelet counts (PC) maintained above 50 × 10 9 /L for three of these patients were stable for 1045, 390, and 334 days.

In a 24-week study [ 19 ], Rilzabrutinib was tested for safety and effectiveness. In this study, 60 patients with an average PC of 15 × 10 9 /L had an average of 6.3 years of ITP experience. Before taking Rilzabrutinib, patients had had different therapies like glucocorticoids, TPO-Ras, IVIG, and splenectomy. 40% of patients demonstrated the primary response in this trial. Interestingly, 40% (18/45) of patients commencing treatment with the highest dose also demonstrated an acceptable response. The most AEs included grade1/2: diarrhea, nausea and fatigue. Also, one grade 1 contusion and one grade 2 erysipelas were reported. Based on these findings of this study, another study was conducted to assess safety and efficacy of Rilzabrutinib at the highest dose for long-term therapy. In this study [ 121 ], of 26 patients with an average of 10.3 years of ITP who underwent six prior ITP treatments, and an average PC of 13 × 10 9 /L, only 9 patients showed an acceptable response over the 24 weeks. Moreover, the most common AEs were grade 1: diarrhea, headache and nausea. Only one patient experienced a grade 3 increase in serum creatinine phosphokinase level.

In a 27-month study [ 122 ], Fostamatinib was surveyed in 138 patients for safety and effectiveness. The average time that patients were diagnosed with ITP was 51 months, and they had been through four therapies on average before. Statistics demonstrated that 79% of patients were detected as responders in the study. Notably, 83 patients who only received Fostamatinib attained a high response rate (85.4%). Patients typically saw an improvement in platelet counts within 11 days. The AEs were mild, rated as grade 1 or 2, with diarrhea and hypertension being the most common. Due to grade 3/4 AEs, 13 patients were coerced into ceasing the therapy.

A study evaluated the safety and effectiveness of Sutimlimab in a two-part trial [ 68 ]. In part 1, 12 patients who did not respond well to at least two previous treatments were enrolled. After the first dose, their platelet count improved quickly within 24 h; 54 × 10 9 /L. 5 of 12 patients (42%) maintained PC level during part 1. However, after a washout period, PC levels returned to the baseline rate. In part 2, which was longer than the first, after administering Sutimlimab, PC level rapidly increased and mainly held above 50 × 10 9 /L, at times above 100 × 10 9 /L. ## Clinical development: phase II and III trials

The transition from mechanistic insight and early-phase studies to late-stage clinical evaluation is a defining step in developing novel therapies for IMT. While preclinical evidence and initial human trials have highlighted the therapeutic promise of BTK, Syk, and complement inhibition, their true clinical relevance is established through phase II and III trials. These studies not only assess efficacy in restoring platelet counts and reducing bleeding risk but also provide crucial data on safety, durability of response, and limitations that shape clinical applicability.

This section reviews important late-stage trials of BTK inhibitors, such as Rilzabrutinib and Zanubrutinib, which affect platelet activation driven by FcγRIIa. We also discuss Syk inhibition, focusing on Fostamatinib, the first approved drug for chronic ITP. Lastly, we explore Sutimlimab in complement-targeted therapy, offering a new method to reduce antibody-mediated platelet clearance. By analyzing the data across these agents, this section provides an integrated perspective on how axis-targeted therapies shape the clinical landscape of aHIT and related disorders.

### BTK inhibitors, phase II and III clinical trials

BTKis were first introduced with Ibrutinib, an irreversible covalent agent that revolutionized the treatment of B-cell malignancies, approved in 2013, but was limited by off-target toxicities such as bleeding and atrial fibrillation [ 126 ]. Acalabrutinib in 2017 and Zanubrutinib in 2023 were developed as next-generation irreversible inhibitors to improve selectivity and reduce off-target binding and a more favorable safety profile, particularly regarding cardiac and gastrointestinal adverse events [ 127 , 128 ]. Furthermore, Tirabrutinib in March 2023 and Orelabrutinib in April 2023 presented with enhanced kinase selectivity [ 129 , 130 ]. Also in August 2025, Rilzabrutinib, a reversible covalent BTK inhibitor, was presented specifically for autoimmune diseases such as ITP to minimize platelet dysfunction and bleeding risk [ 131 ]. The latest advance, Pirtobrutinib (LOXO-305), a non-covalent, reversible BTK inhibitor that retains activity against C481-mutated BTK, a common resistance mechanism to earlier agents [ 132 ]. In total, these agents illustrate an evolution from broad cancer treatments to more targeted and safer options. In cases of autoimmune thrombocytopenia, Rilzabrutinib [ 121 ] and Zanubrutinib [ 120 ] have effectively raised platelet counts, each with different safety issues. This highlights how improvements in how drugs bind, their selectivity, and reversibility can lead directly to better treatment results [ 133 , 134 ].

According to the ClinicalTrials.gov database, four studies are investigating the efficacy of Zanubrutinib in treating primary thrombocytopenia; one is completed, and the other are still under investigation. In a study conducted in Beijing, China, Dr. Zhang and colleagues explored a phase II trial involving a sample of 20 consenting adults of all genders, all of whom had confirmed primary refractory ITP, PCs below 30 × 10 9 /L, or were experiencing bleeding symptoms. The study involved a group of participants (10 patients) who received 80 mg of Zanubrutinib orally once daily for six weeks, followed by 20 weeks of safety follow-up. It indicated that Zanubrutinib is well-tolerated and effective, with only minor ADs observed, such as upper respiratory infections [ 135 ].

Another interventional phase II study, “A Prospective, One-arm and Open Clinical Study of Zanubrutinib in the Treatment of ITP” ( NCT05214391 ) is being performed and is expected to be completed by the end of 2025. This open-label, multicenter study includes 30 adults. It aims to assess the efficacy, safety, and pharmacokinetics of Zanubrutinib in restoring platelet counts in adults with ITP who have failed prior therapies. The primary endpoint is overall response, defined as achieving PCs ≥ 30 × 10⁹/L and at least doubling baseline levels without rescue treatment. Secondary endpoints include response durability, response time, bleeding events, and safety outcomes. By targeting BTK signaling and FcγRIIa-mediated platelet activation, Zanubrutinib is expected to provide a novel therapeutic option for ITP patients resistant to conventional treatments [ 136 ].

Also, as mentioned in the previous section, in recent research [ 120 ], four patients who had failed multiple therapies such as splenectomy, corticosteroids, IVIG, rituximab, and thrombopoietin receptor agonists, received 80 mg of Zanubrutinib twice daily. Except one patient who died due to an intracranial hemorrhage after discontinuation of treatment, the other three demonstrated an acceptable, durable response with Zanubrutinib. Noticeably, Zanubrutinib was well-tolerated, and no renal or hepatic toxicities were recorded.

Rilzabrutinib is another BTKi being studied under various research to indicate its efficacy and safety. The recent one conducted by the Sanofi company, which has the brand name “Wayrilz”, claims that it is the first BTKi approved in the USA for ITP therapy. A phase III clinical trial (LUNA 4) designed to assess the efficacy of 400 mg Rilzabrutinib twice daily orally for eligible patients and it is arranged that be done in 3 steps: a screening period (up to 8 weeks), a primary analysis period (up to 28 weeks), a long-term extension period for selected participants (28 weeks) and a 24-week follow-up period only for eligible participants [ 137 ]. In fact, LUNA 4 is a complementary study for LUNA 3 to improve its understanding and outcomes. LUNA 3 was a clinical trial designed by the Sanofi company and claimed being global, randomized, double-blind, and placebo-controlled. Eligible patients were randomized into Rilzabrutinib and placebo groups. The primary endpoint is the proportion of patients achieving a durable platelet response, defined as achieving ≥ 50 × 10⁹/L PCs for a majority of assessments without rescue therapy. Secondary endpoints include time to platelet response, incidence of bleeding events, safety, tolerability, and quality-of-life measures [ 18 ]. By targeting BTK-mediated FcγReceptor signaling, Rilzabrutinib is expected to reduce immune-mediated platelet destruction and provide a novel therapeutic option for patients with difficult-to-treat ITP. BTK inhibitors act on both B cells and myeloid cells, dampening autoantibody production and FcγR-mediated platelet phagocytosis [ 19 , 138 ]. Rilzabrutinib – an oral reversible BTK inhibitor – has shown encouraging results in immune thrombocytopenia: in a phase I/II trial, about 40% of heavily pretreated ITP patients achieved platelet counts ≥ 30 × 10^9/L at 24 weeks, with durable responses [ 138 , 139 ]. In the pivotal LUNA-3 phase III study, rilzabrutinib produced significantly higher stable platelet responses and fewer bleeding events than placebo [ 19 , 138 , 139 ]. Similarly, zanubrutinib (a highly selective BTK inhibitor) yielded an overall response (≥ 2-fold platelet increase to ≥ 30 × 10^9/L) in 55% of corticosteroid-resistant ITP patients in a phase II trial [ 140 ]. These results establish BTK blockade as effective in antibody-mediated thrombocytopenia. Since aHIT is driven by FcγRIIA-dependent platelet activation, BTK inhibitors directly interrupt this pathway. The positive ITP data (40–55% response rates) suggest potential utility in aHIT, but no clinical data exist specifically in aHIT (remains to be studied).

### SyK inhibitors: phase II and III clinical study evaluation

Among the newly developed active pharmaceutical ingredients for ITP treatment, SYK inhibitors, including Fostamatinib, Solveplenib (HMPL-523), and Cevidoplenib (SKI-O-703), are currently under clinical investigation for their efficacy and safety. To date, Fostamatinib has been the most studied of these agents. For instance, an Italian foundation (GIMEMA) in 2023 designed and conducted a retrospective and prospective multicenter observational study evaluating Fostamatinib’s real-world effectiveness and safety in 95 adult patients with chronic ITP refractory to previous treatments. The study included patients who had received at least one dose of Fostamatinib between October 1, 2021, and April 1, 2023, outside of interventional clinical trials at participating centers. Each participant was to be followed for at least six months, up to October 1, 2023. The study was completed in 2025, but its results have not yet been published [ 141 ]. Rigel Pharmaceuticals is conducting a different study to evaluate patient characteristics, treatment patterns, clinical results, and safety of Fostamatinib as a second-line therapy for adult ITP patients with an inadequate response to previous treatment (steroids ± immunoglobulins). This real-world, two-cohort study aims to enroll about 45 participants. Cohort 1 will include approximately 30 patients initiating fostamatinib as second-line therapy. In comparison, Cohort 2 will consist of approximately 15 patients who have already received at least 12 weeks of Fostamatinib as second-line therapy and plan to continue treatment at the time of enrollment [ 142 ].

Furthermore, Kuwana et al. designed and operated two phase III trials to assess the efficacy, safety, and tolerability of fostamatinib in this patient population under real-world conditions. The results confirmed that Fostamatinib increased platelet counts in a meaningful proportion of treated patients and had an acceptable safety profile, with adverse events consistent with the drug’s known risks. The authors conclude that Fostamatinib is a viable treatment option for Japanese ITP patients refractory to prior therapies, supporting its broader use and further study in diverse populations [ 143 , 144 ].

Fostamatinib is a promising treatment option for patients with chronic ITP, especially for those who have not responded well to other therapies. It effectively increases platelet counts and lowers the risk of bleeding, all while maintaining a manageable safety profile. This highlights its importance in the evolving management of ITP [ 13 ]. Ongoing studies and long-term data will further clarify its role in therapy and explore its potential use in other autoimmune conditions.

Cevidoplenib and Solveplenib are other Syk inhibitors that are under investigation for their role in ITP patients. A study conducted by Oscotec Inc. focused on patients with persistent and chronic ITP who had previously failed to respond to treatment or relapsed after therapy, with a platelet count of less than 30,000/µL. Patients were randomly allocated to 2 groups with two doses of Cevidoplenib, 200 mg and 400 mg, and a placebo; all administered orally twice daily. The results indicated that platelet response is over 30,000/µL in 12 weeks, while minor adverse effects have been observed. The severe adverse effects were that 2 out of 61 participants received 400 mg of Cevidoplenib BID, and improvements in the ITP bleeding scale. Overall, this trial adequately indicated the efficacy and safety of this active pharmaceutical ingredient [ 145 ].

A Phase III clinical study has been conducted by Hutchison Medipharma, USA. This interventional, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial involved 272 adult patients with ITP. The aim was to determine whether HMPL-523 (Sovleplenib) is safe and effective for treating ITP. The results have not yet been published. However, other studies, such as one conducted by Hu and colleagues in China, have shown positive results, with the primary endpoints being met. In their findings, the platelet counts significantly increased in the treatment group compared to the placebo group, which experienced a decrease in platelet count [ 146 ].

Syk is essential for FcγRIIA signaling in antibody-mediated platelet clearance. Fostamatinib is an oral Syk inhibitor approved for chronic ITP. In two phase III trials (FIT1 and FIT2), fostamatinib significantly improved platelet counts: stable platelet responses (≥ 50 × 10^9/L on ≥ 4 of 6 visits) occurred in 18% of fostamatinib-treated patients versus 2% on placebo ( P = 0.0003) [ 13 ]. By retrospective criteria, 43% of patients on fostamatinib had at least one platelet count ≥ 50 × 10^9/L within 12 weeks, versus 14% on placebo ( P = 0.0006) [ 13 ]. Fostamatinib was generally well-tolerated, and responders included those refractory to splenectomy, rituximab, and TPO-agonists. Blocking Syk directly inhibits FcγRIIA-driven platelet activation, the core mechanism in aHIT. The ITP trials confirm that Syk inhibition can reverse antibody-mediated thrombocytopenia, implying benefit in aHIT. However, no aHIT-specific studies have been reported, so efficacy in aHIT is unproven.

### Complement inhibitors phase II and III clinical trials

Complement-targeted therapies are emerging as a new approach in the management of ITP. These therapies aim to interrupt the destruction of platelets caused by antibodies by inhibiting the complement cascade. The most advanced agents in this category include C1s inhibitors such as Sutimlimab (Enjaymo) and SAR445088 (Riliprubart), which are designed to block the classical complement pathway at an early stage. Sutimlimab has already received approval for the treatment of cold agglutinin disease (CAD) following the phase III CARDINAL and CADENZA trials. It has shown rapid and sustained control of hemolysis, leading to improved hemoglobin levels and reduced fatigue. The selective mechanism of C1s blockade presents a biological rationale for its use in ITP, especially for patients who experience complement-mediated platelet destruction. Early phase I/II data from studies involving refractory ITP patients showed rapid recovery of PCs and acceptable safety profiles, supporting the need for larger randomized trials [ 68 ]. SAR445088 is a next-generation C1s inhibitor that selectively targets the activated C1 pathway. It has demonstrated durable complement suppression and hematologic improvement in complement-associated diseases (CAD) [ 147 ] and is currently undergoing phase II evaluation for immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP) ( NCT04669600 ), although published efficacy data are still limited.

While complement inhibition presents a promising and logical approach for treating complement-positive ITP, the current evidence is limited to small, non-randomized studies. Future research should focus on patient stratification using Complement biomarkers, conduct controlled phase III trials, and assess long-term safety to determine the true role of Complement inhibitors in the overall treatment landscape for ITP [ 105 , 148 ].

Evidence increasingly supports a role for complement in immune thrombocytopenias. In a study, sutimlimab (a C1s inhibitor) rapidly increased platelet counts in refractory ITP: mean platelet count rose from ~ 25 to ~ 54 × 10^9/L after the first dose, and 42% of patients achieved a durable response (≥ 50 × 10^9/L on ≥ 50% of follow-up visits) [ 68 ]. More broadly, complement blockade has shown efficacy in autoimmunity: avacopan (a C5a receptor antagonist) matched high-dose steroids in ANCA vasculitis, inducing remission in 72.3% of patients at 26 weeks (versus 70.1% on steroids) and superior sustained remission at 52 weeks (65.7% vs. 54.9%, P = 0.007) [ 149 ]. These trials underscore that inhibiting classical or C5a-driven complement can ameliorate immune-mediated inflammation. Anti-PF4 antibodies can activate the classical complement pathway, so C1s or C5aR blockade could theoretically reduce platelet destruction and thrombosis in aHIT. The positive results with sutimlimab and avacopan in related disorders suggest potential, but no clinical trials have directly tested complement inhibitors in aHIT.

### Analysis of efficacy, safety, and limitations from these clinical trials

Across Phase II and III clinical trials, BTK, Syk, and complement inhibitors have demonstrated significant advances in addressing the multifactorial pathogenesis of ITP. BTK inhibitors such as Rilzabrutinib and Zanubrutinib consistently improved PCs with manageable safety profiles; however, mild bleeding, transient cytopenia, and upper respiratory infections remain common adverse events. Reversible covalent agents, such as Rilzabrutinib, offer an advantage over earlier irreversible inhibitors by reducing the risk of platelet dysfunction. In contrast, Syk inhibitors such as Fostamatinib exhibit sustained platelet recovery in refractory ITP, albeit with gastrointestinal and hepatic adverse effects that require monitoring. Next-generation agents like Solveplenib and Cevidoplenib aim to optimize selectivity and tolerability. Complement inhibitors, particularly Sutimlimab, provide a novel mechanism by targeting C1s-mediated platelet clearance, benefiting patients with complement-positive ITP subtypes, though data remain limited to smaller non-randomized studies. While these agents enhance therapeutic precision and safety, challenges persist regarding long-term durability, patient stratification, and cost-effectiveness, underscoring the need for biomarker-driven trials to tailor therapy and improve outcomes. ## Translational implications for autoimmune HIT

### From IMT trials to autoimmune HIT therapy

Translating therapies from related immune-mediated thrombocytopenias (ITP, APS) provides a roadmap for treating autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In ITP and APS, autoantibody‐driven platelet clearance and hypercoagulability share key pathways with aHIT (IgG anti‐PF4, FcγRIIa activation, complement). For example, syk inhibition with fostamatinib – which blocks FcγRIIa‐mediated phagocytosis – achieved durable platelet responses in heavily pretreated ITP patients [ 13 ]. Likewise, oral BTK inhibitors have raised platelet counts in chronic ITP (such as rilzabrutinib showed 40–55% response rates with low toxicity [ 19 , 140 ]). Notably, BTK inhibitors also abrogate FcγRIIa‐mediated platelet activation by HIT antibodies in vitro [ 94 ], suggesting they could directly prevent PF4-IgG–triggered aggregation in aHIT. Thus, interrupting the FcγRIIa–Syk/BTK axis is a rational strategy for aHIT. Another principle is reducing pathogenic IgG. FcRn blockade (such as efgartigimod) lowers autoantibody levels and significantly improves sustained platelet counts in chronic ITP compared to placebo [ 159 ]. By analogy, FcRn inhibitors might clear anti‐PF4 IgG in aHIT. Complementarily, high‐dose IVIG – already standard in aHIT – competitively blocks FcγRIIa, dampening platelet activation [ 160 ]. These approaches together target the antibody burden and its FcR engagement. Finally, complement plays a central role. In APS (another antibody‐mediated thrombophilia), complement blockade with eculizumab has controlled refractory thromboses [ 161 , 162 ]. In ITP, inhibiting the classical complement pathway (anti‐C1s) rapidly lifted platelet counts [ 68 ]. Given aHIT’s intense pro‐thrombotic state, adding complement inhibitors to anticoagulation could disrupt the complement‐amplified coagulation cascade. (Indeed, unlike classic HIT, simply stopping heparin is ineffective in aHIT [ 160 ], underscoring the need for immunologic intervention.)

### Limitations and gaps in direct evidence

Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) remains a poorly defined syndrome because evidence is limited to small series and case reports. Almost all published data derive from retrospective analyses: Warkentin’s recent review tabulated only ∼50 laboratory-confirmed aHIT patients in the literature [ 11 ], and a 2021 systematic review pooled merely 24 cases treated with IVIG [ 163 ]. No prospective cohorts or trials have ever been conducted specifically in aHIT, and standard HIT assays are usually unhelpful (most laboratories “do not test for aHIT antibodies,” contributing to under-diagnosis) [ 11 ]. Moreover, the serologic basis of aHIT is still provisional: recent data suggest that residual heparin in samples can artifactually convert an “autoimmune” HIT test to conventional HIT [ 164 ], and clinical severity in putative aHIT cases was similar to ordinary HIT [ 164 ]. In short, aHIT is defined by indirect criteria (heparin-independent PF4 antibodies) rather than by hard clinical trials. As one expert panel noted, HIT is rare and there is “limited high-quality evidence” to guide management of even its unusual subtypes [ 12 ]. Until larger, standardized studies are done, diagnoses will rely on specialized assays and extrapolations from related disorders [ 11 , 165 ]. In practical terms, therapeutic recommendations (for example, adding high-dose IVIG) are essentially empirical and based on analogies with classical HIT, reflecting a major knowledge gap in this field. ### Future research directions

To address these gaps, coordinated translational studies and trials are needed. First, diagnostic assays must be standardized. Laboratories should perform platelet-activation tests without heparin and collaborate on standardization of anti-PF4 assays [ 11 ]. Establishing multicenter registries of suspected aHIT cases would clarify its incidence and natural history (the true incidence of any HIT is ~ 0.065% of admissions [ 21 ], but aHIT’s frequency is unknown). Clarifying pathogenesis is another priority: for example, in vitro studies show that PF4 bound to polyanions like chondroitin sulfate can mimic the aHIT antigenic complex [ 166 ], and Khandelwal et al. recently demonstrated a major role for complement in amplifying HIT antibody effects [ 59 ]. Future research could include animal models of heparin‐independent anti-PF4 responses and structural studies of PF4 epitopes.

On the therapeutic side, novel immunomodulators merit investigation. Because aHIT appears to involve FcγR-mediated platelet activation, agents developed for ITP are promising candidates. Future research should explore inhibition of platelet 12-lipoxygenase (12-LOX), a key enzyme in arachidonic acid metabolism, as a complementary strategy for mitigating thrombotic complications in autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia. Experimental studies demonstrate that 12-LOX is required for Fc gamma receptor IIA–mediated platelet activation and immune thrombosis [ 167 ]. Moreover, the selective 12-LOX inhibitor ML355 has been shown to suppress immune complex–driven platelet activation and thrombosis without significantly impairing primary hemostasis [ 168 ]. Although ML355 remains experimental, targeting platelet lipid signaling pathways represents a promising adjunctive approach that warrants biomarker-guided translational and early-phase clinical investigation [ 169 ]. For instance, the Syk inhibitor fostamatinib (approved in chronic ITP) produced clinically meaningful platelet responses in refractory ITP patients [ 13 ], suggesting Syk blockade could dampen aHIT. Similarly, Bruton’s tyrosine kinase inhibitors and FcRn antagonists (which reduce pathogenic IgG) should be explored in ITP. Complement inhibitors (like C5 blockers) could be studied, given the evidence that complement drives thrombosis in HIT complexes [ 59 ]. Clinically, a formal study of adjunctive IVIG (already widely used in practice [ 11 ]) versus plasma exchange, and trials comparing different anticoagulants (including DOACs versus DTIs) would be valuable. Indeed, recent expert consensus already plans to harmonize definitions and guidelines for “autoimmune” and refractory HIT [ 12 ], underscoring the need for standardized protocols. In summary, bridging bench research (on PF4 immunity and effector pathways) with prospective clinical trials (for diagnostics and immunotherapies) will be key to advancing care for autoimmune HIT [ 22 , 165 ].

## Conclusion

Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT) represents a prototypic model of immunothrombosis, where anti-PF4 antibodies trigger both platelet destruction and activation, creating the paradoxical coexistence of thrombosis and thrombocytopenia. Despite its clinical severity, evidence-based therapies remain limited. Translational insights from related IMTs—particularly ITP and APS—highlight the central pathogenic nodes that can be therapeutically targeted. The FcγRIIa–Syk–BTK axis and the complement system emerge as dual drivers of platelet activation and clearance, and their inhibition has demonstrated clinical benefit in analogous diseases. BTK and Syk inhibitors such as rilzabrutinib, zanubrutinib, and fostamatinib have achieved durable platelet responses and reduced thromboinflammatory signaling, while complement blockade with agents like sutimlimab offers a novel means to halt immune complex–driven platelet destruction.

Collectively, these findings support a mechanism-based treatment paradigm for aHIT that extends beyond conventional anticoagulation. Combining non-heparin anticoagulants with immune-targeted agents could suppress both thrombosis and autoantibody-mediated platelet loss, potentially improving outcomes in this high-risk condition. Future clinical translation will depend on adaptive trials incorporating biomarker-guided patient selection, longitudinal immune monitoring, and safety validation. By bridging mechanistic understanding from ITP and APS to aHIT, this integrative strategy holds promise for transforming the management of antibody-driven thrombocytopenia into a precision immunotherapy framework.
