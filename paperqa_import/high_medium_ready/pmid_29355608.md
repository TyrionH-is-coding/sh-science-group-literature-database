# ABSTRACT

<!-- page 3 -->
Background: Antiphospholipid Syndrome (APS) is an autoimmune multifactorial disorder. Genetics is believed to play a contributory role in the pathogenesis of APS, especially in thrombosis development and pregnancy morbidity. In the last 20 years, extensive research on genetic contribution on APS indicates that APS is a polygenic disorder, where a number of genes are involved in the development of its clinical manifestations.

<!-- page 8 -->
Aims: The aim of this systematic review is to evaluate the genetic risk factors in thrombotic primary APS. Additionally, to assess the common molecular functions, biological processes, pathways, interrelations with the gene encoded proteins and RNA-Seq-derived expression patterns over different organs of the associated genes via bioinformatic analyses.

Methods: Without restricting the year, a systematic search of English articles was conducted (up to 4th September 2017) using Web of Science, PubMed, Scopus, ScienceDirect and Google Scholar databases. Eligible studies were selected based on the inclusion criteria. Two researchers independently extracted the data from the included studies. Quality assessment of the included studies was carried out using a modified New-Castle Ottawa scale (NOS).

<!-- page 10 -->
Results: From an initial search result of 2673 articles, 22 studies were included (1268 primary APS patients and 1649 healthy controls). Twenty-two genes were identified in which 16 were significantly associated with thrombosis in primary APS whereas six genes showed no significant association with thrombosis. Based on the NOS, 14 studies were of high quality while 6 were low quality studies. From the bioinformatic analyses, thrombin-activated receptor activity $(q = 6.77\times 10^{-7})$ , blood coagulation $(q = 2.63\times 10^{-15})$ , formation of fibrin clot $(q = 9.76\times 10^{-10})$ were the top hit for molecular function, biological process and pathway categories, respectively. With the highest confidence interaction score of 0.900, all of the thrombosis-associated gene encoded proteins of APS were found to be interconnected except for two. Based on the pathway analysis, cumulatively all the genes affect haemostasis [false discovery rate $\mathrm{(FDR)} = 1.01\times 10^{-8}]$ and the immune system $\mathrm{[FDR = 9.93\times 10^{-2}]}$ . Gene expression analysis from RNA-Seq data revealed that almost all the genes were expressed in 32 different tissues in the human body. Conclusion: According to our systematic review, 16 genes contribute significantly in patients with thrombotic primary APS when compared with controls. Bioinformatic analyses of these genes revealed their molecular interconnectivity in protein levels largely by affecting blood coagulation and immune system. These genes are expressed in 32 different organs and may pose higher risk of developing thrombosis anywhere in the body of primary APS patients.

# 1. Introduction

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a systemic autoimmune disease characterised by the presence of circulating antibodies such as lupus anticoagulant (LA), anticardiolipin (aCL) and anti-β2-glycoprotein I (β2GPI) antibodies to phospholipid binding proteins. There are two major clinical features of definite APS 1) thrombosis and 2) pregnancy morbidity [1, 2]. Thrombus can be formed in any organ in the body since it is generated in both arteries and veins [3]. APS is an idiopathic disease and the aetiology of the disease remains unknown. However, APS is a polygenic disorder where different factors contribute in the development of abnormal laboratory and clinical features. There are three major types of APS, 1) primary (clinical and laboratory features of APS are present with the absence of autoimmune disease); 2) APS-associated with other diseases [besides clinical and laboratory features, at least an autoimmune disease [i.e., systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is present]

and 3) catastrophic APS (the most aggressive mode of APS where multiple organs are affected and patients die due to multiple organ failure) [4]. Genetic aspects - majorly mediated by inappropriate activation of immune system have been believed to play important roles in the development of APS [5-7]. Familial cases of APS also indicate towards the involvement of genetics in the pathogenic development of APS [8, 9].

APS is one of the most common thrombophilic conditions observed in young adults where the presence of both arterial and venous thrombosis is reported [8, 10]. According to the Euro-Phospholipid project, thrombosis is the most frequent clinical manifestations (28.1%) for APS [11]. Approximately $55\%$ of APS patients suffer from venous thrombosis [i.e., deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE)] [12], while cerebrovascular accidents and transient ischemic attacks are the most common $(50\%)$ arterial thrombotic manifestations [13]. Thrombosis is considered as the major cause of death in catastrophic

APS $(55.6\%)$ where the development of thrombi occurs in multiple organs [11].

As there is no other autoimmune disease associated with primary APS, therefore, studying primary APS is unique since the genetics solely reflects the pathogenic mechanism of primary APS. Therefore, the objective of this systematic review was to identify the genes which contribute to the risk factors for thrombosis in primary APS. Subsequently, to assess the common molecular functions, biological processes, pathways, interrelations with the gene encoded proteins and RNA-Seq-derived expression patterns over different organs of the associated genes via bioinformatic analyses.

# 2. Methods

This systematic review was designed in accordance with the recommendations of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) Statement (Appendix A) [14].

# 2.1. Inclusion criteria

# 2.1.1. Type of studies

Published prospective case-control studies assessing the risk of the association of a particular gene in the development of thrombosis in patients with primary APS as compared to controls were considered eligible. Review articles, case reports, clinical trials and editorials were excluded. If a study from the same research group addressing the identical population was published as both meeting abstract and journal article, only the journal article was considered to avoid any overlapping data.

# 2.1.2. Type of participants

Primary APS patients (without any underlying autoimmune diseases; i.e., SLE) of any age, sex or ethnic group were considered eligible. Healthy controls (without any underlying autoimmune diseases) or APS patients without thrombosis or non-APS patients with thrombosis were considered as eligible comparators.

# 2.2. Search strategy

Literature search was done systematically using 'Advanced' and 'Expert' search strategies in Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed, ScienceDirect and Google Scholar databases. Boolean operators "AND" & "OR" were used to combine the following keywords: "antiphospholipid", "aPLs", "anticardiolipin", "lupus anticoagulant", "beta-2-glycoprotein", "β2-glycoprotein", "β2GPI", "mutation", "polymorphism", "gene", "genetic", "amplification", "deletion", "duplication", "translocation", "inversion", "insertion", "sequencing", "exome", "RNA-Seq", "transcriptome", "whole genome", "genome-wide association study", "GWAS" "microarray", "missense" and "nonsense" (Appendix B). To increase the sensitivity, the wild-card term "+" was used for the Web of Science, PubMed, Scopus and ScienceDirect databases.

All the electronic databases were independently searched by two researchers (MAI and FA). Discrepancies were resolved by discussing among all the researchers. Only English language literatures were searched without year restrictions with the search limited to human studies only. The final systematic search was conducted on 4th September 2017 (Fig. 1).

# 2.3. Quality assessment

Quality assessment of each of the included studies was evaluated following the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for case-control studies. We adopted a modified version of the NOS [15] on a 9-star model. Studies scoring above the median NOS value were considered as high quality (low risk of bias) and those scoring below the median value were considered as low quality (high risk of bias) [16].

# 2.4. Bioinformatic analyses

# 2.4.1. Functional enrichment analysis

To understand how the thrombotic genes contribute to the pathogenesis of APS, a comprehensive gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis (molecular functions, biological process, pathways and associated diseases) was performed using the ToppFun application from ToppGene Suite tool, based on functional annotations and protein interactions network [17] (https://toppgene.cchmc.org/) ( $p$ -value cut-off: 0.05; gene limits: $1 \leq n \leq 2000$ ). Controlling for multiple testing, a false discovery rate (FDR) Benjamini-Hochberg (B&H) of 0.05 ( $q$ -value) was used [18].

# 2.4.2. Molecular interaction analysis

Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes (STRING) database version 10.5 (http://string-db.org) [19] was used to analyse the strongest protein-protein interactions. Protein-protein interactions of the thrombosis-associated APS gene encoded proteins were evaluated via different types of molecular actions such as activation, inhibition, catalysis, reaction, binding, transcriptional regulation and post-transcriptional modification with the following stringency settings: highest confidence interaction score (0.900).

# 2.4.3. Pathway analysis

The pathway browser (version 3.5) tool of Reactome database (http://www.reactome.org) version v61 was used to perform overrepresentation analysis of the thrombosis-associated APS gene encoded protein expressions across biological pathways particularly in haemostasis and immunological pathways [20].

# 2.4.4. Gene expression analysis: RNA-Seq data

To identify the human-organs where the thrombosis-associated set of APS genes are expressed, the MBL-EBI Expression Atlas was used (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gxa/home). The RNA-seq analysis tool iRAP version 0.5.1p1 was used to analyse baseline messenger RNA (mRNA) gene expression data from RNA-seq of coding RNA from 122 human tissue samples representing 32 different organs. The read mapping was based on the genome reference (Ensembl release: 79) TopHat2 version 2.0.12.

# 3. Results

# 3.1. Selection of studies

By exploring PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, Google Scholar and Science Direct databases, a total of 2673 articles were retrieved by following the search strategy (Appendix B). In total, 1080 articles were excluded in the identification phase (non-human subjects, $n = 293$ ; review articles $n = 440$ ; case reports, $n = 251$ ; clinical trials, editorials and comments, $n = 96$ ) and the remaining 1593 articles were evaluated via abstract screening. Although 25 studies were eligible; three meeting abstracts were excluded due to overlapping data with the journal articles. Therefore, 22 studies were finally included (consisting of a total of 1268 primary APS patients and 1649 healthy controls) based on the objective and inclusion criteria of our systematic review (Fig. 1).

# 3.2. Characteristics of the included studies

Among the included studies, all of the 22 were prospective case-control studies, conducted in adult population and were journal articles [21-42]. Seventeen studies were conducted on European population across six different countries; Spain [26, 30, 32, 33, 35, 36, 41, 42], Turkey [22, 29, 31, 37], United Kingdom [38, 40], France [23], Greece [21], Russia [24] while the remaining five studies were from Mexico [28], Brazil [27], Argentina [39], Japan [25] and Australia [34]. For the confirmation of primary APS patients, 11 studies [21, 23-27, 29-32, 35] followed the 2006 Sydney classification criteria [2], five studies

# 3.3. Quality assessment

The risk of bias assessment of the included studies for case-control studies based on NOS is shown in Table 3. According to the NOS, the median score of the included studies was 7. Among the 22 studies, 14 studies which scored $\geq 7$ [23, 24, 26, 27, 29-33, 35-37, 39, 42] were considered as a high-quality study with low risk of bias. Eight of the

studies were considered as low-quality study with high-risk of bias since they scored $< 7$ [21, 22, 25, 28, 34, 38, 40, 41].

# 3.4. Bioinformatic analyses

# 3.4.1. Functional enrichment analysis

Based on the lowest $q$ -values (FDR B&H), thrombin-activated receptor activity $(q = 6.77 \times 10^{-7})$ , blood coagulation $(q = 2.63 \times 10^{-15})$ , formation of fibrin clot $(q = 9.76 \times 10^{-10})$ and autoimmune polyendocrinopathies $(1.51 \times 10^{-14})$ were the top hit for molecular functions, biological process, pathways and associated disease categories, respectively (Table 4).

# 3.4.2. Protein-protein interaction analysis

Based on the highest confidence interaction score (0.900), all of the thrombosis-associated gene encoded proteins of APS were inter-related except for two (PF4V1 and ITGA2). All of the 14 proteins were found to interact via activation, inhibition, catalysis, reaction, binding, transcriptional regulation and post-transcriptional modification (Fig. 2).

# 4.1.2.SELP (selectin $P$

P-selectin encoded by the gene SELP is a calcium $(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+})$ -dependent receptor molecule that binds to carbohydrates on neutrophils and monocytes. The soluble form of this molecule is generally found in the plasma which facilitates the interactions among endothelial cells, leukocytes and activated platelets. It mediates the interaction of activated endothelial cells or platelets with leukocytes as well as facilitates the enrolment of leukocytes to thrombi [49, 50]. One of the polymorphisms of SELP $(-825\mathrm{T / C})$ was confirmed as a risk factor of pulmonary hypertension in patients with congenital heart disease as compared to healthy controls $(p < 0.05)$ [51].

In a Turkish cohort of primary APS patients $(n = 40)$ , three single nucleotide polymorphisms (i.e., T715P, c.2363A > C; S290N, c.1087G > A and N562D, c.1902G > A) were detected within the coding region of SELP. Although there were no significant differences of S290N and T715P polymorphisms between primary APS patients and controls $(n = 40)$ , the frequency of N562D-DN and N562D-NN genotypes of N562D polymorphism were significantly higher in APS patients $(p = 0.003$ and $p = 0.004$ , respectively) as compared to the healthy controls [22]. In addition, the frequency of N562D-DN genotype was significantly higher in patients with primary APS with thrombosis compared with those without thrombosis $(p = .03)$ . Therefore, N562D-DN genotype of SELP is associated with higher risk of thrombosis in patients with primary APS.

# 4.1.3. TLR2 (toll like receptor 2)

TLR2 is a membrane receptor protein encoded by the gene TLR2. TLR2 is expressed on the surface of certain cells such as monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, polymorphonuclear leukocytes, B cells and T cells [52]. TLR2 primarily recognizes invading organisms bearing pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) such as lipopolysaccharides in addition to binding with a wide range of exogenous ligands (i.e., lipoproteins) and activating different cells of the immune system [53]. Subsequently, it induces the immune cells to secrete various inflammatory cytokines including tumour necrosis factor (TNF)- $\alpha$ and different ILs like IL-1 $\alpha$ , IL-1 $\beta$ , IL-6, IL-8 and IL-12 [54]. The involvement of TLR2 has been reported in many other autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis [55], SLE [56], Sjogren's syndrome [57], multiple sclerosis [58] and systemic sclerosis [59].

The involvement of TLR2 has also been observed in patients with primary APS. In a case-control study, Benhamou et al. [23] reported that the mRNA expressions, phosphorylated interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase 1 (IRAK 1; a major protein involved in the TLR transduction pathway) in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and cell-surface TLR2 levels (in monocytes) were significantly $(p < 0.05)$ increased in primary APS patients $(n = 22)$ experiencing thrombotic events compared to healthy controls $(n = 26)$ indicating enhanced activity of TLR2 in primary APS.

# 4.1.4. TLR4 (toll like receptor 4)

Various types of toll-like receptors (TLRs) have been identified and TLR4 is one of them that yields from the expression of TLR4 gene which is reported to play a fundamental role in pathogen recognition as well as activation of innate immunity via production of cytokines [60]. TLR4 is ubiquitous to many different cells and tissues. They help in recognizing the microbes and activate macrophages, monocytes, dendritic cells as part of innate immune system activation [52]. TLR4 is involved in the pathogenesis of different types of diseases especially autoimmune diseases such as SLE, psoriasis, multiple sclerosis, systemic sclerosis, autoimmune diabetes, Sjogren's syndrome and rheumatoid arthritis [54].

TLR4 is also associated with APS. A significantly higher mRNA expression of TLR4 and high levels of cell surface expression in circulating monocytes in addition to significantly elevated activation of TLR pathways $(p < 0.05)$ was observed among primary APS patients as compared to healthy controls [23]. In addition, a prothrombotic state was also observed since there was a decrease in nitrite levels with an increase in lipid peroxidation, TNF levels and tissue factor levels. Therefore, vascular thrombosis may appear in patients with primary APS via TLR pathway activation and TLR4 reactivity.

# 4.1.5. SERPINE1 (serpin family E member 1)

# 4.1.6. APOH (apolipoprotein H)

APOH (also known as $\beta 2GPI$ ) is a protein coding gene that encodes for apolipoprotein H (also known as $\beta 2$ -glycoprotein I). This protein usually binds to negatively charged substances (i.e., phospholipids and heparin) and may prevent the activation of intrinsic blood coagulation cascade when it binds with phospholipids on the surface of damaged cells. It is considered as one of the major autoantigens for the production of aPLs - involved in autoimmune diseases including SLE and APS [70-72].

Different APOH polymorphisms (i.e., Val247Leu and Trp316Ser) have been suggested to be significantly associated with thrombotic manifestation of primary APS. For instance, Prieto et al. [28] observed significantly higher frequencies of VV genotype (polymorphism at position 247 of the $\beta 2GPI$ gene) in Mexican primary APS patients $(n = 39)$ when compared with healthy controls [odds ratio $(\mathrm{OR}) = 3.94$ ; $95\%$ $\mathrm{CI} = 1.45 - 10.71$ ; $p = 0.005$ ]. Additionally, significantly higher frequencies of VV genotype was observed in primary APS patients with arterial thrombosis compared to healthy controls $(\mathrm{OR} = 7.89$ ; $95\%$ $\mathrm{CI} = 2.15 - 28.9$ ; $p = 0.003$ ) and primary APS patients without thrombosis $(\mathrm{OR} = 2.8$ ; $95\%$ $\mathrm{CI} = 1.39 - 5.65$ ; $p = 0.01$ ). On the other hand, V allele expression in patients with arterial thrombosis was significantly higher than that of healthy controls $(\mathrm{OR} = 3.33$ ; $95\%$ $\mathrm{CI} = 1.31 - 8.44$ ; $p = 0.012$ ). According to another study on Brazilian primary APS population, polymorphisms at 247 (Val247Leu) codon was again found to be significantly higher in patients experiencing both arterial and venous thrombosis than that of healthy

controls $(p < 0.0001)$ [27]. Later, Pardos-Gea et al., [26] reported that the TS genotype (polymorphism at position 316 of the $\beta 2GPI$ gene) was significantly higher in Spanish primary APS subjects as compared to the controls $(22.8\%$ vs $10\%$ ; $p = 0.02$ ). The expression of S allele was also significantly higher with respect to controls $(11.4\%$ vs $5\%$ , respectively, $p = 0.02$ ) which was further related with increased risk of thrombosis development. Therefore, it is suggested that gene polymorphisms of $\beta 2GPI$ at codons 247 (Val247Leu) and 316 (Trp316Ser) seem to influence the thrombotic pathogenesis of primary APS.

# 4.1.7. ITGA2 (integrin subunit alpha-2)

The ITGA2 gene (also known as GPIa) encodes for the alpha subunit (integrin subunit alpha 2) of a transmembrane receptor (integrin alpha-2/beta-1) which acts for collagens and related proteins (i.e., laminin, collagen, collagen C-propeptides, fibronectin and E-cadherin) [73]. Platelets play a major role in thrombosis development, the integrin alpha-2/beta-1 receptor works as receptor molecules on the surface of platelets thus contributing to thrombogenesis by interacting with adhesive proteins such as von Willebrand factor (vWF), collagen, adenosine diphosphate (ADP), thrombin and fibronectin [74]. The process may initiate platelet adhesion, activation and aggregation and may thus be a pathophysiological occurrence of chronic atherosclerosis and acute thrombosis [75]. ITGA2 is associated with several diseases. Significant overexpression of ITGA2 gene was observed in patients with primary colon tumours $(p < 0.0001)$ and liver metastases $(p < 0.0001)$ [76]. Polymorphic ITGA2 gene has also been proposed as a potential genetic risk factor for thrombotic diseases [77-79].

# 4.1.8. GP1BA (Platelet glycoprotein Ib alpha chain)

GP1BA is a protein coding gene that encodes for a surface membrane glycoprotein of platelets possessing an alpha and a beta chain. GPIb functions as a receptor of vWF. The alpha chain is found on the surface of platelet membrane and acts as a receptor molecule. On the other hand, GPIb binds to von vWF to mediate the platelet adhesion to vascular wall during an endothelial injury. It plays an important role in facilitating platelet adhesion under various conditions such as platelet procoagulation, platelet activation and shear stress [80, 81]. Polymorphism of the gene is reported to contribute to the development of a fatal myocardial infarction, sudden cardiac death and coronary thrombosis [82-84].

In a group of primary APS patients from Turkey, the TC genotype of Kozak polymorphism of $GP1BA$ gene was significantly associated with thrombosis when compared with healthy controls $(p = 0.0007)$ [29]. Therefore, there is a possibility of $GP1BA$ gene's contribution in thrombotic development of primary APS.

# 4.1.9. F2R (coagulation factor II thrombin receptor)

Coagulation factor II thrombin receptor (F2R) also known as proteinase-activated receptor-1 (PAR-1) is a 7-transmembrane G-protein-coupled receptor encoded by the gene F2R [85]. F2R is mainly expressed in human platelets and endothelial cells involved in the regulation of coagulation response (upon exposure to thrombin) and plays a key role in mediating the interplay between coagulation and inflammation [86, 87]. Thrombin-activated F2R can exert endothelial-dependent vasodilation via nitric oxide-dependent mechanisms [88] in addition to inducing inflammatory and angiogenic responses by

increased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines [89] and involvement in platelet secretion and aggregation [90]. As compared to controls, significantly high activation $(p = 0.0001)$ of PAR-1 was observed in the immune-mediated Crohn's disease [91], where a high expression levels of mRNA is reported during kidney disease progressions [92] and myocardial infarction [93].

In patients with thrombotic primary APS $(n = 62)$ , significantly higher F2R mRNA expression $(p < 0.001, 60.2 \pm 6.3\%$ vs $26.55 \pm 7.5\%)$ was observed in monocytes following treatment with IgG aCL as compared to controls $(n = 20)$ . The cell surface protein expression levels of PAR-1 was also significantly higher in APS patients having thrombosis as compared to non-APS subjects with thrombosis $(p < 0.05)$ . Additionally, a significant correlation was observed between the mRNA expression levels of tissue factor (TF) and PAR-1 $(r = 0.83, p = 0.005)$ [32] suggesting that high mRNA expression levels of PAR-1 is a potential cause of thrombotic pathogenesis occurring via immune-mediated pathways in patients with primary APS.

F2RL1 was reported to be associated with obesity $(p = 0.0005)$ in an African-American population [97] and Parkinson's disease [98]. # 4.1.11. TFPI (tissue factor pathway inhibitor)

TFPI gene encodes an anticoagulant protein product of tissue factor pathway inhibitor (a three-domain Kunitz-type natural inhibitor of the tissue factor pathway) that regulates the initiation of blood coagulation. It inhibits the tissue factor-activated factor VII complex while regulating blood coagulation in an activated factor X-dependent process [101]. TFPI is reported to have pathogenic association with venous thrombosis, stroke, cardiovascular and atherothrombotic diseases [102, 103], diabetes, inflammation and cancer [104].

Polymorphism within the gene was associated with higher risk of developing thrombosis in APS. Lincz et al. reported a significant correlation of venous thrombosis with $TFPI - 33\mathrm{T} \rightarrow \mathrm{C}$ (OR = ~13; 95% CI = 2.39–69.91; $p = 0.003$ ) and $-399\mathrm{C} \rightarrow \mathrm{T}$ (OR = ~13; 95% CI = 2.25–71.23; $p = 0.004$ ) polymorphisms among patients with primary APS when compared with controls [34]. # 4.1.12. F3 (coagulation factor III, tissue factor)

The gene F3 encodes a cell surface glycoprotein - coagulation factor III also known as TF. During a vascular injury, membrane bound TF is exposed enabling cells to initiate the blood coagulation cascade by forming a complex with the circulating factor VII or VIIa [105]. Additionally, TF is also reported to be involved in tumour progression [106], venous thrombosis [107] and atherosclerosis [108].

$TF$ mRNA expression levels were significantly elevated (monocyte extracted RNA) in primary APS patients with thrombosis as compared to non-APS subjects with thrombosis $(p < 0.001)$ and healthy controls $(p < 0.001)$ [35]. Additionally, the mean levels of $TF$ mRNA were significantly higher in monocytes $(p < 0.03)$ of primary APS subjects with history of thrombotic events compared to patients without thrombosis. Later, in another study on a Spanish population of thrombotic primary APS, the mRNA expression of $TF$ was significantly high compared with the healthy controls $(p < 0.001)$ [36]. Therefore, F3 gene may have a contributory pathogenic role in the development of thrombi in patients with primary APS.

In thrombotic primary APS subjects, the mRNA expression levels of FLT1 was also significantly higher as compared with the levels observed in patients without thrombosis $(p = 0.035)$ or with non-APS subjects with

thrombosis $(p = 0.002)$ . These results indicate that FLT1 is a potential risk factor in developing thrombosis in patients with primary APS.

# 4.1.15. F2 (coagulation factor II, thrombin)

Thrombin, also known as blood coagulation factor IIa is a serine protease enzyme which is encoded by the gene F2 [122]. Coagulation factor II (prothrombin) is proteolytically cleaved to convert it into its active form (thrombin) during the coagulation process. Thrombin functions in converting factors XI to Xla, VIII to VIIIa, V to Va, soluble fibrinogen to insoluble strands of fibrin and XIII to XIIIa in addition of functioning in blood homeostasis, inflammation and wound healing [123]. Different polymorphisms of F2 gene (i.e., G20210A) have been reported in subjects experiencing venous thrombosis with elevated levels of prothrombin [124-126]. Recent meta-analyses observed that G20210A mutation of F2 gene is significantly associated with recurrent pregnancy loss $(\mathrm{OR} = 1.8$ $95\% \mathrm{CI} = 1.2 - 2.6)$ [127], cerebral venous thrombosis $(\mathrm{OR} = 5.8$ $95\% \mathrm{CI} = 3.9 - 8.5)$ [128] and portal vein thrombosis $(\mathrm{OR} = 5.0$ $95\% \mathrm{CI} = 3.0 - 8.3)$ [129].

In case of APS patients, the G20210A mutation of the $F2$ gene was significantly higher (8.7% vs 2.0%; $p = 0.02$ ) than that of healthy controls [39]. Additionally, the G20210A mutation was observed more prevalent among subjects with APS (8.7%) when compared with non-APS subjects (2.8%), though this difference was statistically significant. In general, the G20210A mutation of the $F2$ gene is considered as an important risk factor for developing thrombosis [128]. As in APS patients, the frequency was significantly high. Therefore, $F2$ gene in particular, G20210A mutation was an associated risk factor in the development of thrombi in APS patients.

# 4.1.16. TNF (tumour necrosis factor)

TNF (also known as TNF- $\alpha$ ) gene encodes a multifunctional proinflammatory cytokine (mainly secreted by macrophages) that belongs to the tumour necrosis factor superfamily. TNF contributes in the regulation of cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, lipid metabolism and blood coagulation [130]. TNF- $\alpha$ is implicated in a variety of diseases. Recent meta-analyses revealed the association of different TNF- $\alpha$ polymorphisms (i.e., G308A and T1031C) in coronary heart disease [131], primary Sjogren's syndrome [132], gastric cancer [133] and polycystic ovary syndrome [134] as well as in a variety of autoimmune diseases [135].

In 2001, Bertolaccini et al. reported a significantly elevated level of plasma TNF- $\alpha$ in patients with APS compared to controls $(p = 0.0001)$ [38]. Moreover, they observed a strong association between TNFA $\cdot 238^{*}\mathrm{A}$ polymorphism and APS subjects with arterial thrombosis (OR $= 3.7$ ; $95\%$ CI $= 1.37 - 10.1$ ; $p = 0.007$ ), although no association could be established between TNFA- $238^{*}\mathrm{A}$ and plasma TNF- $\alpha$ levels. Hence, it is plausible that TNFA- $238^{*}\mathrm{A}$ polymorphism may be a genetic marker where elevated plasma TNF level may be correlated with the development of arterial thrombosis in patients with APS.

# 4.2. Bioinformatic analyses

Based on the cumulative effect of the identified genes on molecular functions, biological process and pathways, blood coagulation system was found to be majorly affected. Most of the diseases associated with the set of identified genes were associated with dysfunction in blood clot formation or dissociation and autoimmunity. Interestingly, eclampsia was one of the top diseases associated with nine (F2, F2R, FLT1, TNF, TLR2, TLR4, TFPI, SERPINE1, VEGFA) of the 16 identified genes which possibly represents the comorbid presence of thrombosis and pregnancy complications in women with APS [11, 136]. From the protein-protein interaction web, at least 10 gene-encoded proteins were found to activate one another while five inactivating one other (Fig. 2). This is possibly how both haemostasis and immune systems are disturbed in APS. The gene expression heatmap with all over 32 organ expression pattern possibly represents the phenomena of thrombus development in any organ of the body including the arteries and the veins [137].

# 5. Limitations

There are a few limitations of this systematic review with bioinformatic analyses. Firstly, the search strategy was restricted to the English language literatures only, therefore, there is a possibility of excluding eligible studies published in other languages. Secondly, more than two different classifications of definite primary APS were followed at different time frame by the included studies (before and after 1999 Sapporo and 2006 Sydney classification criteria of definite APS) while two studies did not report any followed criteria to confirm definite primary APS patients. Therefore, some clinical and laboratory discrepancies may arise in confirming the primary APS patients.

# 6. Future directions

It would be interesting to apply next generation sequencing technologies like RNA-Seq to screen the whole transcript of a large cohort of thrombotic primary APS patients to reveal the alternative gene spliced transcripts, post-transcriptional modifications, gene fusion, mutations/ polymorphisms and changes in the gene expression as compared to healthy controls or non-thrombotic primary APS subjects. Additionally, an epigenetic or epigenomic study on patients with thrombotic primary APS would help to identify and better understand the epigenetic factors and how it affects the abnormal expressions and activities of the genes identified in this systematic review to contribute to the thrombogenesis in primary APS.

# 7. Conclusions

Sixteen genes (PF4V1, SELP, TLR2, TLR4, SERPINE1, APOH, ITGA2, GP1BA, F2R, F2RL1, TFPI, F3, VEGFA, FLT1, TNF and F2) contribute significantly while six (PLSCR1, PTPN22, ADAMTS13, F13A1, ACE and F5) were not associated with thrombosis in primary APS when compared with the controls. Bioinformatic analyses of these genes revealed their molecular interconnectivity in protein levels mostly by affecting blood coagulation pathway and the immune system. These genes are expressed in 32 different organs indicating the high risk of developing thrombosis anywhere in the body of primary APS patients.

Take-home messages

- Sixteen genes are significantly involved in thrombotic primary antiphospholipid Syndrome.   
- These genes affect mostly the immune system and blood coagulation pathways.   
- RNA-Seq data shows mRNA expression almost all over 32 different human organs.   
- Different molecular interactions exist among the 14-thrombotic gene-encoded proteins.
