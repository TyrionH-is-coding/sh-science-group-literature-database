# Beta-2-glycoprotein-I, infections, antiphospholipid syndrome and therapeutic considerations

Miri Blank and Yehuda Shoenfeld*

Department of Medicine B and The Center for Autoimmune Diseases, Sheba Medical Center, Tel-Hashomer 52621, Israel

Sackler Faculty of Medicine, Tel-Aviv University, Israel

Received 13 February 2004; accepted with revision 27 February 2004

Available online 28 May 2004

# Abstract

Evidence supports the association between infectious agents, antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), and the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies and anti- $\beta 2$ -glycoprotein-I ( $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ ) antibodies. Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain the role of bacteria/viruses in induction of an autoimmune condition, such as molecular mimicry between structures of a pathogen and self antigen and bystander activation or bacterial/viral superantigens. Protein databases reveal high homologies between the $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -related synthetic peptides and infectious agents. Studies employing experimental APS models proved molecular mimicry between $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -related synthetic peptides, which serve as target epitopes for anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ Abs, and structures within bacteria, viruses (e.g., CMV), and tetanus toxoid. Any explanation of how microbial infections might induce APS must take into account the genetic predisposition. In this paper, we discuss the association of antiphospholipid antibodies, infectious states, and molecular mimicry as a proposed mechanism for development of APS.

# Introduction

There is a general consensus that autoimmune diseases have a multifactorial etiology, depending on both genetic and environmental factors. Thus, it has been suggested that many autoimmune diseases are caused or triggered by infections. Despite this, however, the exact nature of their contribution is not finally deciphered yet. For example, molecular mimicry between pathogen-driven antigen and host antigen might directly induce immune cell or antibody cross-reactivity with host antigens. In contrast, inflammatory and destructive processes during infection might lead to inadvertent activation through release of previously sequestered antigen. Alternatively, it may lead to the exacerbation of a silent autoimmune process through activation that results from the action of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Finally, accumulating evidences point to the role for TOLL receptors in the provoking autoimmune state. For example, TLR9 appears to be relatively restricted to B cells and

CD123+ dendritic cells. Upon the detection of CpG motifs of bacterial DNA (a signal of infection stimulate), B cells are induced to secrete immunoglobulin and dendritic cells secrete a wide array of cytokines.

The classical "Hughes Syndrome"—antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an autoimmune disease. The disease is characterized by elevated titers of circulating antiphospholipid antibodies which bind target molecules mainly via beta-2-glycoprotein-I ( $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ ), and/or lupus anticoagulant in association with recurrent fetal loss, thromboembolic phenomena thrombocytopenia, CNS, heart, and other organ involvement [1-5].

Accumulated evidence suggests that APS is a systemic autoimmune disease, associated not exclusively with thromboembolic phenomenon, coagulation failure or recurrent fetal loss, but with many diverse systemic clinical manifestations associated with brain, heart, skin and other organs involvements [5,6].

The common denominator for all the systemic features in APS is the association with the presence of mainly circulating pathogenic polyclonal anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ and to other antibodies to phospholipids such as phosphatidylserine or phosphatidylethanolamine.

# $\beta 2GPI$ molecule

The human $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ molecule is a plasma protein, belonging to the complement control protein family (CCP), also known as the short consensus repeat protein family, membrane-adhesion single chain glycoprotein (326aa), present in plasma at a concentration of approximately $150 - 300~\mu \mathrm{g / ml}$ [7-9]. $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ has five domains, the amino-terminal first through fourth domains are typical, and the fifth domain contains an extra approximately 20-aa C-terminal loop ending in an extra terminal disulfide bond and a lysine-rich region that functions as the major anionic phospholipid-binding site [8,9]. It has several properties in vitro which define it as an anticoagulant (e.g. inhibition of prothrombinase activity, ADP-induced platelet aggregation, platelet factor IX production) [10,11]. It has a role in the clearance of apoptotic bodies from the circulation [12,13]. $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ molecule was found to be immunogenic in vivo. Immunization of BALB/c, PL/J mice or New Zealand white rabbits with $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ resulted in generation of anti- $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ Abs [14-17]. The high titers of mouse anti- $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ was associated with an increased percentage of fetal resorptions (the equivalent of fetal loss in human APS), thrombocytopenia and prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), indicating the presence of lupus anticoagulant, a presentation characterized as experimental APS [16]. Acceleration of APS manifestations was observed in MRL/lpr mice (a mouse model of APS with a genetic background) immunized with $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ [18]. Furthermore, experimental APS was prevented in $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -fed tolerized mice via promotion of regulatory cell generation, accompanied by elevated expression of TGF $\beta$ [19]. Herein, we will discuss the link between circulating antibodies directed to the $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ molecule and infections, lighting the possible mechanisms involved in the APS origin.

# Anti- $\beta 2GPI$ and infections

The $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ molecule is an ubiquitous molecule. Several pathways were proposed to explain the generation of anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ Abs. (A) Exposure of hidden epitopes or neoepitopes as adducts of oxidized phospholipid associated proteins, including $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ molecule [20,21]. (B) Oxidized form of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ undergoes conformational changes presenting neoepitopes, which may induce anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ Abs [21,22]. Presentation of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ molecules on apoptotic cells via binding to phosphatidylserine could induce B cells with immunoglobulin receptors for apoptotic cells and DNA which are positively selected and could cause generation of anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ in the appropriate condition [23-25]. (C) Recently, the infectious origin of APS was proven to be one of the explanations for generation of anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ Abs by sharing molecular mimicry with common bacteria [26] or with CMV derived synthetic peptide [27]. The target epitopes for anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ binding the $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ molecule, as was shown by us [28] and others [29-32], are spread all over the five domains of the molecule.

Anti- $\beta 2$ GPI is a polyclonal heterogeneous population of immunoglobulins composed of subpopulations which differ in their biological activities, avidity, CDR composition and targeting different epitopes on the $\beta 2$ GPI molecule, all leading to the diverse clinical manifestations in APS. APS patients present interindividual diverse panel of anti- $\beta 2$ GPI repertoire, directed to different epitopes on the $\beta 2$ GPI molecule [28,33]. The general population of anti- $\beta 2$ GPI exert biological activities in vitro and in vivo evident by: (A) interfering with homeostatic reactions occurring on the surface of monocytes, platelets or vascular endothelial cells [34-39]; (B) activating monocytes leading to tissue factor release [35,36] or endothelial cells via induction of adhesion molecules expression, including E-selectin, ICAM-I and VCAM-I associated with enhanced expression of NFkB [37-39]. Recently, Raschi et al. [40] pointed to the role of MyD88 transduction signaling pathway in endothelial activation by anti- $\beta 2$ GPI. (C) In an ex vivo model of thrombosis, these family of Abs induce thrombus formation [41,42]. (D) Passive transfer of anti- $\beta 2$ GPI monoclonal, polyclonal or anti- $\beta 2$ GPI/peptides with homology to common bacteria, into naive mice, results in induction of experimental APS manifested by elevated percentage of fetal resorptions, prolonged coagulation time and thrombocytopenia [26,28,38,43-45]. (E) $\beta 2$ GPI-immunized mice were impaired neurologically and performed several reflexes less accurately, including placing reflex, postural reflex and grip test. The APS mice also exhibited hyperactive behavior in an open field test [46]. The disease could be prevented by subjecting the mice to $\beta 2$ GPI-related synthetic peptides detected by peptide phage display library to experimentally induced APS in naive mice [28]. The pathogenic potential of anti- $\beta 2$ GPI was ascribed to the CDR3 part of the anti- $\beta 2$ GPI immunoglobulin, evident by exchanging of heavy and light chains between pathogenic and nonpathogenic anti- $\beta 2$ GPI, expressed as a single chain Fv (scFv), which caused experimental APS [47]. Furthermore, Giles et al. [48] showed that the human anti- $\beta 2$ GPI are germline gene encoded, somatically mutated and emphasized the importance of arginine, asparagine and lysine residues in their biological function. Distinguishing features of anti- $\beta 2$ GPI between patients with leprosy and the APS was defined by their avidity [49], which might be a reflection of the CDR amino acid charge [48].

During the last years, various epitopes on $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ have been identified as target molecules for anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ Abs. Previously, using a hexapeptide phage display library, we identified three hexapeptides that were specifically recognized by pathogenic anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ mAbs which caused endothelial cell activation and induced experimental APS [28]. These peptides, A: $^{58}\mathrm{LKTPRV}^{63}$ (corresponds to the first and second domains of the $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ molecule), B: $^{208}\mathrm{KDKATF}^{213}$ (corresponds to the fourth domain of the $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ molecule) and C: $^{133}\mathrm{TLRVYK}^{138}$ (corresponds to the third domain), specifically inhibited, both in vitro and in vivo, the biological functions of the anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ mAbs (e.g. endothelial cell

Saccharomyces cerevisiae, anti- $\beta 2GPI$ Abs and APS

Presence of circulating anti-S. cerevisiae IgA/IgG Abs (ASCA) is one of the main markers for Crohn's disease, an

idiopathic chronic inflammatory bowel disease [73]. The serologic responses seen in Crohn's disease include antibodies to S. cerevisiae, mycobacteria, Bacteroides, Listeria and Escherichia coli. Many of these organisms may be involved in the pathogenesis of the Crohn's disease. Oligomannose, paratuberculosis p35 and p36 antigens are epitopes of the S. cerevisiae, defined and demonstrated in 60- $70\%$ of the patients with Crohn's disease. Patients with inflammatory bowel diseases have elevated titers of circulating anti-cardiolipin/anti- $\beta2$ GPI Abs [74-76]. Episodes of thrombosis associated with Crohn's disease were described some times accompanied with elevated titers of anti- $\beta2$ GPI Abs [74-76].

The association of ASCA was described also with other autoimmune condition, Behcet's disease (BD), with no relevance to the clinical picture [77]. Some of the patients $(30 - 32\%)$ with BD have circulating anti- $\beta 2$ GPI Abs [78,79]. Thrombosis, usually venous, occurs in $10 - 25\%$ of patients with BD, but its pathogenesis is poorly understood [79]. The association of APS with BD was raised as a cause for the total thrombotic occlusion of the vena cava in a case [80]. Famularo et al. [81] described a patient with BD

# Molecular mimicry between $\beta 2GPI$ and pathogens in APS

Epitope mimicry is widely thought to be one of the mechanisms for the induction of autoimmune disease [82-84]. The theory is that an infectious agent such as bacteria, virus, yeast or parasite displays epitopes immunologically resembling host determinants due to the minor antigenic differences between the two. The pathogen's epitope is able to induce an immune response that breaks tolerance to the host epitope. The cross-reactive T or B cell is then able to induce a pathogenic autoimmune response that leads to disease. Autoimmunity resulting from epitope mimicry may be an unfortunate result of the antipathogen immune response that can lead to disease in predisposed individuals.

We hypothesized that molecular mimicry mechanism between pathogen and $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ molecule may be the cause for APS, based on (1) a correlation between APS clinical manifestations and infectious agents in human, and (2)

strong homology between $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -related peptides (target epitopes for anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ Abs) and different common pathogens, detailed in the protein database, summarized in Table 3. To address the involvement of molecular mimicry between $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -related peptides and different pathogens sharing amino acid homologies in the induction of experimental APS, we performed the following study (Fig. 1). We immunized naive BALB/c mice with microbial pathogens, which share structural homology with the TLRVYK hexapeptide. Mouse anti-TLRVYK was affinity purified from the sera of the TLRVYK-immunized mice on a TLRVYK-sepharose column and passively infused intravenously into naive mice at day 0 of pregnancy. APS clinical parameters were evaluated in the infused mice on day 15 of pregnancy. Following immunization, various levels of mouse anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ Abs were observed, the highest being detected in those mice immunized with Haemophilus influenzae, Neisseria gonorrhoeae or tetanus toxoid, and were specific for the molecular weight defined in the protein database, as shown by Western blot. Mice infused with these anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ Abs had significant thrombocytopenia, prolonged aPTT and elevated percentage of fetal loss, similarly to a control group of mice immunize with a pathogenic anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ monoclonal Ab [26]. Hence, our study established a mechanism of molecular mimicry in experimental APS, demonstrating that $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -structure homologous bacteria are able to induce the generation of pathogenic anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ Abs along with APS manifestations [26].

Further support for epitope mimicry as a possible mechanism for APS development was given by Gharavi et al. [85,86]. Synthetic peptides which share structural similarity with the putative phospholipid-binding region of the $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ molecule, and share high homology with cytomegalovirus, $72\mathrm{-kDa}$ human adenovirus type 2 DNA-binding protein and Bacillus subtilis, were able to induce antiphospholipid and anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ Abs in NIH/Swiss mice [85,86].

We believe that pathogen particles are digested and presented on macrophages, dendritic cells or by B cells.

# Autoimmunity through infection

Toll-like receptors (TLRs) are pattern recognition receptors that trigger innate immunity, providing both immediate protective responses against pathogens and instructing the adoptive immune response [87]. Recently, a link was introduced between the innate and adoptive immune systems, in the development of systemic autoimmune diseases such as SLE [88]. Accumulating evidences paved the way to explain the importance of TLRs in promoting autoimmune conditions [89,90]. B cells play an essential role in adaptive immune response and in innate immunity. Chromatin-IgG complexes were found to activate B cells by a dual engagement of IgM to the TLR9 [91], which is known to detect bacterial CpG DNA [92]. Recent studies have revealed that common molecular patterns of microorganisms such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) are recognized by TLRs. B cells have two known TLRs that mediate LPS signaling, TLR4 and PR105 (CD180). The role of pathogens mimicking the

$\beta 2$ GPI in B cell TLR triggering may promote the humoral response leading to the development of APS. The importance of TLRs in the procoagulative state of endothelial cells in APS was proposed recently by Raschi et al. [40] showing the role of MyD88 transduction signaling pathway in endothelial cell activation by anti- $\beta 2$ GPI. The Abs reacted with $\beta 2$ GPI in association with TLR/IL-1 receptor family on the endothelial cells surface [40].

# Therapeutic considerations

As has been summarized above, there is a quite strong evidence for infectious etiology in APS. This raises the big question of instituting antibiotic therapy as prevention and/or interventional therapy especially in catastrophic APS. More information will be required in the future to solve this enigma. It will depend if the mechanism of the disease is "hit and run" [93,94] or a continuous stimulation of the immune system. The study where the APS was abrogated by eradicated $H.$ pylori with antibiotic [63] points to the impact of the continuous presence of the bacteria on the induction of the disease. # Conclusions

Molecular mimicry is one of the mechanisms by which experimental APS can occur in association with pathogens. Experimental APS could be induced by antibodies targeting peptides that share common epitopes with bacteria, viruses or tetanus toxoid, and the $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ molecule. We speculate that the mimicking antigen, similar in only one epitope, may initiate a primary cross-reactive response to that epitope that subsequently results in recognition of numerous epitopes in the mimicked host $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ [100]. Mimicry may be one of the mechanisms for braking the tolerance and triggering the autoimmune response. Yet, the mere presence of self-determinants on a virus or bacteria does not necessarily result in a disease. Toll-like receptors may play a role in triggering this autoimmune condition. Developing APS needs the appropriate genetic predisposition in concert with the exposure to the pathogen.
