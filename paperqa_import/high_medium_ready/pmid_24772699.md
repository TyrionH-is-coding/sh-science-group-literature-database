# Prevalence and Pattern of Antiphospholipid Antibody Syndrome in a Hospital Based Longitudinal Study of 193 Patients of Systemic Lupus Erythematosus

NK Singh*, A Agrawal**, MN Singh**, V Kumar**, M Godhra**, A Gupta**, DP Yadav***, Usha#, RG Singh#, TB Singh##

# Abstract

Background: Antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (APS) is a systemic autoimmune disease characterised by thrombophilic state and obstetrical complications. Prevalence of APS varies in different parts of the world. So this study was conducted to find out the prevalence and pattern of APS in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) in this region.

Material and Methods : In this hospital based longitudinal study from 2004 to 2011, we studied 193 patients of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) for prevalence of APS and its different characteristics. The diagnosis of SLE was made according to American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria and diagnosis of APS was made according to Sapporo criteria.

Results: Prevalence of APS in SLE was $25.38\%$ . Mean age at study entry was $25.5 \pm 6.9$ years and majority of APS patients were in the age group 21-30 yrs (44.89%). The most common clinical manifestation in both SLE with APS and SLE without APS was musculoskeletal involvement (79.59% and 84.72% respectively). Among 49 patients of SLE having APS, multisystem involvement was present in 16 patients and life threatening complications were present in 12 patients. Late foetal loss was the most common obstetrical manifestation of APS (26.53%) and deep vein thrombosis was most common thrombotic manifestation (16.32%). Anticardiolipin antibodies(IgG aCL) were the most common antibody (85.71%) detected. Lupus anticoagulant was present in 71.42% cases of SLE having APS. ANA and anti-dsDNA antibodies were present in 97.95% and 77.55% cases of SLE having APS.

Conclusion: APS is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in patients of SLE. The incidence of secondary APS in SLE varies in different geographical regions and it was $25.38\%$ in our study. Pregnancy morbidity and deep vein thrombosis were the most common complications of APS. IgG aCL was the most common antibody in APS patients. Screening for the presence of aPL antibodies in SLE patients and timely initiation of prophylactic treatment can prevent many of the complications.

# Introduction

Antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (APS) is a systemic autoimmune disease characterised by thrombophilic state and obstetrical complications. It was first described in 1983 by Graham Hughes as Anticardiolipin syndrome. Subsequently this term has been replaced by "Antiphospholipid Syndrome" (APS), when it became clear that antibodies against phospholipids other than anticardiolipin were also associated with APS. It may be primary or secondary, if associated with another autoimmune disease such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

Several studies have been conducted to establish the prevalence of antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL) in cohorts of healthy subjects and different clinical settings. But studies about the association APS with SLE are very scanty from our

# Material and Methods

This prospective, longitudinal study was carried out on the patients attending Departments of Medicine, Rheumatology, Nephrology and Obstetrics and Gynaecology at Sir Sundar Lal Hospital, Banaras Hindu University, Varanasi, from May 2004 to July 2011. This comprehensive study is a part of MD (General Medicine) thesis carried out by various residents registered under Prof. N.K. Singh from May 2004 to July 2011 as per details given in Table 1. During this study period after a detail history, clinical examination and laboratory testing, patients who fulfilled the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria<sup>1</sup> for the diagnosis of SLE were taken up for the study. Then they were subjected to find out for the presence of APS based on Sapporo's Criteria.<sup>2</sup>

# Results

During the study period there was a total of 193 patients having SLE (Table 3). The mean age at study entry was $25.5 \pm 6.9$ years and majority of patients were in the age group 21 to 30 years $(n = 92; 47.66\%)$ with female predominance $(n = 168; 87\%)$ . Duration of illness prior to presentation varied from 3 weeks to 2 years with a mean $3 \pm 1.2$ months. Maximum number of patients presented with duration of illness of more than 2 months. Among the 193 patients of SLE, 49 patients had APS giving a prevalence of $25.38\%$ . The most common clinical manifestation in SLE patients was musculoskeletal involvement (84.72%) followed by fever (72.91%) (Table 5). And also in those patients having SLE with APS musculoskeletal involvement involvement was the most common manifestation (79.59%) followed by fever and mucocutaneous involvement (73.46% and 61.22% respectively). Arthritis was present in 13 (26.53%) and arthralgia in 25 (51.02%) patients of SLE having APS. Among the 49 patients of SLE having APS, multisystem involvement was seen in 16 patients and 12 had life threatening complications. Late foetal loss was noted in 13 (26.53%) of 49 patients, recurrent early foetal losses (< 10 weeks gestation) was seen in 5 (10.20%) patients whereas preterm labour (< 34 weeks gestation) was seen in 7 (14.28%). Out of 5 patients having early foetal losses, 3 patients had 3 consecutive pregnancy losses and 2 patients had 4 consecutive pregnancy losses. Deep vein thrombosis was noted in 8 (16.32%) of which one patient had thrombosis of inferior vena cava just proximal to bifurcation of IVC, 2 patients had thrombosis of right common iliac and popliteal vein and 5 patients had DVT of left lower limb from femoral to calf veins. Six patients had stroke in whom CT scan revealed infarct with haemorrhagic conversion and 6 patients showed cortical sinus thrombosis. Two patients had Budd-Chiari Syndrome and one patient had splenic vein thrombosis.

ANA and Anti-dsDNA were positive in $97.95\%$ and $77.55\%$ respectively (Table 6). IgM aCL and IgG aCL was present in $20.40\%$ $(n = 10)$ and $85.71\%$ $(n = 42)$ respectively. LA was present in 35 $(71.42\%)$ patients out of which 26 $(53.06\%)$ had anticardiolipin antibody as well. Ten patients of SLE with APS $(20.4\%)$ and thirty patients of SLE without APS $(20.8\%)$ were lost to follow-up after variable period.

The prevalence of APS among SLE patients is variable in different geographical regions and ranges from $22\%$ to $61\%$ .[47] However one study in South India found that the incidence in adult SLE was only $13.7\%$ .[8] In this study we found that 49 patients were having APS out of 193 SLE patients, giving a prevalence of $25.38\%$ . The prevalence is slightly lower than other studies.[47] This difference in disease pattern may be because of different environmental or genetic factors, or both. In our study the most commonly affected age group was 21-30 years $(n = 22; 44.89\%)$ and females were more affected than males.[7] The overwhelming female population in the present study may be due to the fact that majority of patients who formed the subject matter of the present study were pregnant females having one or the other pregnancy related morbidity. In contrast to other studies which showed a male dominance during the childhood,[8] our study showed that APS in age group of 10-20 years was only one male $(2.04\%)$ .

In addition to aPL, Cervera et al<sup>5</sup> found that some patients with APS had ANA $(59.7\%)$ , anti-dsDNA $(29.2\%)$ , anti-Ro $(14.0\%)$ , and rheumatoid factor $(7.8\%)$ . In our study we found that ANA and anti-dsDNA were positive in $97.9\%$ and $77.55\%$ of cases.

The most common clinical manifestation in our study was the musculoskeletal $(79.59\%)$ , of which arthralgia was the most common $(51.02\%)$ . This is in contrast to other study where arthralgia $(38.7\%)$ and arthritis $(27.1\%)$ are less common among the major clinical manifestations.[11] Haematological manifestations in our study was the second most common manifestation, among which anaemia was the most common $(51.02\%)$ . This is in contrast to other studies[5,8] where thrombocytopenia was the most common manifestation $(25 - 29.6\%)$ . In our study $22.44\%$ of the patients had thrombocytopenia. In patients of APS, autoimmune haemolytic anaemia is seen in $14 - 23\%$ cases.[12] The difference in the finding may be a reflection of the fact that our population is undernourished because of poverty and nutritional anaemia contribute a large percent in addition to haemolytic anaemia of APS.

Third most common clinical manifestation in our study was muco-cutaneous involvement $(61.22\%)$ in the form of malar rash $(32.65\%)$ and oral ulcer $(57.14\%)$ . Dermatologic manifestations are extremely common in secondary APS. Almost $40 - 50\%$ of patients exhibit various skin lesions including levido reticularis, ulceration and splinter haemorrhages.[11,13] and our finding was not different.

Antiphospholipid antibodies occur in up to $20\%$ of women with recurrent pregnancy loss and are strongly associated with pregnancy complications, such as first- and second-trimester miscarriages, preeclampsia, eclampsia, intrauterine growth retardation (IUGR) and preterm labour.[14] On the other hand, patients with aPL have approximately a $22 - 75\%$ chance of foetal loss.[15,16] In this study, $51.02\%$ had foetal loss of which late fetal loss was most common $(26.53\%)$ which is in accordance with the other study. Out of 5 patients of early foetal losses, 3 patients had 3 consecutive losses and 2 patients had 4 consecutive losses of $< 10$ weeks gestation. In addition to foetal loss, APS patients may have other obstetrical complications including pre-eclampsia $(10\%)$ , eclampsia $(4\%)$ and abruptio placentae $(2\%)$ .[5] However, in our case these were rare.

# Conclusion

APS syndrome is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in patients of SLE. Severity of disease varied from mild mucocutaneous involvement to severe and life threatening multisystem involvement. The prevalence of secondary APS syndrome in SLE varies in different geographical regions. The associated complications and its incidence is also variable in different parts of the world because of genetic susceptibility related to aPL and APS. Screening for the presence of aPL antibody and timely initiation of prophylactic treatment can prevent many of complications. However, it has been difficult to determine genetic risk factors for aPL antibody and APS because of the heterogeneity in the antigen specificity, and pathogenesis of the clinical manifestations of APS.
