# Abstract

# Objectives:

Severe thrombotic antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) frequently affects the kidney, heart, and central nervous system. The precise frequency, clinical picture, differential diagnoses, and outcome of APS-related hematological involvement are lacking, especially in patients requiring ICU admission. This study aimed to describe the hematological manifestations associated with critically ill thrombotic APS patients and catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome.

# Methods:

This French, national, multicenter, retrospective study, conducted, from January 2000 to September 2018, included all APS patients admitted to 24 participating centers' ICUs with any new thrombotic manifestation. The prevalence of hematological manifestations and their associated outcomes were studied.

# Results:

One hundred and thirty-four patients, female $72\%$ , median [IQR] age 45 [34-56] years, with 152 episodes were included. Anemia was present in $95\%$ of episodes and thrombocytopenia in $93\%$ . The lowest values for hemoglobin and platelets were 7.1 [6.3-8.8] g/dL and 38 [21-60] g/L, respectively. The lowest platelet count below $20~\mathrm{g / L}$ was significantly associated with a higher in-ICU mortality rate $(50\%, p < 0.0001)$ . A thrombotic microangiopathy syndrome (TMA) syndrome was seen in 16 patients $(12\%)$ and was associated with higher in-hospital mortality $(p = 0.05)$ . Median ADAMTS-13 levels were $44\%$ [27-74]. Anti-ADAMTS13 antibodies were tested in 11 patients and found negative in all. A suspicion of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was raised in 66 patients but only four patients were classified as definite HIT. Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) was seen in $51\%$ of patients.

# Conclusion:

Thrombocytopenia is very frequent in severe APS patients and may be related to TMA, HIT, or DIC. Deciphering the mechanisms of thrombocytopenia is decisive in CAPS patients. Key Points - Thrombocytopenia is the hallmark laboratory finding in CAPS. - A complete thrombotic microangiopathy pattern is infrequent in CAPS patients. - Alternate diagnoses of CAPS, especially heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, need to be adequately investigated.

# 1. INTRODUCTION

The antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a systemic autoimmune disease defined by thrombotic and/or obstetrical events occurring in patients with persistent antiphospholipid antibodies (aPLAs) [1]. In rare cases, APS patients require intensive care unit (ICU) admission because of organ dysfunctions caused by macrovascular and/or microvascular thromboses [2]. Catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (CAPS) is the most severe complication of APS with mortality rates ranging from 26 to $50\%$ [3-6]. Classification criteria for CAPS have been established in the early 2000[7] but lack sensitivity and fail to identify patients with poor outcomes [2, 8]. Early recognition of CAPS is crucial to improve patients' outcome with early and tailored therapeutic interventions. Severe thrombotic APS frequently affects kidney, heart and central nervous system [5]. Hematological involvement is a hallmark of CAPS, especially thrombocytopenia. The precise frequency, laboratory findings and differential diagnoses of critically-ill APS-related hematological features is poorly reported.

We conducted this study to determine the prevalence, characteristics and prognosis of hematological manifestations in thrombotic critically-ill APS patients.

# 2. METHODS

# 2.1. Patients

This French, national, multicenter, retrospective study, conducted, from January 2000 to September 2018, included all APS patients admitted to 24 participating centers' ICUs with any new thrombotic (arterial, venous, or microvascular) manifestation. APS patients admitted to the ICU without any new thrombotic manifestation, were not included. APS was defined using the APS international diagnosis criteria available at the time of each patient's ICU admission [9-11]. Patients with newly diagnosed APS who died in the ICU who were classified with only one aPLA determination (namely, without aPLA positivity confirmed after 6 or 12-week depending on APS criteria available at the time of their admission to ICU) could be included

APS-related thromboses were diagnosed clinically, 135 using conventional imaging (Doppler ultrasound, echocardiogram, computed-tomography scan, arteriography and magnetic resonance imaging) or through the analysis of any histopathological sample, as previously reported [2,3]. Organ involvement was defined as any new thrombotic event involving an artery or vein and large or small vessels in the corresponding organ. Microvascular involvement seen in any tissue biopsy or when histopathological proof could not be obtained, was diagnosed as follows: kidney (50% serum creatinine rise, severe systemic hypertension $(>180/100\mathrm{mmHg})$ and/or proteinuria $(>500\mathrm{mg}/24$ hours), after exclusion of differential diagnoses, especially lupus nephritis), lung (bronchoalveolar lavage or computed-tomography scan revealing intra-alveolar hemorrhages after exclusion of differential diagnoses, especially cardiogenic pulmonary oedema), and brain (delirium, coma, seizure or status epilepticus after exclusion of differential diagnoses).

<!-- page 7 -->
# 2.2. Data collection

<!-- page 17 -->
Standardized forms were used to collect the following information: epidemiological parameters; APS clinical, biological and therapeutic history; clinical manifestations; laboratory findings, including complete blood count, blood smear, prothrombin time, D-dimer level, fibrinogen, haptoglobin, ADAMTS13, anti-ADAMTS13 antibodies, anti-PF4 antibodies and functional test; in-ICU treatments; complications; and outcomes. Patients were classified according to previously published CAPS criteria [7, 8]. Triple therapy was defined by the use of anticoagulation plus corticosteroids plus plasma exchange and/or intravascular immunoglobulins (IVIGs). The Damage Index of APS was calculated for every survivor 6 months post-ICU discharge with available follow-up, as previously reported, with every item weighted the same (1 point) [12]. Systemic lupus erythematosus was classified according to American College of Rheumatology criteria [13]. Diagnostic criteria for laboratory thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) syndrome were as follows [14]: (1) hemolytic anemia (hemoglobin level below $120~\mathrm{g / L}$ [12 g/dL] with haptoglobin levels below $0.3~\mathrm{g / L}$ ; (2) schistocytes per high-power field in the peripheral blood smear; (3) thrombocytopenia (platelet count below $150\times 10^{9} / \mathrm{L}$ ). Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was defined according 161 to the American Society of Haematology 2018 guidelines [15]: HIT was suspected when 4T score was $\geq 4$ , and definite if both PF4 antibodies and a functional test were positive and with positive effect of heparin-to-heparin substitute conversion. Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) was considered in patients with a DIC scoring system $\geq 5$ , as previously published and recommended [16-18].

# 2.3. Statistical analyses

Results for categorical variables, expressed as number $(\%)$ , were compared with $\chi^2$ tests or the exact test of Fischer when appropriate; those for continuous variables, expressed as mean $\cdot + / -$ standard deviation or median [interquartile range (IQR)], were compared using Student's t-test or Wilcoxon's rank test. First, we conducted a descriptive analysis of the 134 patients' clinical characteristics and laboratory findings, then we analyzed prevalence and significance of (i) thrombocytopenia, (ii) TMA syndrome, (iii) HIT and (iii) DIC. Statistical significance was defined as $p \leq 0.05$ . Analyses were computed with R software 4.0.2 version (R project for Statistical Computing).

# 3. RESULTS

# 3.1. Study population

One hundred and thirty-four patients (female $72\%$ , median [IQR] age 45 [34-56] years) with 152 episodes, were included in this study (Table 1). Systematic lupus erythematosus (SLE) was associated in $34\%$ cases. Triple aPLA positivity was present in $53\%$ patients. In-ICU and in-hospital episodes mortality rates were 18 and $23\%$ , respectively. The summary of hematological manifestations occurring in study patients is shown in the study flow-chart (Figure 1). Ninety-six patients received a red blood cells transfusion, 37 platelet concentrates, and 48 received fresh-frozen plasma.

# 3.2. Thrombocytopenia

Anemia was present in $95\%$ episodes and thrombocytopenia in $93\%$ . Lowest values for hemoglobin and platelets during ICU stay were 7.1 [6.3-8.8] g/dL and 38 [21-60] G/L respectively. The course of median platelets level counts in all patients during ICU stay shown in Figure 2. Patients were divided into three groups according to their lowest platelet levels ( $>150$ G/L, 150-20G/L, $<20$ G/L) and subsequently compared (Table 2). Variables significantly associated with lower platelet count were: high Charlson comorbidity index, day-0 SOFA score, presence of a precipitating event, positive lupus anticoagulant, high delay from first to third organ involvement, presence of schistocytes, low haptoglobin and high d-dimers. Patients with lowest platelets count below 20 G/L had a significantly poorer in-ICU mortality rate ( $50\%$ , p<0.0001).

# 4. DISCUSSION

Antiphospholipid syndrome is a rare autoimmune disease affecting mainly young women and responsible for a large range of thrombotic and non-thrombotic manifestations. Given its rarity, the pleomorphic hematological features and the numerous hematological alternative diagnoses (ADAMTS-13-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia...), the diagnosis and management of critically-ill APS remains a challenge. We present herein a real-life picture of APS-related hematological features during acute thrombotic episodes. Our series shares similarities but also differs from previous reports. Noteworthy, we included critically-ill thrombotic APS patients, some not meeting CAPS classification criteria while other series from literature only included definite/probable CAPS patients. The gender distribution, the frequencies of systemic lupus erythematosus and lupus anticoagulant were comparable with previous reports [5]. We report a higher prevalence of thrombocytopenia (92% vs. 67%) than in the CAPS registry [5], possibly reflecting a selection bias in our series: sicker patients and a case-mix of CAPS and non-CAPS patients. Yet, as we described the daily evolution of platelet counts, our data might be more precise and more accurate than previous reports. The frequencies of TMA and schistocytes were 14% and 22% respectively in the CAPS registry and were similar to ours (12% and 25%, respectively) [5]. The prevalence of suspected or confirmed HIT have never been previously reported in CAPS. According to international definition, the frequency of DIC was higher in our series than in the CAPS registry (51% vs. 13%) [17].

Thrombocytopenia was the main hematological manifestation, affecting more than $90\%$ patients. Its exact mechanism remains poorly understood in CAPS [1]. Profound thrombocytopenia was associated with worse outcome, suggesting more extensive thrombotic disease or sicker patients, as multifactorial thrombocytopenia is frequent in critical care. Contrary to previous literature, a very low platelets count, below $< 20~\mathrm{G / L}$ , was not unusual. Overall, thrombocytopenia is one of the most frequent manifestation pre-CAPS or CAPS patients. It is both a diagnostic and a prognostic parameter that we believe can also be used to monitor treatment efficacy. Yet, platelet count is not included in CAPS criteria, and we herein advocate for its inclusion in the next CAPS classification criteria.

<!-- page 21 -->
CAPS is considered a cause of TMA [19], however its frequency in our series is low, even in patients with probable/definite CAPS. When a TMA pattern was present, it was usually mild, with few schistocytes on blood smear. Haptoglobin levels were infrequently undetectable 265 but frequently "abnormally normal", given the marked inflammatory syndrome with high concomitant fibrinogen levels. The two main entities responsible for TMA are thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and hemolytic and uremic syndrome (HUS). They share many clinical and laboratory manifestations with CAPS, especially in the most severe patients. Moreover, many cases have been reported with systemic lupus erythematosus/APS and TTP overlap [20]. When assessed, ADAMTS-13 levels were not evocative of TTP in our patients and none had anti-ADAMTS-13 antibodies. Yet, two patients with ADAMTS-13 levels below $10\%$ could be suspected to have APS and TTP overlap. We believe that TMA differential diagnoses, such as TTP, should be careful scrutinized facing a CAPS-suspected patient, especially in those having a very pronounced TMA syndrome. As TMA was associated with a higher in-hospital mortality, eculizumab could be considered sooner in the therapeutic algorithm of these high-risk patients [21, 22].

HIT is a rare life-threatening disorder whose clinical and laboratory manifestations strongly resemble those of CAPS episodes and could also overlap with CAPS. Similarities are so obvious that nearly half our patients had an HIT suspicion raised. Yet, a very low number of HIT were confirmed. Given the serious consequences of anticoagulation cessation and/or switch toward heparin substitute, physicians should be really cautious when evoking the diagnosis of HIT in severe thrombotic APS patients. Functional tests are needed to cope with anti-PF4 antibody poor specificity. Discussion with a hemostasis specialist seems to be mandatory in this setting.

DIC was frequently seen in our patients and was associated with in-ICU severity. A previous study found an association between DIC and higher rate of thrombocytopenia in CAPS patients [17]. Given the critically-ill nature of our patients, it may be difficult to draw conclusion on the relationship between DIC and severe APS as it is a frequent complication of critical care.

Our study has limitations and strengths. First, it has a retrospective, observational design but many patients with a very rare disease came from 24 ICUs in participating centers. Second, patient inclusion lasting $>20$ years means inevitable heterogeneity of diagnoses and management, but most patients were included during the last decade. Third, the significant number of missing hematological investigation data (ADAMTS-13, anti-ADAMTS-13 autoantibodies, anti-PF4 autoantibodies...) may have under or overestimate the true frequency of differential diagnoses. Last, a lack of power may have prevented to show significant differences in terms of prognosis. However this is the largest series focusing on APS-related hematological features reported to date.

In conclusion, hematological manifestations are very frequent in critically-ill APS patients, especially thrombocytopenia, which was associated with worse outcome. Albeit infrequent in our cohort, alternate diagnoses that encompasses TMA, HIT and DIC need to be adequately investigated.
