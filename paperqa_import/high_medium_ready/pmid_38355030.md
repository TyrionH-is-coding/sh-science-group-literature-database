# Non-criteria antiphospholipid antibodies and calprotectin as potential biomarkers in pediatric antiphospholipid syndrome

# Abstract

Our study aimed to evaluate the presence, clinical associations, and potential mechanistic roles of non-criteria antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL) and circulating calprotectin, a highly stable marker of neutrophil extracellular trap release (NETosis), in pediatric APS patients. We found that $79\%$ of pediatric APS patients had at least one non-criteria aPL at moderate-to-high titer. Univariate logistic regression demonstrated that positive anti-beta-2 glycoprotein I domain 1 (anti-D1) IgG $(p = 0.008)$ , anti-phosphatidylserine/prothrombin (aPS/PT) IgG $(p < 0.001)$ , and aPS/PT IgM $(p < 0.001)$ were significantly associated with venous thrombosis. Positive anti-D1 IgG $(p < 0.001)$ , aPS/PT IgG $(p < 0.001)$ , and aPS/PT IgM $(p = 0.001)$ were also associated with non-thrombotic manifestations of APS, such as thrombocytopenia. Increased levels of calprotectin were detected in children with APS. Calprotectin correlated positively with absolute neutrophil count $(r = 0.63$ , $p = 0.008)$ and negatively with platelet count $(r = -0.59$ , $p = 0.015)$ . Mechanistically, plasma from pediatric APS patients with high calprotectin levels impaired platelet viability in a dose-dependent manner.

# INTRODUCTION

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an autoimmune thromboinflammatory disorder classically characterized by thrombosis and/or obstetric morbidity in the presence of persistently positive antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL) (1). Pediatric APS, typically defined as the syndrome presenting before age 18, is distinct from adult APS and remains understudied (2, 3). The pediatric population inherently has fewer risk factors for thrombotic events and less opportunity for obstetric morbidity. Conversely, non-thrombotic manifestations are highly prevalent in pediatric APS (4-7). The largest published cohort of pediatric APS patients, the Ped-APS Registry, comprises a group of 121 patients (4). In this cohort, non-thrombotic clinical manifestations were common, particularly hematologic disorders $(38\%)$ , skin manifestations $(18\%)$ , and non-thrombotic neurologic involvement $(16\%)$ . In clinical practice, non-thrombotic manifestations can be the presenting feature of APS, and lack of recognition can lead to diagnostic delays. Mechanism-informed biomarkers that may improve diagnosis for non-thrombotic pediatric APS clinical manifestations are not yet available.

Currently, there are no universally accepted criteria for pediatric APS. Historically, the revised Sapporo criteria for adult-onset APS have been applied to pediatric populations, though they have never been validated in children. These criteria require evidence of persistent aPL at moderate to high titers in the setting of thromboembolic events or pregnancy morbidity (1). While non-thrombotic clinical manifestations have been well described in APS, particularly among children, they were not previously considered for APS classification. Recently, new APS classification criteria were developed through a collaborative international effort. In addition to thrombotic and obstetric events, the new 2023 ACR/EULAR APS classification criteria include several non-thrombotic clinical domains, such as microvascular disease, cardiac valve disease, and hematologic disease (8). While some of those new clinical domains might be more applicable to pediatric populations, their performance for classifying pediatric APS cases remains to be validated.

aPL are a heterogeneous group of autoantibodies targeting phospholipids or phospholipid-binding proteins. "Criteria aPL," as defined by the revised Sapporo criteria and the recently published ACR/EULAR criteria, can be detected by the lupus anticoagulant test or by solid-phase ELISAs that seek moderate-to-high titers of anticardiolipin (aCL) IgG/IgM or anti-beta-2 glycoprotein I (αβ2GPI) IgG/IgM (1, 8). While not included in the classification schema, several non-criteria aPL have shown promising utility in identifying APS patients and improving risk stratification. These include anti-phosphatidylserine/prothrombin (aPS/PT) IgG/IgM and anti-β2GPI domain 1 (anti-D1) IgG (9–15). The clinical utility of these non-criteria aPL and their association with thrombotic or non-thrombotic manifestations in pediatric APS is unknown.

Recent studies suggest that the overexuberant release of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs)—prothrombotic tangles of DNA and microbicidal proteins—is essential to APS pathogenesis (16). Studies from adult APS have demonstrated that aPL engage neutrophils to trigger NETosis, that elevated levels of NETs markers are seen in the blood of APS patients, and that high levels of NETs promote thromboinflammation in vitro and in

<!-- page 3 -->
vivo (16-19). With particular relevance to this research, our group demonstrated that calprotectin, a heterodimer of S100A8/A9 and a marker of circulating NETs, associates with non-thrombotic features among adult APS patients, such as thrombocytopenia (20). The role of calprotectin in pediatric APS remains to be elucidated.

Here, we aimed to evaluate the presence, clinical associations, and potential mechanistic roles of non-criteria aPL and circulating calprotectin in pediatric APS patients.

# METHODS

This study complied with all relevant ethical regulations and was approved by the UT Southwestern Medical Center IRB (STU092010-167, STU122015-029) and the University of Michigan IRB (HUM00122519).

# Pediatric patients' samples.

Sodium citrate blood samples were collected from 19 patients with pediatric APS, 11 pediatric lupus patients without aPL, 20 pediatric patients with a history of venous thrombosis without APS, and 13 healthy pediatric controls (Table 1). Blood was centrifuged twice at $2000\mathrm{g}$ for $10\mathrm{min}$ at room temperature to acquire platelet-poor plasma. Plasma samples were stored at $-80^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ and used for various aPL and circulating calprotectin measurements.

# Pediatric patients' clinical data.

Patient's demographic information was recorded at enrollment. Race and ethnicity were self-reported by participants or their guardians. Charts were reviewed for clinical manifestations and medication usage at time of blood sampling.

# Quantification of aPL.

aPL were quantified in plasma using Quanta Lite® ACA IgG, ACA IgM, ACA IgA, β2GPI IgG, β2GPI IgM, β2GPI IgA, aPS/PT IgG, and aPS/PT IgM kits (Inova Diagnostics Inc., Werfen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. All assays are approved for clinical use and received 510(k) clearance from the FDA. Anti-D1 IgG was measured by QUANTA Flash® chemiluminescent immunoassay (CAT.701188, Inova Diagnostics, Werfen) using the BIO-FLASH® system (Inova Diagnostics Inc., Werfen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Absorbance was measured with Cytation 5 Cell Imaging Multi-Mode Reader (BioTek Instruments). While Werfen's aPL ELISA assays are deemed the gold standard for aPL evaluation due to their minimal inter-assay variability, we took additional steps to ensure the reliability of our data. Specifically, we randomly selected 9 samples and conducted a repeat analysis of aPS/PT IgG and IgM testing. The repeated results strongly correlated with the original findings, further validating our data.

# Quantification of circulating S100A8/A9 (calprotectin).

According to the manufacturer's instructions, plasma calprotectin levels were measured using the Human S100A8/S100A9 Heterodimer DuoSet ELISA (DY8226-05, R&D Systems). Absorbance was measured with Cytation 5 Cell Imaging Multi-Mode Reader

<!-- page 4 -->
(BioTek Instruments). Although R&D's calprotectin assays are highly regarded in the field for their strong reliability, we implemented additional measures to ensure the reproducibility of our data. Specifically, we randomly selected 22 samples and conducted a repeat round of calprotectin testing. The subsequent results demonstrated a robust correlation with the original findings, further validating our data.

# Donor platelet isolation.

Sodium citrate-treated blood from healthy donors was centrifuged for 15 minutes at $200\mathrm{g}$ . EDTA $(0.5\mathrm{mM})$ and PGE1 $(1\mu \mathrm{M})$ were added to the isolated platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and centrifuged at $700\mathrm{g}$ for 6 minutes. Platelet pellets were then washed with Tyrode's buffer [HEPES $(20\mathrm{mM})$ , NaCl $(135\mathrm{mM})$ , $\mathrm{Na_2HPO_4}$ $(0.34\mathrm{mM})$ , KCl $(2.9\mathrm{mM})$ , $\mathrm{NaHCO_3}$ $(12\mathrm{mM})$ , $\mathrm{MgCl_2}$ $(1\mathrm{mM})$ , and glucose $(5\mathrm{mM})$ , pH 6.5] in the presence of EDTA and PGE1. After centrifugation, the supernatant was aspirated, and platelet pellets were resuspended in Tyrode's buffer of pH 7.35 with $0.3\%$ BSA. Platelet count was determined using an automated cell counter (DeNovix-CellDrop, DeNovix Inc.) and was adjusted to $1\times 10^8$ cells/mL with Tyrode's buffer $(\mathrm{pH}7.35)$ .

# Impact of APS plasma on platelet viability.

For in vitro experiments, pediatric APS patients were divided into two groups based on calprotectin concentration in plasma: low (APS-Low-CP, $n = 5$ ) and high (APS-High-CP, $n = 6$ ). The APS-High-CP and APS-Low-CP groups were divided based on the $99^{\text{th}}$ percentile of healthy control calprotectin levels, which was $609~\mathrm{ng/mL}$ . These groups were compared to healthy controls ( $n = 5$ ). Two platelet viability dye-based assays were performed to assess the potential effects of pediatric APS patients' plasma on platelet survival. The first assay determines the platelet survival utilizing Calcein-AM dye. Donor platelets were incubated with $5\%$ plasma of healthy controls and pediatric APS patients having low and high calprotectin at $37^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for 1 hour. After the incubation, platelets were incubated with Calcein-AM ( $0.5~\mu\mathrm{M}$ ) for 15 minutes, and cells were analyzed in a BioRad-ZE5 flow cytometer analyzer (BioRad-ZE5 flow cytometer, Bio-Rad Laboratories). A second assay estimated the platelet survival based on the levels of phosphatidyl serine (PS) externalization on the platelet plasma membrane measured using FITC-Annexin V (FITC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit, Bio-legend). After the initial incubation with plasma from healthy controls and APS-Low-CP and APS-High-CP patients, annexin V-binding buffer was added to the treated platelets. Then, platelets were incubated with annexin V-FITC ( $2~\mu\mathrm{g/mL}$ ), for 15 minutes in the dark at $37^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ . Excess dye was removed by centrifugation, and platelet samples were then resuspended in an annexin-binding buffer and analyzed by a BioRad-ZE5 flow cytometer analyzer.

# RESULTS

Nineteen pediatric patients with APS were included in this study. The majority (74%) were female, with a mean age of 12.7 years (SD 4.5). Eleven APS patients had primary APS (58%). The rest had other systemic autoimmune diseases, including systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE, n=6), Behcet's disease (n=1), and Crohn's disease (n=1). Most (89%) had experienced at least one venous thromboembolism (VTE), and four had recurrent

<!-- page 5 -->
VTE. The remaining two children with APS exhibited non-thrombotic clinical domain manifestations specified in the 2023 ACR/EULAR criteria and a clinical diagnosis of APS (8). None of the patients had experienced an arterial event. Almost half $(47\%)$ had at least one non-thrombotic clinical domain manifestation, including hematologic, dermatologic, or neurologic involvement. Neurologic involvement in the patients with APS manifested as white matter lesions, one of which occurred in a patient with APS secondary to SLE. Another patient with primary APS developed white matter lesions over a year after sample collection. No movement disorders were present in our cohort. In addition to patients with APS, pediatric patients with VTE without APS $(n = 20)$ , pediatric patients with SLE without positive aPL $(n = 11)$ , and healthy controls $(n = 13)$ were included in the study. Demographic and clinical characteristics of these participants are described in Table 1.

# Non-criteria aPL are commonly detected in pediatric APS

We measured criteria and non-criteria aPL in pediatric patients with APS, VTE, and SLE, as well as healthy controls. Interestingly, fifteen pediatric APS patients had at least one non-criteria aPL (79%) (Table 2). While lupus anticoagulant remained the most prevalent aPL in our cohort (84%), various non-criteria aPL [aPS/PT IgM (68%), aPS/PT IgG (58%), and anti-D1 IgG (47%)] were more prevalent than aCL IgG/IgM (26%, 16%) and aβ2GPI IgG/IgM (26%, 26%). None of the individuals from the VTE, SLE without aPL, and healthy control cohorts had moderate-to-high titer non-criteria aPL. In this cohort, the presence of lupus anticoagulant associated with aPS/PT IgG (OR 28.93, 95% CI 4.92–141.8, p<0.0001), aPS/PT IgM (OR 32.27, 95% CI 6.62–124.1, p<0.0001), and anti-D1 IgG (OR 8.8, 95% CI 2.08–34.76, p=0.006) at titers 40 units.

# Thrombotic and non-thrombotic manifestations are associated with non-criteria aPL

We next performed univariate logistic regression to investigate potential associations between non-criteria aPL and various APS clinical manifestations. Both thrombotic and non-thrombotic clinical manifestations were associated with aPS/PT IgG, aPS/PT IgM, and/or anti-D1 IgG positivity, as defined by the more stringent cutoff of 40 units (Table 3). All three non-criteria aPL were associated with venous thrombosis (aPS/PT IgG $\chi^2 = 16.32$ , $p < 0.001$ ; aPS/PT IgM $\chi^2 = 21.71$ , $p < 0.001$ ; anti-D1 IgG $\chi^2 = 6.69$ , $p = 0.008$ ). Additionally, aPS/PT IgM was associated with recurrent thrombosis ( $\chi^2 = 4.19$ , $p = 0.041$ ).

As for non-thrombotic manifestations, we found persistent thrombocytopenia (platelet count $< 140\mathrm{K/uL}$ ) was associated with aPS/PT IgG ( $\chi^2 = 12.83$ , p < 0.001), aPS/PT IgM ( $\chi^2 = 10.18$ , p=0.001), and anti-D1 IgG ( $\chi^2 = 16.66$ , p<0.001). Hemolytic anemia was associated with aPS/PT IgM ( $\chi^2 = 4.39$ , p=0.036) and anti-D1 IgG ( $\chi^2 = 8.81$ , p=0.003). Additionally, livedo reticularis/racemosa was associated with anti-D1 IgG ( $\chi^2 = 4.08$ , p=0.044) but not aPS/PT antibodies.

# Circulating calprotectin is elevated in some individuals with pediatric APS

While it is now well recognized that NETs play a role in the pathogenesis of adult APS, this topic has not been investigated for pediatric APS. We measured circulating calprotectin in pediatric APS patients $(n = 19)$ , patients with VTE without APS $(n = 20)$ , patients with SLE without aPL $(n = 11)$ , and healthy controls $(n = 13)$ . A higher level of circulating calprotectin

<!-- page 6 -->
was observed in pediatric APS patients [mean $825\mathrm{ng / mL}$ (SD $521\mathrm{ng / mL})$ ] compared to healthy controls [mean $388\mathrm{ng / mL}$ (SD $154\mathrm{ng / mL})$ ] (Figure 1A). Calprotectin positively correlated with the absolute neutrophil count $(r = 0.63, p = 0.008$ , data not shown) and aPS/PT IgM titers $(r = 0.49, p = 0.035$ , data not shown). Interestingly, higher levels of calprotectin were associated with lower platelet counts $(r = -0.59, p = 0.015)$ (Figure 1B). No significant correlation was seen between calprotectin and criteria aPL.

# Calprotectin impairs platelet viability in pediatric APS

To investigate the potential mechanistic role of calprotectin in APS-associated thrombocytopenia, we performed Calcein-AM dye and annexin V-based platelet viability assays (Figure 2A), which quantify platelet viability based on cellular esterase activity and phosphatidylserine externalization, respectively. Plasma from APS-High-calprotectin $(n = 6)$ and APS-Low-calprotectin $(n = 5)$ pediatric APS patients, as well as healthy controls $(n = 5)$ , were added to donor platelets. Platelet viability was then determined. Platelets stimulated with APS-High-calprotectin pediatric APS plasma had significantly impaired platelet viability compared to platelets stimulated with both APS-Low-calprotectin and healthy control plasma, based on both platelet viability assays (Figure 2B, 2D). Moreover, a discernible positive correlation surfaced in this cohort between circulating calprotectin and platelet death, as evaluated by the percentage of non-viable platelets (Figure 2C) and phosphatidylserine externalization (Figure 2E).

# DISCUSSION

In this multicenter cross-sectional study, we found that non-criteria aPL are highly prevalent in the pediatric APS population and are associated with both thrombotic and non-thrombotic clinical manifestations. Furthermore, we found that circulating calprotectin, a marker of NETosis, is elevated in pediatric APS. Increased calprotectin levels were associated with thrombocytopenia, the most common non-thrombotic manifestation among pediatric APS patients. Mechanistically, calprotectin impaired platelet viability.

While criteria aPL are the most commonly tested aPL in clinical practice, several new "non-criteria" aPL have been discovered in the past 20 years. Growing evidence has suggested the clinical utility of non-criteria aPL in identifying APS manifestations in the context of adult patients. Among those, aPS/PT and anti-D1 assays have received the most attention. aPS/PT recognizes phosphatidylserine and prothrombin complexes. Multiple studies have demonstrated that positive aPS/PT is associated with thrombotic and non-thrombotic clinical manifestations of APS (9, 12, 21). Notably, a positive aPS/PT is also highly associated with the presence of LA (22). This is highly relevant in the APS population because most patients are managed with life-long anticoagulation, complicating the LA evaluation; LA results may not be reliable while on certain types of anticoagulation, and interpretation may depend on lab expertise. While less studied than aPS/PT antibodies, anti-D1 antibodies have also received increasing interest from the APS research community. Anti-D1 IgG targets the peptide sequence Arg 39 to Arg 43 in Domain I of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ . Positive anti-D1 is associated with thrombosis and obstetric events in adult APS patients, but there is little data on its utility in identifying non-thrombotic APS clinical manifestations (23, 24).

<!-- page 7 -->
Non-criteria aPL are little studied in the pediatric population. In one small retrospective case series of children with rheumatic disease, eight patients had positive non-criteria aPL along with features of mostly non-thrombotic manifestations of APS. Yet, their criteria aPL were mostly negative (25). In another retrospective case series, aPS/PT was tested in only two of the 21 pediatric APS cases, and both patients were positive for aPS/PT; one had durable aPS/PT antibodies despite the normalization of criteria aPL (5). Most cohorts of pediatric patients do not address non-criteria aPL testing. To our knowledge, this is the first evaluation of the clinical significance of non-criteria aPL in the pediatric APS population. Indeed, our data suggest non-criteria aPL may identify patients with APS features, including those who have not yet experienced a thrombotic event. This may have important implications for recognizing and managing pediatric APS patients in the future. Currently, aPS/PT antibodies are available locally at select institutions and commercially through providers like Quest Diagnostics®, LabCorp®, and Mayo Clinic Laboratories. These tests could prove valuable for pediatricians in diagnosing and risk-stratifying pediatric APS patients, especially those that lack criteria aPL and in scenarios where lupus anticoagulant testing is impractical, such as patients actively receiving anticoagulation.

Thrombotic events are less common in the pediatric population when compared to adults (26). By nature, pediatric patients have had less time to accrue pro-thrombotic risk factors or participate in behaviors that increase their risk for hypercoagulability. Additionally, pregnancy is less common in the pediatric age group. On the other hand, non-thrombotic clinical manifestations are much more prevalent in pediatric APS and are often observed long before the first thrombotic event. In our cohort, over half the patients experienced at least one non-thrombotic clinical manifestation, most commonly thrombocytopenia followed by hemolytic anemia. Interestingly, non-criteria aPL positivity in our cohort was associated with several non-thrombotic clinical manifestations, suggesting non-criteria aPL may improve the diagnosis and risk stratification of pediatric APS.

Recent studies among adult APS patients suggest excessive formation of NETs plays a role in promoting APS thromboinflammation (27). For example, NETs and their contents can activate endothelial cells and promote vasculopathy in APS (16). NETs can act as a fertile platform for complement activation, while they also induce type I interferon production by plasmacytoid dendritic cells (16). It has also been revealed in recent years that NETs are prothrombotic. NETs potentiate thrombosis through multiple mechanisms, including platelet activation, promotion of the coagulation cascade, and provision of scaffolding for thrombus formation (27, 28). The role of NETs in pediatric APS has not been previously explored. Here, we found pediatric APS patients had heightened levels of a circulating marker of NETs, calprotectin. Furthermore, we found calprotectin levels were associated with lower platelet counts in pediatric APS patients, and mechanistically, calprotectin provoked aPL-mediated platelet death. While further studies are warranted, circulating calprotectin can potentially be a functional biomarker for thrombocytopenia among pediatric APS patients.

Our study has several limitations. Due to the cross-sectional design, we are not able to evaluate the durability of non-criteria aPL and NETs markers in our cohort. Moreover, the association between non-criteria aPL and various clinical manifestations in pediatric APS is hypothesis-generating. It will need to be confirmed in other larger cohorts, allowing

<!-- page 8 -->
for adjustment for multiple covariates. The longitudinal relationship between non-criteria aPL, circulating NET markers, and various APS clinical manifestations warrants additional studies. Most of our cohort consists of adolescent patients, so our findings may not be generalizable to the younger pediatric population, whose coagulation and immune systems are in earlier stages of development. Furthermore, our cohort does not include patients with arterial events, limiting the generalizability of these results.

In summary, our study highlights the potential roles of non-criteria aPL and calprotectin, a circulating marker of NETs, as clinically actionable biomarkers in the pediatric APS population. We underscore the unique nature of pediatric APS and the importance of improving the understanding of the non-thrombotic APS clinical manifestations among children. Further research should aim to improve early diagnosis and risk stratification of pediatric APS patients.
