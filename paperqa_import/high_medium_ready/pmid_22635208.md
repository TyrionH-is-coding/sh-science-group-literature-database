# SPECIAL ARTICLE

# Obstetric and vascular APS: Same autoantibodies but different diseases?

PL Meroni $^{1,2}$ , E Raschi $^{3}$ , C Grossi $^{3}$ , F Pregnolato $^{3}$ , L Trespidi $^{4}$ , B Acaia $^{4}$ and MO Borghi $^{2,3}$

<sup>1</sup>Division of Rheumatology, Istituto G Pini, Italy; <sup>2</sup>Department of Internal Medicine, University of Milan, Italy; <sup>3</sup>Experimental Laboratory of Immunological and Rheumatologic Research, IRCCS Istituto Auxologico Italiano, Italy; and <sup>4</sup>Unità Operativa Ostetrica e Ginecologia, Fondazione Ospedale Maggiore, Milan, Italy

Beta2 glycoprotein I ( $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -dependent antiphospholipid antibodies (aPLs) are the main pathogenic autoantibody population and at the same time the laboratory diagnostic tool for the antiphospholipid syndrome (APS).

These antibodies are responsible for both the vascular and the obstetric manifestations of the syndrome but the pathogenic mechanisms behind these manifestations are not the same. For example, thrombotic events do not appear to play a major role in APS miscarriages and a direct reactivity of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -dependent aPLs on decidual and trophoblast cells was reported. A local expression of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ on these tissues was reported both in physiological conditions and in APS women, thus explaining the local tropism of the autoantibodies.

The two hit hypothesis was suggested to explain why the vascular manifestations of APS may occur only occasionally in spite of the persistent presence of aPLs. This is not apparently the case for the obstetric variant of the syndrome, making the difference even more striking. A different pathogenesis may also provide the rationale for the well-known fact that the vascular and the obstetric manifestations may occur independently although in a minority of cases. Lupus (2012) 21, 708-710.

Key words: Antiphospholipid syndrome; pregnancy; thrombosis; anti-β2GPI antibodies

# Introduction

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is characterized by vascular thrombosis and/or pregnancy morbidity in association with antiphospholipid antibodies (aPLs) detectable by anticardiolipin (aCL), anti- $\beta 2$ glycoprotein I (β2GPI) and/or lupus anticoagulant (LA) assays.

Although APS is currently considered as a single disease, the clinical and biological characteristics of the vascular involvement are different from those associated with the obstetrical problems. For example, thrombosis - which appears to be the most common denominator of the vascular events - does not seem to have sole responsibility for the obstetrical complications, for which involvement of additional pathogenic mechanisms has been reported.[2-4] Moreover, it is widely accepted that

there are sub-groups of patients displaying obstetric manifestations only or vascular thrombosis with no pregnancy complications.

aPLs are diagnostic for APS but are also believed to play a pathogenic role, mediating several clinical manifestations of the syndrome.2-4 aPLs reacting with beta2 glycoprotein I (β2GPI) are generally accepted as the main pathogenic autoantibody population.5 There is in fact evidence that β2GPI-dependent aPL assays are much more strongly associated with the clinical manifestations of the syndrome and at the same time that β2GPI-dependent aPLs mediate several experimental APS models.5-7

The diagnostic and pathogenic role of aPLs is the only common denominator for vascular and obstetric APS, a syndrome whose clinical manifestations have become increasingly heterogeneous over the last ten years since additional symptoms have been described. So, demonstration of the fact that thrombotic events cannot explain all the manifestations, and the evidence that a significant minority of patients may display vascular but not obstetric

<!-- page 2 -->
manifestations and vice versa, are raising the hypothesis that we are not dealing with a unique disease but rather with a syndrome, due to the occurrence of aPLs, but in which additional variables may eventually influence the clinical picture.

# Pathogenic mechanisms

The thrombophilic state in APS is related to several mechanisms mediated by aPLs and affecting different fluid phase coagulation stages, such as natural anticoagulants and fibrinolysis. On the other hand, aPLs may also affect cells involved in the coagulation as peripheral blood monocytes, endothelial cells and platelets.[2-5] Most of these mechanisms are mediated by $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -dependent aPLs and require complement (C') activation, although apparently without a clear and systemic inflammation.[5]

Although thrombotic events at the placental level may play a role, it is widely accepted that nonthrombotic mechanisms are the main cause of the defective placentation found in APS. $^{3,5,9}$ $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -dependent aPLs are the most important pathogenic autoantibodies and their specific tropism for placental tissues is probably due to the local overexpression of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ . $^{10-12}$ In particular, $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -dependent aPLs may recognize their own antigen on trophoblast and decidual cells as a 'planted antigen' and it was suggested that the binding affected several trophoblast cell functions eventually leading to a defective placentation. $^{5,10-12}$ At the same time, $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}/$ anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ complex formation may activate C' inducing local inflammatory damage. $^{13}$ While there is convincing evidence for C' activation in some fetal loss experimental models, the clinical evidence for such a mechanism in APS women is still the subject of debate, as discussed in another article in this issue of the journal. $^{14}$

It should be emphasized that a direct effect of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -dependent aPLs on other cell types has also been suggested to explain some recent APS clinical manifestations unrelated to ischemic events such as epilepsy or cognitive abnormalities.[15]

So, in addition to pathogenic mechanisms in common with the vascular variant of the syndrome, obstetric APS manifestations are apparently sustained by 'tissue' specific aPL-mediated events.

As previously stated, there is evidence that $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -dependent aPLs are pathogenic in several in vitro experimental models, but more importantly in most if not all the in vivo APS models for both thrombosis and fetal loss owing to their cross reactivity with the animal $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ molecule.

On the other hand, the suggestion that anti $\beta 2$ GPI antibodies are the only pathogenic aPLs for thrombosis is in apparent contrast with the widely accepted view that LA positivity is strongly associated with the risk of thrombosis and miscarriages.[6,7] In fact, LA activity is more frequently linked to anti-prothrombin (PT) than to anti $\beta 2$ GPI antibodies. This finding raises the possibility that anti-PT antibodies may also play a pathogenic role in APS patients.[16] However, in vivo studies carried out with purified anti-PT polyclonal antibodies are lacking and their pathogenic activity is mainly supported by in vitro experiments performed with monoclonal anti-PT antibodies and by the epidemiological association with thrombosis.[5-7]

# Two hit hypothesis

To explain why thrombotic events occur only occasionally in spite of the persistent presence of aPLs a two hit hypothesis has been suggested. The antibody (first hit) induces a thrombophilic state but clotting takes place only in the presence of another thrombophilic stimulus (second hit). Since human $\beta 2$ GPI-specific aPL IgG display the thrombogenic effect in in vivo animal models after the administration of small amounts of lipopolysaccharide (LPS), it has been suggested that infectious processes may be the second hit in line with the finding that infections are associated with or precede the syndrome and may be the trigger for the catastrophic subtype. This hypothesis fits well with the suggested involvement of Toll-Like Receptors (TLR)-2/4 in endothelial cells and/or monocyte activation by $\beta 2$ GPI-dependent aPLs. In other words, the effect of the infectious process plus the TLR activation mediated by the autoantibodies may exceed the threshold for clotting. Alternatively, infections/inflammation may modulate the expression of the target antigen for aPLs or may induce the expression of antigenic epitopes that are hidden in resting conditions. In line with the first hypothesis, LPS was shown to upregulate $\beta 2$ GPI expression in murine tissues in vivo.

The two hit hypothesis does not fit well with the obstetrical APS. In fact, passive infusion of IgG fractions with aPL activity alone induces fetal loss in naive pregnant mice without requiring any additional factor (second hit). $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ is largely expressed in placental tissues even in physiological conditions and binding of labelled exogenous $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ infused into naive pregnant mice to

<!-- page 3 -->
trophoblast and endothelial cells in the labyrinth was recently documented in vivo.10-12 So, the ample availability of the target antigen for pathogenic aPLs at the placental level is in strong contrast with the lack of a comparable expression in other murine tissues and even in highly vascularized human tissues such as kidney (Agostinis et al.,12 Meroni et al. personal communication). It is possible to speculate that the increased expression of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ at the placental level together with the hormonal and blood flow modification linked to the pregnancy itself may represent local factors - equivalent to the second hit - favouring the pathogenic activity of the autoantibodies.

# Conclusions

$\beta 2$ GPI-dependent aPLs are the pathogenic autoantibodies in APS that mediate both the vascular and the obstetric manifestations. However, the pathogenic mechanisms responsible for miscarriages and pregnancy complications cannot be sustained by thrombotic events only and different aPL-mediated effects have been suggested. This finding emphasizes the fact that the same autoantibody population is apparently able to mediate different effects, making the vascular and the obstetric APSs two conditions which are close but not the same. In line with such a view, there is no evidence that an inflammatory second hit is required for obstetric as it is for vascular APS. A local over-expression of the target autoantigen ( $\beta 2$ GPI) for pathogenic aPLs has been suggested to explain at least in part the pathogenesis of the obstetric manifestations.
