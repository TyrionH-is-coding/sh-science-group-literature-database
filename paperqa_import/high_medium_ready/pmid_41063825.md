# Young-Onset Central Retinal Vein Occlusion with Antiphospholipid Syndrome and Hypophysitis

## Abstract

Introduction: The aim of the study was to describe a rare case of central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO) in an adolescent with primary antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) and coexisting lymphocytic hypophysitis (LH).

Case Presentation: A 17-year-old female presented with acute vision loss in the right eye and was diagnosed with CRVO. She had no cardiovascular risk factors. Fundoscopy revealed optic disc swelling, macular exudates, and venous dilation. Fluorescein angiography demonstrated optic disc leakage without any signs of vasculitis. IgG anticardiolipin antibodies were persistently elevated on two separate occasions, thereby fulfilling the 2023 ACR/EULAR criteria for APS. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed pituitary stalk thickening and asymmetrical intrasellar enhancement, consistent with LH, although visual field loss and optic nerve abnormalities were not detected. A single intravitreal injection of ranibizumab was effective, resulting in complete resolution of serous retinal detachment and improvement in visual acuity. Anticoagulants were also administered. At the 12-month follow-up, no progression of LH was observed.

Conclusion: Coagulation should be thoroughly evaluated in cases of early-onset CRVO without systemic risk factors. This case highlights the importance of considering APS in young patients with CRVO without systemic risk factors. There is a shared immunologic predisposition when coexisting autoimmune disorders such as LH.

Keywords: Central retinal vein occlusion, Antiphospholipid syndrome, Lymphocytic hypophysitis, Autoimmune disease, Adolescent, Hypercoagulability

## Introduction

Retinal vein occlusion is one of the most common retinal vascular disorders, typically characterized by retinal hemorrhage and macular edema resulting from impaired venous outflow. Central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO), a subtype of retinal vein occlusion involving obstruction of the central retinal vein, can occasionally lead to severe vision loss accompanied by neovascular glaucoma [ 1 , 2 ]. CRVO predominantly affects older adults, and its pathogenesis is thought to involve venous stasis and thrombus formation, often secondary to compression by the adjacent sclerotic central retinal artery [ 3 , 4 ]. Although CRVO is rare in younger individuals, approximately 10% of cases occur in patients under the age of 40, with only a minority affecting those younger than 24 years [ 5 , 6 ]. This rarity may be attributed to the absence of atherosclerotic changes in the central retinal artery, which plays a key role in the pathogenesis of CRVO [ 7 ]. Since traditional risk factors such as hypertension and diabetes are uncommon in younger populations, alternative etiologies, such as hypercoagulable states and autoimmune disorders, should be considered when CRVO is diagnosed in young patients. Among the hypercoagulable disorders, antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a relatively common acquired cause of thrombosis in young adults [ 8 ]. APS is defined by the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies, such as anticardiolipin antibodies and lupus anticoagulants, which promote thrombosis through endothelial injury and immune-mediated mechanisms [ 9 , 10 ]. APS has also been implicated in various vascular occlusions, including retinal vessels. However, APS-associated CRVO in adolescents, particularly in the absence of systemic autoimmune diseases like systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), has only rarely been reported [ 9 , 11 , 12 ]. The previously reported cases of APS-related CRVO typically involve patients with concurrent autoimmune diseases, such as SLE. Only a few reports have described primary APS manifesting as CRVO in teenagers without coexisting systemic autoimmune diseases.

Lymphocytic hypophysitis (LH) is a rare autoimmune inflammatory disease of the pituitary gland, which is more frequently seen in women, particularly during late pregnancy and the postpartum period, but it can also occur in nonpregnant individuals. Notably, up to one-third of cases are associated with other autoimmune disorders or circulating autoantibodies, suggesting an underlying immune dysregulation [ 13 , 14 ].

After conducting a literature review on June 15, 2025, utilizing PubMed, Google Scholar, and Ichushi-Web using the keywords “central retinal vein occlusion,” “antiphospholipid syndrome,” and “lymphocytic hypophysitis,” we did not find any prior reports of CRVO associated with both APS and LH in an adolescent patient. This report describes a case of CRVO in a 17-year-old female who fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for primary APS and with coexisting LH based on systemic evaluation and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). This case highlights the importance of considering autoimmune conditions such as APS in young patients with CRVO, as well as the potential contribution of underlying immune dysregulation, such as coexisting LH.

## Case Report

A 17-year-old female presented to our hospital for evaluation of sudden blurred vision and decreased visual acuity in the right eye. She had no significant medical history. Her only medication was antihistamines, with no history of oral contraceptive use. At presentation, her best corrected visual acuity was 0.7 and 1.2 in the right and left eyes, respectively. She reported that visual symptoms in the right eye had developed approximately 2 weeks prior and had persisted since onset. Fundoscopy revealed optic disc swelling, dilated and tortuous retinal veins, and peripheral retinal hemorrhages in the right eye. The intraocular pressure was 16 and 17 mm Hg in the right and left eyes, respectively. Her blood pressure was 119/66 mm Hg. A mild relative afferent pupillary defect was noted in the right eye. The central flicker frequency was 27.5 and 41.2 Hz in the right and left eyes, respectively. No abnormalities were detected in the anterior segment or vitreous of either eye, and no signs of inflammation were observed.

Optical coherence tomography images revealed serous retinal detachment and stellate macular exudates, as well as significant optic disc edema with elevation and thickening of the optic nerve head ( Fig. 1 a–d). No obvious abnormalities were observed in the left eye. Fluorescein angiography of the right eye revealed retinal venous dilation and fluorescein leakage from the optic disc, with no signs of retinal vasculitis ( Fig. 2 ). Goldmann perimetry demonstrated enlargement of the Mariotte blind spot by approximately 6° and constriction of the I-2 isopter by about 27° in the right eye. The left eye showed no abnormalities ( Fig. 3 ).

The retinal veins are dilated and tortuous, whereas the arteries appear normal. c Optical coherence tomography (OCT) image of the macular area exhibiting serous retinal detachment (SRD) and stellate macular exudates (yellow arrow). d OCT image of the right eye revealing significant optic disc edema with pronounced thickening and elevation of the optic nerve head (white arrow).

There were no areas of capillary nonperfusion or signs of retinal vasculitis suggestive of uveitis.

No abnormal signals were observed in the bilateral optic nerves or brain parenchyma.

Blood tests, including complete blood count, coagulation panel, and metabolic markers, were within normal limits. Moreover, inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate) were not elevated, thereby ruling out infectious etiologies. Notably, IgG anticardiolipin antibodies were persistently elevated on two separate occasions, thereby fulfilling the 2023 American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism (ACR/EULAR) classification criteria for APS. Other antiphospholipid antibodies (i.e., IgM aCL, LAC, anti-β2GP1 IgG/IgM) were within the normal ranges.

Due to the pronounced optic disc swelling, contrast-enhanced MRI was performed to evaluate potential retrobulbar optic nerve involvement. This revealed asymmetrical enhancement within the sella turcica, thickening and enhancement of the pituitary stalk, and an upwardly protruding intrasellar lesion abutting the optic chiasm, which was consistent with LH. No abnormalities were observed in the optic nerves, brain parenchyma, or vasculature on magnetic resonance angiography ( Fig. 4 ).

**Fig:** Fig. 4. Goldmann perimetry (GP) of both eyes. The right eye had an enlarged Mariotte blind spot, extending approximately 6° more compared to the fellow eye, as well as constriction of the isopter, with the I-2 isopter reduced by approximately 27°(red arrow). No visual field abnormalities were observed in the left eye.

Due to the pituitary abnormalities detected on MRI, hormonal testing was conducted, revealing mildly elevated levels of growth hormone, thyroid-stimulating hormone, and antidiuretic hormone. Additionally, thyroid autoantibodies were mildly elevated. Anti-aquaporin-4 antibody, a known marker of neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, was negative.

An intravitreal injection of ranibizumab (0.5 mg) was administered to improve the macular edema. One month later, the serous retinal detachment had resolved, and improvements were noted in venous tortuosity, optic disc swelling, and best corrected visual acuity, which increased to 1.2 in the right eye. Figure 5 showed 1-month follow-up optical coherence tomography and Optos fundus photography after intravitreal injection. Although optic disc swelling and stellate macular exudates persisted, both retinal hemorrhages and macular edema showed signs of improvement.

**Fig:** Fig. 5. One month follow-up findings after intravitreal ranibizumab injection. a , b Horizontal and vertical optical coherence tomography scans of the macula showing resolution of the serous retinal detachment (SRD) and reduced intraretinal fluid (yellow arrow). c Optos fundus photograph showing the resolution of the retinal hemorrhages, with residual stellate exudates at the macula and disc swelling.

Four months later, MRI revealed no change in the size of the pituitary lesion. To prevent systemic thrombosis, low-dose warfarin therapy was initiated. The lesion in the LH was managed conservatively with regular MRI monitoring. Throughout the follow-up period, the patient did not exhibit clinical symptoms such as polyuria, menstrual irregularities, or visual field disturbance, and pituitary hormone levels remained stable without requiring hormone replacement therapy. At the 12-month follow-up, no progression of the lesions was observed.

## Discussion

APS is a significant acquired cause of venous thrombosis, particularly in young adults. Among patients aged 18–50 presenting with a first unprovoked venous thromboembolism, approximately 9% meet the diagnostic criteria for APS [ 10 ]. Moreover, APS has been implicated in up to 20% of cases of deep vein thrombosis. Thus, APS should be considered in the differential diagnosis of thrombotic events in younger populations [ 8 ]. APS can be classified into either primary APS, which occurs without an underlying systemic disease, or secondary APS, which is associated with autoimmune conditions such as SLE. Secondary APS accounts for approximately 47% of all cases of APS, with SLE being the most common underlying disorder (77.1%), followed by lupus-like syndrome (10.7%), Sjögren’s syndrome (4.7%), rheumatoid arthritis (3.8%), systemic sclerosis (1.5%), systemic vasculitis (1.5%), and dermatomyositis (1.1%) [ 15 ]. Meanwhile, among patients with SLE, 20%–40% eventually develop secondary APS [ 16 ], thereby highlighting the strong association between the two diseases.

This case describes a 17-year-old female who developed CRVO in the absence of traditional cardiovascular risk factors. Persistent elevation of IgG anticardiolipin antibodies led to a diagnosis of primary APS, with no clinical or serologic findings of SLE or other connective tissue diseases. Only a few reports have described primary APS manifesting as CRVO in individuals under 20 years of age without comorbid autoimmune disease [ 9 , 11 , 12 ]. Notably, systemic evaluation via MRI revealed pituitary enlargement and infundibular thickening, which are findings consistent with LH, a rare autoimmune inflammatory condition of the pituitary gland. Although LH is occasionally associated with SLE and other autoimmune diseases, no such associations were observed in this case.

The coexistence of primary APS and LH is extremely rare, with only one previous case report describing their association in a patient with panhypopituitarism [ 17 ]. Although LH was not considered an underlying cause of APS in this case, their coexistence may reflect a broader autoimmune predisposition. Imaging revealed no evidence of optic nerve compression or optic neuritis, and no clinical signs of optic neuropathy were detected, suggesting that LH did not directly contribute to CRVO. However, several reports have described optic nerve dysfunction in LH via mechanisms independent of the mass effect. For instance, Uemura et al. [ 18 ] reported a case of bilateral optic neuritis associated with LH, wherein the visual evoked potentials had prolonged latencies despite the absence of radiographic optic nerve compression. This suggests that the local autoimmune inflammation from LH can indirectly affect the optic nerve via parainflammatory mechanisms. Therefore, although LH was not causally linked to CRVO in this patient, its presence may reflect a broader autoimmune context that could enhance the risk of vascular events in the background of APS. The coexistence of LH and APS, albeit rare, may indicate a shared underlying immunological dysregulation [ 17 ].

Although optic disc swelling and macular exudates are common in CRVO, the presence of a stellate pattern of maculopathy is atypical. This appearance – characterized by star-shaped hard exudates centered around the fovea – is more commonly associated with neuroretinitis, a condition often linked to infectious or inflammatory etiologies such as cat-scratch disease, toxoplasmosis, or idiopathic inflammation [ 19 , 20 ]. In our case, no signs of systemic infection or intraocular inflammation were found, and the diagnosis of CRVO was supported by the presence of retinal venous dilation, flame-shaped hemorrhages, and the absence of vasculitis on fluorescein angiography. Nevertheless, we agree that neuroretinitis and other inflammatory optic neuropathies, such as LH-related optic neuritis, should be considered in the differential diagnosis. A relevant report by Uemura et al. [ 18 ] describes optic nerve involvement in LH without compressive findings, underscoring that immune-mediated mechanisms can mimic inflammatory disc pathology. Another report described CRVO associated with Bartonella henselae infection that initially presented with similar fundus findings [ 21 ]. Although our patient’s presentation did not meet the clinical or laboratory criteria for infectious or inflammatory optic neuropathy, the consideration of these differential diagnoses is crucial for accurate diagnosis and management.

The present case suggests that in young patients with CRVO who do not have traditional risk factors, APS should be included in the differential diagnosis. Furthermore, if autoimmune comorbidities are present, a comprehensive systemic evaluation is warranted to assess the thrombotic and inflammatory risk, including coagulation tests, autoantibody screening, hormonal assays, and neuroimaging. This case underscores the importance of evaluating the autoimmune and thrombotic mechanisms in young patients with CRVO. Although APS was the likely cause of CRVO, the incidental detection of LH suggests a complex autoimmune predisposition in this patient.

## Patient’s Perspective

The patient expressed relief after a diagnosis was reached and appreciated the multidisciplinary approach. She has remained stable on anticoagulation therapy and under regular endocrinologic follow-up.
