# Abstract

<!-- page 7 -->
Lupus anticoagulant (LA) is a well-established risk factor for the clinical manifestations of antiphospholipid syndrome (APS). Accurate LA detection is an essential prerequisite for optimal diagnosis and management of patients with APS or aPL carriers. Variability remains a challenge in LA testing, with reliable detection influenced by multiple factors, including pre-analytical conditions, anticoagulation treatment, choice of tests and procedures performed, as well as interpretation of results, that can lead to false-positives or negatives. A standardised approach to LA testing, following current guidance, based on published data and international consensus, and with attention to detail, is required to underpin accurate detection of LA. Future work should focus on better characterisation of the nature of LA, which may ultimately lead to improved diagnosis and management of patients with APS and aPL carriers. This article reviews current practice and challenges, providing an overview on detection of LA.

Keywords: antiphospholipid syndrome, thrombosis, pregnancy morbidity, antiphospholipid antibodies, lupus anticoagulant, dilute Russell's viper venom time, Taiwan snake venom time/earin time, silica clotting time, three-step procedure.

# Lupus anticoagulant: definition and historical perspective

Lupus anticoagulant (LA) is one of the three criteria antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL) [along with IgG and/or IgM anticardiolipin (aCL) and anti-beta2 glycoprotein I antibodies (aβ2GI)], for diagnosis of antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), an acquired autoimmune prothrombotic disorder, with the cardinal clinical features thrombosis (venous, arterial and/or microvascular) and/or obstetric morbidity [1]. The phenomenon of LA was identified in 1952 by Conley and Hartmann, who reported a circulating anticoagulant in two patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) with haemorrhagic symptoms [2]. In 1972, Feinstein and Rapaport conferred the term 'lupus anticoagulant' [3]. The relationship between LA and thrombosis was noted in 1963 [4] and an association with intrauterine fetal death and severe fetal growth restriction associated with placental infarction was identified in 1975 [5]. In 1981, Carreras et al. reported an association with recurrent

miscarriages [6]. aPL are a heterogeneous family of immunoglobulins that, in complex with proteins such as $\beta 2$ -GPI, prothrombin and others, interact with negatively charged phospholipids.

In contrast to the other two criteria aPL, LA are not defined by antigenic targets and detection is based on their in vitro functional activity [1]. The LA mechanism of action involves binding to its target proteins/antibodies and to phospholipids, blocking the binding of the prothrombinase complex to phospholipids that delays conversion of prothrombin to thrombin and prolongs clotting times. LA interact with prothrombin/phospholipid and $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ phospholipid complexes and may also have additional target proteins [7-9].

# Clinical relevance of lupus anticoagulant

LA is a well-established risk factor for APS-associated clinical manifestations.

# Thrombosis

An early systematic review reported that LA are stronger risk factors for thrombosis than aCL, with LA showing significant associations with cerebral stroke and deep venous thrombosis (DVT) [10]. The Vienna Lupus Anticoagulant and Thrombosis prospective, observational study (LATS) demonstrated that new onset thrombosis was an independent risk factor for increased mortality in LA-positive individuals [11]. In a prospective study, carriers of LA had a 3.9-fold increased risk of developing venous thromboembolism (VTE) than LA-negative aPL carriers [12]. Triple aPL positivity (presence of all three criteria aPL) is associated with high thrombotic risk: a cumulative incidence of $26.1\%$ (95% CI, 22.3-29.9) after 5 years in triple aPL-positive APS patients [13]; and in triple aPL-positive carriers, an annual risk of $5.3\%$ after an average follow-up of 4.5 years [14].

The presence of aPL strengthens the decision to offer lifelong anticoagulation following a first unprovoked VTE [15]. In this regard, a prospective study suggested that aPL and raised D-dimer levels are independent risk factors for recurrence after a first unprovoked VTE [16]. Another consideration in aPL-positive individuals is the review of appropriateness of long-term direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) treatment (standard treatment following a first VTE in the general population) [17, 18].

In individuals $< 50$ years, $17\%$ of strokes and $12\%$ of transient ischaemic attacks (TIA), the commonest arterial manifestations in APS patients, are associated with persistent aPL [19]. It is of clinical importance to diagnose APS-associated acute ischaemic stroke (AIS), as antithrombotic treatment differs from the standard treatment for non-cardioembolic AIS of antiplatelet treatment alone. In patients with APS-associated AIS, treatment with vitamin K antagonist (VKA), target INR (International Normalised Ratio) range 2-3 (standard intensity) or 3-4 (high intensity) is recommended, considering the individual's risk of bleeding and recurrent thrombosis. VKA, target INR range 2-3 plus low dose aspirin (LDA) may also be considered [20]. Optimisation of traditional cardiovascular risk factors to minimise risk of recurrent thrombosis, influenced by the adjusted global APS Score (aGAPSS) [21], and potentially, also by vitamin D status [22] should be undertaken, and vitamin D insufficiency/deficiency corrected [18].

# Obstetric morbidity

Two systematic reviews and meta-analyses suggest that LA is associated with pre-eclampsia (OR 2.34; $95\%$ CI 1.18-4.64), fetal growth restriction (OR 4.65 $95\%$ CI 1.29-16.71) and late fetal loss (OR 4.73; $95\%$ CI 1.08-20.81) [23, 24]. In the PROMISSE (Predictors of Pregnancy Outcome: Biomarkers in APS and SLE) prospective, observational study, LA was the primary predictor of adverse pregnancy outcome after 12 weeks' gestation in aPL-associated pregnancies: $39\%$ of patients with LA had adverse pregnancy outcome vs $3\%$ of those without LA $(P < 0.0001)$ [25]. This observation was confirmed in an independent group of aPL-positive patients subsequently recruited into the PROMISSE study [26]. Ruffatti et al. reported that triple aPL positivity and/or a history of thromboembolism (TE) predicted new TE events $\mathrm{OR} = 57.5$ , $95\%$ CI 2.7-1160, $P = 0.0004$ and new unsuccessful pregnancies $\mathrm{OR} = 34.4$ , $95\%$ CI 3.5-335.1, $P = 0.003$ [27]. aPL have classically been associated with

recurrent early miscarriages, but definitive evidence to support this association is lacking and well-designed studies are required to better define the association between aPL and recurrent early pregnancy loss [28].

# Systemic lupus erythematosus

In SLE, $30 - 40\%$ of patients are positive for aPL [29], with the prevalence of a positive LA and aCL varying between $11\%$ and $30\%$ , and $17\%$ and $40\%$ , respectively [30-32]. Thrombotic APS, found in $\sim 15\%$ of patients with SLE, was reported to be a major predictor of irreversible organ damage and death [33]. However, the same group subsequently reported that aPL did not predict damage accrual in patients diagnosed in more recent years (after 2000), with hydroxychloroquine the main individual protecting factor against damage in aPL-positive patients with SLE [34].

# Patient selection for testing for lupus anticoagulant/antiphospholipid antibodies

LA/aPL testing should be performed when there are clinical features suggestive of APS [35, 36]. Suggested indications include patients with recurrent thrombosis unexplained by subtherapeutic anticoagulation, patient non-adherence or malignancy, and thrombosis in unusual sites (i.e. other than lower limb DVT or pulmonary embolism). Following a provoked VTE where the provoking environmental factor is disproportionately mild or in younger patients (<50 years) with an unprovoked TE [36], LA/aPL testing should be considered, particularly if it is a major thrombotic event. LA/aPL testing should be considered in younger patients (<50 years) with AIS, TIA or other evidence of brain ischaemia, or arterial thrombosis in other sites [36].

Other indications for LA/aPL testing include pregnancy morbidity: fetal loss after 10 weeks, recurrent early (first trimester) miscarriages, prematurity (<34 weeks' gestation) associated with severe (pre)eclampsia/HELLP syndrome, placental insufficiency (fetal growth restriction) and stillbirth [36]. In SLE, LA/aPL are part of the diagnostic criteria and contribute to risk assessment [20, 36]. LA testing could be considered in other situations, including immune thrombocytopenia, particularly with presence of arthralgias or arthritis, hair loss, sun sensitivity, mouth ulcers, rash, thromboembolism; younger patients (<50 years) with noncriteria clinical manifestations, e.g. cognitive dysfunction, valvular heart disease with presence of evidence of other systemic autoimmune diseases; patients with unexplained prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) [36].

# Testing for lupus anticoagulant

Optimal laboratory detection of LA and interpretation of results can be affected by a number of factors that could impact accurate diagnosis of APS/aPL. The key factors are addressed below, in accordance with International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis Scientific and Standardisation Scientific and Standardisation Committee (ISTH-SSC) guidance [36].

# Timing of LA testing

Results of LA tests during the acute phase, as in the early post-thrombotic state, should be interpreted with caution as they may be confounded by acute phase reactants, such as raised coagulation factor (F) VIII levels or C-reactive protein, as well as effects of anticoagulation [43-45]. Testing during the acute phase, however, may be required, as in catastrophic APS [46]. It may be preferable to defer screening for aPL for most patients with acute VTE until 12 weeks post-VTE, by which time acute phase changes would have resolved. However, earlier LA testing may be performed if there is clinical suspicion of APS (e.g. patients with a new VTE and obstetric or non-criteria manifestations of APS), with appropriate interpretation of the results.

Early assessment for LA (and other aPL) after AIS/TIA may benefit patients [20, 36]. However, the influence of early $\nu s$ later initiation of anticoagulation on the outcome following AIS/TIA is unknown [36]. Notably, in an ISTH survey, $64.4\%$ (65 of 101) of respondents imposed no restriction on when to test for aPL after an acute arterial thrombotic event; and over half $(56.4\%)$ , 57 of 101) would start anticoagulation prior to establishing that aPL are persistently positive, with comments indicating that this might be contingent on clinical features (severity of event, antiplatelet therapy failure, evidence of an embolic source and bleeding risk) and/or the laboratory aPL profile (perceived higher risk aPL profiles: triple positivity, a high titer of aPL and LA positivity) [47].

Positive LA tests should be rechecked after at least 12 weeks to establish persistence.

LA positivity may fluctuate during pregnancy, and results during pregnancy should be interpreted with caution. Repeat LA testing should be undertaken postdelivery to establish true LA/aPL status, although the optimal timing of testing is not established [36]. It is reasonable to repeat LA testing six weeks postdelivery, as pregnancy-induced increases in levels of blood coagulation factors have been reported to be resolve by then [48]. LA tests have been shown to return to baseline status when assessed three months postpartum [49].

aPL can occur in association with many infections, including COVID-19/SARS-CoV-2, and other viral infections such

as human immunodeficiency virus and hepatitis C, with the prevalence of LA in patients with COVID-19 at $50.7\%$ in a meta-analysis and systematic review of 1159 patients. COVID-19 associated aPL were generally transient, disappearing with no associations with mortality or thrombosis [50]. The potential for viral infection-associated aPL should be considered when interpreting LA testing, and that COVID-19-aPL may persist for approximately six months [51].

# Blood collection and sample preparation

Venous samples should be collected into $0.109\mathrm{mol} / \mathrm{l}$ sodium citrate with a 9:1 blood to sodium citrate ratio [35, 52]. Platelet poor plasma (PPP) (platelet count $< 10\times 10^{9} / \mathrm{l}$ ), prepared by double centrifugation of blood at $2000\mathrm{g}$ for $15\mathrm{min}$ at room temperature, should be used [53]. This is crucial as any residual platelets will enable binding of LA on their activated surface, that expresses negatively charged phospholipids, resulting in false-positive results [53]. PPP should be used immediately after preparation, as it is unstable at room temperature, or frozen within four h of collection at $-80^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ [36, 54]. Freeze/thawing should be avoided as it can lead to release of phospholipids from residual platelets and potential quenching of LA and thus false-negative results [55, 56]; and frozen samples should be rapidly thawed at 37 degrees in a water bath for about five min [57]. False positive/negative results can occur with haemolysed, lipoemic or icteric samples [58, 59].

# Detection of lupus anticoagulant

Prior to LA testing, a coagulation screen [prothrombin time (PT), aPTT, thrombin time and fibrinogen activity] should be performed to provide information on coagulopathy and guide appropriate choice of LA methodology. Abnormalities may reflect the effects of anticoagulation, coagulation factor deficiencies or inhibitors, or presence of acute phase reactants [54]. For routine aPTT testing, an LA-insensitive reagent should be used to avoid unnecessary evaluations for LA [36, 54].

# Screening test

This should include a phospholipid-dependent assay in which prolongation of clotting time in a phospholipid-dependent manner is suggestive of LA positivity.

# Mixing test

This will discriminate between the presence of an inhibitor or coagulation factor deficiency(ies). The mixing test is performed on equal volumes of test plasma and normal pooled plasma (NPP). If % correction is used, and there is correction of the clotting time to within the normal range, this would suggest that the NPP has corrected any deficiency(ies) in clotting factor(s), whereas a persistently prolonged clotting time without any correction is indicative of the presence of an inhibitor such as LA [60, 63]. If normalised ratios are used, the normalised mixing test ratio should be above the locally established mixing cut-off value to be suggestive of the presence of LA [61].

The NPP used can be locally or commercially prepared from 20-40 donors [35, 36, 54, 61, 62]. Inclusion of a mixing step reduces the possibility of false-positive or negative results [64-66]. Usually, a mixing test is performed on the screening assays, but it can also be performed on the confirmatory test if the clotting time on the neat plasma is prolonged. A mixing step on the confirmatory step increases diagnostic efficacy: (i) when prolongation of clotting time is due to LA plus a factor deficiency; (ii) the LA is strong enough to overcome the excess of phospholipid added; and (iii) when the tests are performed on anticoagulation [64, 67, 68].

# Confirmatory step

This should comprise excess phospholipid concentration in the reagent, as an overwhelming quantity of phospholipid will eliminate competition for phospholipid-binding to LA, with correction of clotting time usually to within the reference range. When normalised ratio cut-offs are used, LA presence is confirmed when ratios are above the established cut-off. The overall LA procedure should include comparison of clotting time with a screening test with limited phospholipid concentration and clotting time of a confirmatory test using excess of phospholipid [36, 54, 60].

ISTH-SSC guidance advises that a screening test should be performed, followed by a confirmatory and mixing test simultaneously after an abnormal screening test [36].

# Tests for lupus anticoagulant

The three-step procedure can be applied to various tests for LA [54, 60]. However, due to challenges with various assays and concerns about the number of false-positive results associated with an increased number of tests, the ISTH-SSC guidance has recommended restriction of assays for LA to only DRVVT and LA-sensitive aPTT [36, 61]. This recommendation is based on association of this pairing with high detection rates, lower false-positive frequency and a reduction in the inter-laboratory and inter-assay variability usually observed [36].

The DRVVT is the mainstay for LA detection [69-71]. However, differences between commercial reagents result in variations in diagnostic performance [72-74]. The DRVTT is sensitive to pathological aβ2GPI and can also detect LA activity caused by antibodies against prothrombin [36]. Similarly, aPTT-based assays can also detect both subtypes of LA. Special care should be taken in choosing an assay that is ideally designed for LA detection and in the use of reagents with good sensitivity [75, 76]. The majority of LA-sensitive aPTT assays employ silica as an activator (silica clotting time; SCT) [61, 77, 78] and dilute phospholipids resulting in a dilute aPTT [79]. The sensitivity of the aPTT assay used should be independently evaluated by individual laboratories using well-characterised LA-positive samples [36, 61, 77, 78].

Due to the complexity of LA testing in addition to the lack of gold standard reference plasmas and gold standard assays, ISTH-SSC guidance acknowledges that other assays with similar diagnostic strength can also be performed [36]. Taipan snake venom time (TSVT) is a dilute phospholipids screening test based on FII activation by the Coastal Taipan venom that is FV independent [80]. The Ecarin time (ET) does not employ phospholipids and therefore LA cannot affect the assay, making it the perfect confirmatory test [81, 82]. TSVT and ET venom can activate the undercarboxylated FII generated during VKA anticoagulation, making it potentially the most suitable assay to be performed while on anticoagulation (as less affected by VKA and FXa-inhibitor DOACs). In addition, TSVT has been shown to detect LAs that both DRVVT and aPTT assays cannot detect [80, 81, 83-85]. The TSVT/ECT has been validated by the ISTH-SSC in a multicentre study showing good sensitivity for LA detection in patients on VKA and DOAC FXa inhibitors, and also non-anticoagulated patients. In this study the First World Health Organization International Reference Panel for LA (13/172) was used. However confirmatory studies with standardized assays are required before it can be recommended for diagnostic use [81]. Of note, interference with the TSVT/ET was seen with direct thrombin inhibitors, UFH and LMWH [81].

# Anticoagulation interference

If anticoagulation should not be discontinued for clinical reasons, strategies to mitigate potential anticoagulant effects are outlined in Tables 1 and 3 and summarised below.

# Patients on VKA

Tests should be performed on an equal mixture of plasma with PNP. Results should be interpreted with caution as mixing can lead to false-negative results, as weak LAs can be missed. TSVT/ET tests appear to be the best alternative for VKA-treated patients as this test system is the least affected.

# Patients on UFH

aPTT assays can be affected; also, DRVVT, if UFH levels exceed the heparin neutralising ability of the reagent and a concomitant anti-Xa should be checked.

# Patients on LMWH

LA can be performed (with caution as LMWH can affect aPTT and SCT tests), with the first line test the DRVVT, with a concomitant anti-FXa LMWH assay to check that LMWH levels are within the heparin neutralising ability of the reagent.

# Patients on DOAC

The TSVT/ET assay could be an alternative for patients on direct anti-FXa inhibitors, but not for those on direct thrombin inhibitor [81]. Commercially available absorption techniques, based on activated carbon, remove DOAC from plasma [87,

88]. Filtration techniques are also available for DOAC removal from plasma samples [89-91]. However, these solutions are not standardised, with concerns that DOAC removal may be incomplete, especially when levels are supratherapeutic, or uncertainty about the effects of these products/filtration on LA testing [38, 41, 89, 91, 92]. A recent study that compared DOAC adsorption and TSVT/ET for anti-FXa DOAC highlighted uncertainty and disagreement between the two methods [93]. Further studies are needed to define the optimal LA testing strategy in DOAC-treated patients.

# Interpretation of results

Results should be interpreted either according to the specific cut-off values by calculating either the percentage correction [(screen - confirm)/screen $\times$ 100] [94] or by expressing as normalised ratios with test-specific cut-off [95, 96]. Normalised ratios are calculated by dividing the clotting time of the tested sample by the clotting time of the PNP that has been tested in the same run. This procedure has been shown to reduce variations introduced by reagents, instruments and operators [36].

Prolongation of the clotting time with the screening and the mixing test (above the locally derived cut-off), but correction within the normal range with the confirmatory test (where a modified phospholipid reagent is used) is indicative of phospholipid-dependent prolongation and therefore LA positivity. If both the screening and confirmatory tests are prolonged but the mixing test is normal, and the patient is not on anticoagulation, then it is suggested that procoagulant factor activity levels be measured to identify the reason for the prolongation. In the case that coagulation factor levels are within normal ranges, the LA test should be repeated. When normalised ratio cut-offs are used, results of each step (screening, mixing and confirmation) should be above the test-specific cut-offs to indicate LA presence.

Mixing tests should be interpreted according to a mixing test specific cut-off expressed as a normalised ratio as this has been shown to increase sensitivity compared with the previously widely used index of circulating anticoagulant [36, 95, 96].

# Cut-off values

The ISTH-SSC guidance recommends establishing locally the 99th centile as the most appropriate cut-off as this reduces false positives and has higher specificity despite the also associated lower sensitivity [36]. It is suggested to use nonparametric procedures as LA test results do not appear to be normally distributed. Identification of outliers prior to calculation of cut-offs should be performed, regardless of which method is used. Methods such as Reed-Dixon are recommended for simplicity [97]. It could be argued that a lower cut-off, e.g. 95th centile, could enable identification of weaker LA that may be of clinical significance. In addition, establishing the 99th centile requires a minimum of 120 healthy donors [98] with previous data suggesting closer to 300 donors, something that is prohibitive for many diagnostic laboratories [97]. However, the 99th centile remains the best suggested approach for establishing cut-offs.

If the above approach is not feasible, validation of the manufacturer's cut-off values could be performed using 40 healthy controls [36, 54, 62].

# Reporting of lupus anticoagulant results

Reporting of the results should be accompanied by the cut-off values for all three step procedures and results should be reported as positive or negative [13, 14, 25]. If the LA result is borderline, this should be reported with a comment, and testing should be repeated after a week or more. Any positive results are advised to be repeated after an interval of at least 12 weeks to confirm persistence and exclude transient LA. The LA results should be reported together with aCL and aB2GPI results for accurate diagnosis of APS [36].

# Other tests for LA detection

None of the aforementioned tests for LA detection provides an optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity, hence the search for better performing coagulation assays in LA testing continues.

The calibrated automated thrombography thrombin generation (TG) assay provides a global assessment of coagulation function and may be able to contribute to the diagnosis and management of APS [99, 100]. With use of the normalized TG-derived peak height/lag time ratio, TG has been shown to identify LA in plasma with high sensitivity, irrespective of the patient's anticoagulation treatment, something that can potentially be utilised to quantify LA in a single assay [65, 101]. However, TG use in routine practice needs standardisation and remains complex, as it is a heavily user-specific assay. ISTH-SSC recommendations on the measurement of TG could help in better agreement between methods and support its application in APS diagnosis and management [102]. More recently, fully automated TG analysers have been introduced, with customised reagents sensitive to procoagulant and anticoagulant protein deficiencies. These analysers are designed to offer enhanced reproducibility and standardisation with the use of dedicated calibrators and controls, with the aim of reducing inter-laboratory and inter-assay variability [103] that may aid in LA detection without the need to perform multiple assays.

# Interactions with other antiphospholipid antibodies: antiphosphatidylserine/ prothrombin and anti- $\beta 2$ glycoprotein I antibodies

The coexistence of LA and anti- $\beta 2$ GPI, together with aCL, identifies APS patients or aPL carriers at high risk for TE and pregnancy morbidity [104]. Antibodies to b2GPI and those directed to prothrombin are seen as the major autoantibodies responsible for LA activity. LA activity has been long reported to be due to the presence of a $\beta 2$ GPI in patients with triple aPL positivity [105, 106]. Those a2GPI directed to domain 1 of the molecule (anti-domain 1) display LA activity and are associated with thrombosis [105, 106].

Bevers et al. demonstrated that antibodies to prothrombin (aPT) have LA activity. It was also demonstrated that some antibodies responsible for LA activity were able to bind to prothrombin in certain experimental conditions [8]. In addition, prothrombin was shown to be required for expressing LA activity in aCL-depleted LA-positive plasma samples [9, 107]. However, evidence suggests that not aPT, but antibodies to phosphatidylserine/prothrombin complex (aPS/PT) are the ones clinically relevant for thrombosis and pregnancy morbidity [108] and reported to strongly correlate with the LA [108], with aPS/PT being reported positive in around $55\%$ [109] and up to $100\%$ of LA-positive individuals [110, 111]. While aPS/PT seems to confer a higher risk of VTE and pregnancy loss in the LA-positive group when compared with the LA-negative group, the risk is present in both groups, with the IgM isotype the most commonly reported [104, 112, 113]. Due to the strength of these associations, some authors have suggested their use as a surrogate test for LA when this functional assay cannot be performed in a reliable manner [113]. However, our data shows that while aPS/PT confer a higher risk of thrombosis in patients with LA, this risk is independent of the presence of LA [114, 115]. There remains controversy in this area: ISTH SSC studies have reported that aPS/PT analysis is not essential for thrombotic APS diagnosis, but could be useful if a double positive aPL profile $(\mathrm{aCL} + / \mathrm{ab2GPI + })$ is present [104]; and the added value of aPS/PT testing in the current diagnostic workup of obstetric-APS seems limited compared with LA, aCL and aβ2GPI, although aPS/PT might be useful in specific subsets of patients [116]. Future multicentre studies are needed for definitive conclusions.

Recent data from APS ACTION brought back the concept of 'aPL load' into the picture [116]. In 2010, Roubey suggested that a positive LA test could signify the presence of a high concentration of aPL and reflect the combined effect of all antibodies (i.e. aCL plus aβ2GPI plus aPT) or 'load' in a particular specimen [117]. Recently, the aPL load was calculated for each of 351 patients to assess the overall aPL burden. This study showed that for every 1-point increase in the aPL load, the possibility of a positive LA test increased by $32\%$ , suggesting that the combined quantitative burden of aPL may provide a mechanistic explanation of a positive LA [116]. As a functional assay, LA presence may imply a high aPL concentration that is sufficient to turn the functional assay positive. Future work in this area should enable further delineation of the nature of LA.

# Conclusions and future directions

LA is a well-established risk factor for clinical manifestations of APS. It is integral to the diagnosis of APS and informs the optimal management of patients with APS or aPL carriers.

Accurate LA detection is an essential prerequisite for optimal diagnosis and management of patients with APS or aPL carriers. Variability remains a challenge in LA testing, with reliable detection influenced by multiple factors, including preanalytical conditions, anticoagulation treatment, choice of tests and procedures performed, and interpretation of results, that can lead to false-positives or negatives. A standardised approach to LA testing, following current guidance, based on published data and international consensus, and with attention to detail, is required to underpin accurate detection of LA. Clinical studies involving LA detection are limited by the challenging methodological issues and are to an extent addressed in the APS ACTION Registry cohort, with clinical aspects linked to an international network of Core Laboratories, using standardised methodology for LA testing.

Questions for the future include whether a clearer understanding of the nature of LA could lead to replacement of the current coagulation-based assays with their inherent challenges, and whether quantitative assessment of LA activity could be achieved. TG assays are not developed or standardised enough to be considered for inclusion in the diagnostic analytical tests. The TVST/ET should be considered following further validation. LA detection remains a challenge and future work should include further characterisation of LA to delineate the paradox and nature of LA, which may ultimately lead to improved diagnosis and management of patients with APS and aPL carriers.
