# Direct oral anticoagulants versus warfarin in patients with single antibody-positive anti-phospholipid syndrome

# Abstract

Background: The role of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) among patients with antiphospholipid syndrome (APLS) remains unclear. Warfarin has been shown to be superior to DOACs among high-risk APLS patients (particularly those with triple-positive APLS). However, it remains unknown whether DOACs may be appropriate for lower-risk patients such as those with single-positive APLS.

Methods: We conducted a retrospective study comparing the risk of recurrent thrombosis among single-positive APLS patients treated with DOACs (apixaban or rivaroxaban), and those treated with warfarin.

Results: One-hundred-forty-three single-positive APLS patients, newly started on anticoagulation following a first thrombotic event, were included. Median follow-up was 54 months (IQR 29-73 months). Ninety-one patients $(64\%)$ received warfarin and 52 patients $(36\%)$ received a DOAC. Six patients $(6.6\%)$ who received warfarin experienced a recurrent thrombotic event compared with 3 of 52 $(5.8\%)$ patients who received a DOAC $(p = .845)$ . There was no difference in event-free survival between groups (HR DOAC:Warfarin $= 0.952$ , $95\%$ CI 0.232 - 3.908). Major bleeding was similar in both groups.

Conclusions: These findings suggest that DOACs may be a safe and effective option for patients with single-positive APLS. Prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings.

# KEYWORDS

anticoagulation, antiphospholipid syndrome (APLS), direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC), warfarin

# 1 | INTRODUCTION

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APLS) is an acquired autoimmune disorder characterized by arterial/venous thrombosis and/or obstetrical morbidity, and persistent positivity of at least one antiphospholipid antibody (aPLA): lupus anticoagulant (LA), anti-b2-glycoprotein (AB2GP), or anti-cardiolipin (ACL) antibody. Lifelong warfarin is the standard of care for thrombosis treatment and prophylaxis in APLS. The use of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) has been explored as

an alternative given their advantages of fixed dose prescribing, less need for therapeutic drug level monitoring, simplified perioperative management, and fewer drug-drug and food-drug interactions.

A pivotal randomized controlled trial known as the TRAPS study compared the safety and efficacy of the DOAC rivaroxaban to warfarin among patients with high-risk APLS. This study was terminated early due to an excess of thromboembolic events in the rivaroxaban arm compared to the warfarin arm.[2] This trial did much to reinforce the role of warfarin as the preferred anticoagulant in APLS. Notably

<!-- page 2 -->
however, only triple-positive APLS patients (those positive for LA, AB2GP, and ACL antibodies) were included in this trial. The risk of recurrent thromboembolic events increases with the number of positive aPL antibodies. $^{3-6}$ Thus, the findings of the TRAPS study may only apply to the highest risk APLS patients, and the role of DOACs among lower risk APLS patients remains less clear. The results of other studies evaluating DOAC use in APLS patients have been mixed. $^{7-9}$ Some major professional societies including the American Society of Hematology $(\mathrm{ASH})^{10,11}$ and the European Society of Cardiology $(\mathrm{ESC})^{12}$ recommend warfarin over DOACs in all patients with APLS. However, other societies including the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR), $^{13}$ British Society of Hematology $(\mathrm{BSH})^{14}$ the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis $(\mathrm{ISTH})^{15}$ recommend warfarin as first-line but note that providers can discuss the option of DOACs for patients who are lower risk.

Single-antibody positivity is associated with lower risk APLS compared to double- and triple-positivity, and single positive disease may be more amenable to DOACs than higher risk forms of APLS. We sought to evaluate the safety and efficacy of DOACs in patients with single-positive APLS, and to compare their outcomes to those of single-positive patients treated with warfarin.

# 2 PATIENTS AND METHODS

# 2.1 | Patients, outcomes, and exposures

We searched the medical records of our urban multi-hospital system for all adult patients (age $>18$ years), with single-positive APLS, newly started and subsequently maintained on anticoagulation with either warfarin or an anti-Xa DOAC, for either venous thromboembolism (VTE) or an arterial thrombotic event, between 1/1/2008 and 7/1/2020, and followed for at least 12 months.

Single-positive APLS was defined by diagnosis of VTE or arterial thrombosis in the setting of positivity for only one of anticardiolipin (ACL) antibody (IgG or IgM), anti-beta2-glycoprotein (AB2GP) antibody (IgG or IgM), or lupus anticoagulant (LA). Positivity for ACL and AB2GP was defined as a titer $>40$ GPL or MPL. Positivity for LA was defined via prolonged dilute Russell viper venom time (dRVVT) and via aPTT mixing study with addition of the phospholipid reagent actin FS (failure to correct aPTT with addition of normal plasma, and subsequent correction or significant shortening with addition of actin FS). The above testing may have been sent prior to a patient starting anticoagulation, following initiation of anticoagulation, or following discontinuation of anticoagulation. Importantly, each patient was tested for LA at least once while off anticoagulation (either before starting anticoagulation, during an interruption of anticoagulation, or after anticoagulation had been discontinued). Therefore, no patient was deemed to be LA positive based on a result obtained while on anticoagulation. VTEs included both deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE). Patients diagnosed with only superficial vein thrombosis were not included. Arterial events included thrombotic stroke, thrombotic/occlusive (type I) myocardial infarction, and peripheral artery thrombosis. Patients with atrial fibrillation were excluded.

The primary outcome was first recurrent thrombotic event (either VTE or arterial thrombosis). The primary exposure of interest was type of anticoagulant used (warfarin vs. anti-Xa DOAC). Patients were censored at loss-to-follow-up, at first recurrent thrombotic event, or if/when they discontinued their initial anticoagulant. All patients were followed for at least 12 months or until first recurrent thrombotic event.

In addition to data regarding APLS testing and recurrent thrombosis, data was also gathered regarding patient characteristics including age, sex, presence of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), presence of chronic kidney disease (CKD), presence of cardiovascular disease (CVD), presence of peripheral vascular disease (PVD), antiplatelet use, and major bleeding events (defined as World Health Organization grade 3 or 4 bleeding events).

# 2.2 Statistical analysis

Continuous patient-related, disease-related, and treatment-related variables were summarized by the median and interquartile range (IQR), while categorical variables were summarized by N (%). Distributions of continuous and categorical variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test and chi-square test, respectively.

The primary outcome (first recurrent thrombotic event) was assessed using the Kaplan-Meier method (which was used to compare

All patients who did not have a recurrent thrombotic event were censored at last follow-up. All patients who changed anticoagulation were censored at time of discontinuation of their initial anticoagulant. Comparisons of time to event distributions between groups were made using the log-rank test.

# 3 | RESULTS

One-hundred-forty-three patients with single-positive APLS were followed for a median of 54 months (IQR 29-73 months). Ninety-one patients $(64\%)$ received warfarin and 52 patients $(36\%)$ received a DOAC. In the DOAC group, 31 patients $(60\%)$ were on rivaroxaban and 21 $(40\%)$ were on apixaban. Baseline characteristics, as seen in Table 1, were similar between those on warfarin and those on a DOAC. Of note, patients on warfarin had longer follow-up than those on a DOAC (56 months (IQR 32-85) vs. 48 months (IQR 29-62), $p = .0488$ ). Among patients receiving warfarin, $68\%$ of international normalized ratio assessments were in therapeutic range (between 2.0 and 3.0). Anti-Xa levels were not routinely monitored among patients receiving DOACs.

Six of 91 patients $(6.6\%)$ who received warfarin experienced a recurrent thrombotic event during follow-up, compared with 3 of 52 $(5.8\%)$ patients who received DOAC $(p = .845)$ , (Table 2). # 4 | DISCUSSION

In this large retrospective cohort of single-positive APLS patients, use of DOACs was associated with a similar risk of recurrent thrombotic events when compared with use of warfarin. This is the first study comparing DOACs to warfarin in single-positive APLS patients. These findings suggest that DOACs may be acceptable anticoagulants among patients with such low-risk laboratory profiles.

In our cohort, the rate of recurrence was $5.8\%$ in the DOAC group with a median follow-up of 4 years, and $6.6\%$ in the warfarin group with a median follow-up of 4.6 years. Recurrence rates reported in the APLS literature have generally been higher than what we found, likely because we excluded higher-risk patients with double- or triple-positivity. In a meta-analysis of 447 APLS patients treated with DOACs, the recurrence rate was $16\%$ , with triple-positivity being a significant risk factor for thrombosis.[16] Similarly, APLS patients are still at risk for recurrent thrombosis despite treatment with warfarin. In a large prospective study following 1,000 patients with APLS, the 5-year recurrent rate for thromboembolic events for those treated with warfarin was $22\%$ .[17] Twenty nine percent of these patients were triple-positive and these accounted for $56\%$ of all recurrent events. Our findings support the notion that single-positive APLS patients represent a lower-risk group.

In our cohort of single-positive patients, the risk of recurrent thrombosis and bleeding were similar in the DOAC and warfarin groups. There have been a small number of studies comparing DOACs and warfarin in APLS, however these have typically included patients across risk groups (single-, double-, and triple-positive patients), or have focused only on the higher risk groups (such as triple-positive patients). In the TRAPS trial, warfarin was superior to rivaroxaban in triple-positive APLS patients.2 A prospective cohort study of 178 APLS patients which expanded criteria to include single-, double-, and triple-positivity demonstrated that DOACs were still associated with a significant increase in recurrent thromboembolic events compared to those treated with warfarin $(\mathrm{HR} = 3.98$ , $95\%$ CI 1.54–10.28, $p = .004)$ . Notably, however, of the ten patients treated with DOACs that had recurrent events, four had double-positivity and four had triple-positivity.18 Only one prior study

In our cohort, the LA antibody profile was associated with a 4.5-fold risk of recurrence though this was not statistically significant. Notably, all three of the recurrent events in the DOAC group were LA-positive. This is consistent with prior studies that show LA positivity is more strongly associated with recurrent thrombosis than aCL or B2GP positivity.[6,22] Perhaps DOACs may be most suitable for single-positive APLS without LA positivity, although our study was not sufficiently powered to show that definitively.

Our study has several limitations. It is subject to all the potential biases implicit in a retrospective study. Although baseline characteristics were similar between groups, it is possible that some underlying factors that could not be identified on a retrospective basis motivated clinicians to choose DOACs or warfarin in particular cases. Although time-based analysis (via the Kaplan-Meier method) was able to compensate for differences in follow-up length between groups, regression modelling could not be done due to the low total number of recurrent thrombotic events. We were therefore unable to identify additional risk factors for recurrence. Clearly, prospective studies will be needed to further explore and validate these results.

Based on available data it seems that the highest risk APLS patients (particularly those with triple-positive disease) may best be managed with warfarin. However, as suggested by our findings, DOACs may be appropriate for some lower-risk groups including those with single-positive ACL or B2GP antibodies. There is clearly a

spectrum of APLS severity and it remains incompletely clear where the demarcation should lie between patients who should be restricted to warfarin, and those in whom DOACs may be adequate therapy. Additional studies are needed to clarify this.
