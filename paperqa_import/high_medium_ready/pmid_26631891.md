# Cerebral Venous Thromboembolism in Antiphospholipid Syndrome Successfully Treated with the Combined Use of an Anti-Xa Inhibitor and Corticosteroid

Masayuki Sugie $^{1,2}$ , Natsuko Iizuka $^{1}$ , Yuki Shimizu $^{1}$ and Hiroo Ichikawa $^{1}$

# Abstract

We herein report a case presenting with cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST) associated with primary antiphospholipid syndrome (APS). The patient developed recurrent CVST followed by a hemorrhagic ischemic stroke despite the use of warfarin during the appropriate therapeutic window. Thus, we substituted warfarin to rivaroxaban with prednisolone and obtained a good clinical course. In addition to the effect of prednisolone of inhibiting elevated lupus anticoagulants and the recurrence of arterial thrombosis, rivaroxaban may prevent CVST and inhibit hypercoagulability induced by corticosteroids. The combination of an anti-Xa inhibitor and corticosteroid may be an alternative treatment for CVST and arterial thrombus with warfarin-resistant APS.

Key words: cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, antiphospholipid syndrome, anti-Xa inhibitor, corticosteroid

(Intern Med 54:3051-3056,2015)

DOI: 10.2169/internalmedicine.54.5045)

# Introduction

The current mainstay of treatment for venous and/or arterial thromboembolism due to antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is anticoagulation with oral vitamin K antagonists (VKA) (1, 2). The American Heart Association/America Stroke Association (AHA/ASA) guideline (3) recommends warfarin with a target international normalized ratio (INR) of 2.0-3.0 in cases of APS with venous thromboembolism (VTE). It is also advised that patients with confirmed APS and arterial thrombosis should receive warfarin (INR $>3.0$ ) or a combination of aspirin $(100\mathrm{mg}$ daily) and warfarin (INR 2.0-3.0), however, there is a lack of consensus (4). Although corticosteroids are recommended with a low evidence level (Grade C1) for ischemic stroke with APS accompanied by SLE according to the Japanese guidelines for management of stroke (5), no other guidelines for APS refer to treatment with corticosteroids in APS with thromboembolism (1).

Recently, new-generation oral anticoagulants have been developed, including dabigatran etexilate (a direct thrombin

inhibitor) and rivaroxaban [the first oral direct factor Xa (FXa) inhibitor]. These anticoagulants are expected to prevent cardioembolism due to non-bulbar atrial fibrillation and are recommended as alternative therapeutic agents for VTE, however, there is no evidence and little experience for their use in APS (2).

We herein describe a patient with primary APS who presented with cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST), followed by arterial thromboembolism and hemorrhagic stroke, and was successfully treated with the combined use of an anti-Xa inhibitor and corticosteroid.

# Discussion

The present case diagnosed with primary APS presented with a rare CVST. Furthermore, despite the administration of warfarin, the patient developed arterial thrombosis followed by a hemorrhagic stroke. Hence, we changed warfarin to rivaroxaban and added prednisolone to counter the advancing disease progress of APS reflected by the increasing

level of LA. The patient was ultimately discharged with a good prognosis.

The Einstein-Extension Study, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, superiority evaluated study, suggested that rivaroxaban $20\mathrm{mg/day}$ for a mean duration of approximately 6 months after the completion of treatment for 12 months with VKA following acute VTE was associated with significantly fewer recurrent confirmed symptomatic VTE events than with placebo (6). Including the aforementioned study, a number of clinical trials using new-generation anticoagulants, such as dabigatran and apixaban, to prevent or treat VTE have been reported (2), and they are now licensed in the UK and Europe for treatment of adult VTE patients. Among patients with acute VTE, approximately $10\%$ have APS, and it is likely that patients with APS were included within the study population (2). Furthermore, the ROCKET AF trial revealed that the rates of intracranial hemorrhage and hemorrhagic stroke per year were significantly lower in the group randomized to rivaroxaban $15\mathrm{mg/day}$ compared with that in patients taking warfarin (7). According to these advantages, rivaroxaban may be an agent that has potential effects in the treatment of acute CVST, including cases caused by APS (8). Therefore, we selected rivaroxaban to avoid secondary intracranial hemorrhage.

On the other hand, a few reports have so far discussed the utility of new-generation oral anticoagulants to prevent VTE in patients with APS (9, 10). In these reports, after patients with APS were switched from warfarin to dabigatran or rivaroxaban, they developed symptomatic arterial or venous thrombosis. Hence, these agents should be used after careful

<!-- page 5 -->
consideration in patients with APS; however, these studies are based on a small number of cases. The prospective randomized controlled Rivaroxaban in Antiphospholipid Syndrome (RAPS) trial of warfarin versus rivaroxaban is currently ongoing in patients with thrombotic APS (11).

In contrast to cases with only positive anticardiolipin or anti- $\beta 2$ glycoprotein I, isolated LA was significantly associated with an increased risk of arterial thrombosis in cases of APS (12), especially in those without systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) (13). Furthermore, it was recognized that patients with APS and persistently elevated antiphospholipid antibodies, including LA, appear to be at an increased risk of thrombosis (14). In the present case without SLE, we speculated that the persistently increased level of LA revealed by repeated blood samples caused the arterial thrombosis with bleeding stroke, despite the use of warfarin. The administration of prednisolone may have decreased the level of LA and prevented the recurrence of ischemic stroke with low-dose aspirin. Therefore, we believe that the corticosteroid may have played an important role in suppressing the secondary ischemic stroke in cases of APS with arteriothrombotic stroke.

It is recognized that hypercoagulability induced by corticosteroid administration is one of the causes of venous thrombosis including CVST (15). A previous in vivo study showed that dexamethasone increases plasma concentrations of coagulation factors VIII and IX in healthy volunteers, resulting in a direct stimulatory effect on blood coagulation (16). Coagulation FXa is the primary site of amplification and a rate-limiting step in thrombin generation; thus, it may represent a better target than thrombin for coagulation system inhibition. Hence, anti-FXa inhibition is potentially a more effective strategy for anticoagulation than targeting downstream thrombin since smaller doses of anticoagulant are required to block the coagulation progression (2). Furthermore, we speculate that a direct FXa inhibitor may effectively inhibit increases in factors VIII and IX, which are located upstream of factor X in the blood coagulation cascade, in cases in which steroids are administered. Furthermore, to avoid a deterioration of CVST and the occurrence of other venous thrombosis due to steroid-induced hypercoagulability, we administered low-dose prednisolone (equivalent to $0.5\mathrm{mg / kg}$ ), which is lower than the recommended dosage of $1\mathrm{mg / kg}$ in catastrophic APS (17). Although there was no consensus regarding the effect and appropriate dose of corticosteroid for preventing arterial thrombosis due to APS without a catastrophic condition, we speculated that low-dose prednisolone with rivaroxaban may produce a good prognosis in this case.

In addition, the first brain MRI of the patient showed no obvious lesions in the cerebral venous sinus. It has been suggested that lumbar puncture (LP) leads to decreased CSF pressure and result in the downward displacement of intracranial vascular structures and injury of the dural sinus wall, venous vasodilation, stasis, and thrombosis (18). Therefore, in some studies (18-20), in addition to corticosteroid ther

apy, LP is recognized as a risk factor for CVST, especially in the cases of multiple sclerosis (20). We believe that the repeated LP may have played a pro-coagulant role in the development of CVST in the present case.

In conclusion, prednisolone may inhibit LA elevations and prevent the recurrence of APS-induced arterial ischemic stroke. Furthermore, an anti-FXa inhibitor may repress the coagulant factors increased by corticosteroids and help prevent the recurrence of VTE including CVST. Therefore, the concomitant use of anti-FXa inhibitors and corticosteroids may be an effective treatment in cases of APS with CVST, especially those accompanied by hemorrhagic and ischemic intracranial complications despite warfarin administration.
