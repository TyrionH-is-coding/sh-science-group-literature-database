Antiphospholipid syndrome is a rare systemic autoimmune disorder with complex pathophysiology and high heterogeneity in clinical presentation and treatment responses. The core idea of precision medicine is that the varying treatment responses among patients with the same clinical diagnosis are due to differences in their underlying pathogenetic mechanisms and genetic makeup. A better understanding of the pathophysiology and multiple clinical subtypes of antiphospholipid syndrome has led to better classification and subphenotyping of the syndrome. Advances in microarray analysis, cytometry, and omic technologies have helped to identify genes, epigenetic variations, and pathway-informed biomarkers and identified new factors in disease development. By stratifying patients with antiphospholipid syndrome based on clinical or laboratory phenotypes and cellular and molecular profiles in the blood and affected tissues, treatments can be more effectively tailored, improving efficacy and reducing toxicity. This Review explores the current evidence on clinical, genetic, and biomolecular stratification in antiphospholipid syndrome and how artificial intelligence algorithms from clinical and molecular profiles can guide precision medicine in antiphospholipid syndrome.

# Introduction

Antiphospholipid syndrome is a thromboinflammatory autoimmune disorder with a complex antiphospholipid antibody-mediated pathogenesis, and high heterogeneity in clinical presentation and disease course. Clinical presentation in antiphospholipid syndrome includes venous and arterial thrombosis, pregnancy complications, and a broad range of microvascular and non-thrombotic manifestations.[12] Although it was first described only four decades ago, advances in the understanding of its clinical spectrum and pathophysiology contributed to a new disease classification and better subphenotyping of the syndrome.[3-6]

The treatment of antiphospholipid syndrome remains challenging and treatment response varies among disease subgroups. Antiphospholipid syndrome treatment is currently based mainly on anticoagulation. However, recurrent thromboses despite adequate anticoagulant treatment can occur in antiphospholipid syndrome, whereas microvascular and non-thrombotic manifestations of antiphospholipid syndrome do not respond to anticoagulants, supporting the need for an individualised approach. Stratifying patients based on their clinical, cellular, and molecular profiles can help to identify tailored therapeutic interventions, improving efficacy and reducing toxicity. This Review explores the current evidence on the clinical, laboratory, and biomolecular phenotyping of antiphospholipid syndrome and discusses how this information can guide precision medicine and targeted therapeutic approaches.

# Demographic, laboratory, and clinical stratification

# Demographics

Demographic features can distinguish people with rheumatic diseases based on their risk of developing the disease or having a worse prognosis (figure 1). Antiphospholipid syndrome is more common in women than men. In the European registry of 1000 patients with antiphospholipid syndrome, the female to male ratio was

3-5 to 1 in primary antiphospholipid syndrome and 7 to 1 in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)-antiphospholipid syndrome. Some studies showed worse outcomes in male than female patients with primary antiphospholipid syndrome; however, large multi-centre studies with long follow-ups are required to investigate the role of sex in antiphospholipid syndrome prognosis. Antiphospholipid syndrome can affect all age groups, but mainly young adults. Age at disease onset can also affect the phenotypic expression of the disease. In the European antiphospholipid syndrome registry, chorea was more frequent in childhood-onset antiphospholipid syndrome whereas stroke and angina occurred more frequently in patients older than 50 years. Older age (>50 years old) can affect the clinical phenotype, considering a higher prevalence of comorbidities, especially cardiovascular risk factors. The 2023 American College of Rheumatology-European League Against Rheumatism (ACR-EULAR) antiphospholipid syndrome classification criteria involved a risk stratification process by evaluating the co-existence of cardiovascular risk factors at the time of thrombosis which might affect the confidence in antiphospholipid syndrome diagnosis. Evidence has shown a higher prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors and lower target control in patients with antiphospholipid syndrome versus age-matched and sex-matched patients with high cardiovascular risk disorders.

Although scarce and mainly reporting on antiphospholipid syndrome and SLE co-existence, data about ethno-geographical variation and susceptibility to antiphospholipid syndrome have been reported and support the contribution of genetic and environmental factors. An association between ethnicity and antiphospholipid antibody phenotype has also been reported but evidence is limited by the heterogeneity in antiphospholipid antibody measurement methodology. In a stratified analysis of combined data from multiple SLE cohorts, Black patients had the lowest prevalence of clinically meaningful antiphospholipid antibody titers. # Laboratory stratification

Assessment of antiphospholipid antibody phenotypes, including antiphospholipid antibody type (anti-cardiolipin [aCL antibodies], anti- $\beta_{2}$ glycoprotein I [anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI] antibodies, and lupus anticoagulant [LA]), isotype, titre, and persistency, are essential to improve the laboratory diagnosis of antiphospholipid syndrome and to stratify the risk for the first or recurrent thromboses and pregnancy complications (figure 1). The presence of antiphospholipid antibody can vary greatly from temporary, low-titre aCL or anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibody positivity defined as low-risk antiphospholipid antibody profile, to persistent, high-titre aCL or anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies, positive LA test, or triple-antiphospholipid antibody. The aCL and anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies are tested by solid phase assays, whereas LA is detected by functional assays of phospholipid-dependent coagulation. Anti-prothrombin antibodies cause LA positivity by competing with factor Xa for phospholipid binding sites, and anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI by a direct interaction with factor V. A major issue is that antiphospholipid antibody measurements vary wildly due to weaknesses in the standardisation of the assays. Another issue is the fluctuations in antiphospholipid antibody titres that can lead to uncertainty about the risk of recurrence in patients in whom antiphospholipid antibodies are no longer detectable.

LA and triple antiphospholipid antibody positivity are key biomarkers of antiphospholipid syndrome, representing the strongest predictors of thrombotic and obstetric antiphospholipid syndrome events. The presence of positive LA, double or triple antiphospholipid antibody positivity, or persistent high antiphospholipid antibody titres is defined as a high-risk antiphospholipid antibody profile. The antiphospholipid antibody isotypes also have diagnostic and prognostic implications. In the laboratory criteria of the 2023 ACR-EULAR antiphospholipid syndrome classification criteria, the aCL or anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI IgG isotype is weighted higher than the IgM isotype. Increasing evidence has shown that anti-domain I $\beta_{2}$ GPI (anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI-DI) antibodies define disease subgroups at high risk for thrombotic and late gestational obstetric complications. The concurrent diagnosis of SLE in patients with antiphospholipid syndrome defines distinct phenotypes characterised by an increased prevalence of thrombocytopenia, haemolytic anaemia, and low complement levels.

# Clinical stratification

Insights into the diverse clinical manifestations and the disease pathogenetic mechanisms of antiphospholipid syndrome contributed to its new classification. In the 2023 ACR-EULAR classification criteria, clinical manifestations were classified into six domains (figure 1).

The first two domains were defined by the size of involved vessels: macrovascular (domains 1 and 2) and microvascular (domain 3), but they also represent different pathophysiology. The fourth domain refers to obstetric complications that also represent a distinct clinical and pathophysiology phenotype. Domains 5 and 6 (cardiac and haematological manifestations) are part of a separate phenotype of non-thrombotic manifestations in antiphospholipid syndrome.

This phenotyping of domains also mirrors different responses to treatment. The current mainstay treatment of antiphospholipid syndrome is anticoagulation. The EULAR recommendations for antiphospholipid syndrome management suggested the use of long-term treatment with vitamin K antagonists for patients with venous or arterial thrombosis to prevent recurrent thrombosis. However, a large proportion of patients with thrombotic antiphospholipid syndrome present recurrent thromboses despite adequate anticoagulant treatment, especially those with a high-risk antiphospholipid antibody profile. In a 6-year follow-up study of patients with triple-positive antiphospholipid syndrome, recurrent thromboses occurred in $29\%$ of patients receiving anticoagulant treatment (median international normalised ratio at the time of event was $2 \cdot 3$ ). Worse outcomes or recurrent obstetric complications could also develop despite a combination treatment with low-molecular-weight heparin and low-dose aspirin. In addition,

# Genetic risk stratification

Variations of $HLA$ genes in the MHC region have been shown to affect the risk of several autoimmune disorders. Early studies have identified genetic predispositions for antiphospholipid syndrome in the MHC. The association between $HLA$ class II genes and antiphospholipid antibody production has been reported in different ethnic groups.[28] A recent multicentre and multiethnic genome-wide association study of 410 patients with

primary antiphospholipid syndrome identified genetic susceptibility loci linked to thrombotic risk $^{29}$ (figure 2). Two major loci with genome-wide significance were highlighted: one in the STAT1-STAT4 region (rs11889341) and another near the HLA-DRA gene (rs9269041). Additionally, the study discovered 34 suggestive loci connected to thrombotic events, including known thrombotic-related genes like $ESR2$ , associated with vascular function and thrombotic disorders and $HGF$ , shown to play a role in angiogenesis.

Beyond MHC genes, variations in several haemostatic system components also contribute to the risk of thrombosis. These include genes encoding for PAI-1, endothelial protein C receptor, prothrombin, TFPI, TNF, platelet glycoproteins (GPIa [integrin alpha-2] and GP-Ib), P-selectin, PSGL-1, and $\beta_{2}$ GPI. Polymorphisms in these genes have been associated with an increased risk of venous or arterial thrombosis (figure 2). The interplay of these genetic variations is complex and not fully understood. The association between gene variants and the type of thrombosis (arterial vs venous) also needs additional investigation.

To identify subgroups of patients prone to have more severe manifestations, such as catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome, Chaturvedi and colleagues[31] showed that $60\%$ of patients with catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome had rare germline variants in genes related to complement regulation, involving genes of the complement factor H-related family (homozygous CFHR1-CFHR3 deletion), THBD Pro501Leu, or the complement receptor 1 (CR1Ser1532Gly, CR1Val1675Leu, CFHR4 Arg287His, and CR1 splice acceptor variant; figure 2).

# Molecular signatures in thrombotic antiphospholipid syndrome identified by advanced technologies

# Transcriptomic profiles

A small number of studies performed transcriptomic analysis in the whole blood, monocytes, neutrophils, or tissue samples of patients with primary antiphospholipid syndrome and revealed specific genomic alterations that correlated with macrovascular or microvascular thrombosis pathogenesis. Pérez-Sanchez and colleagues demonstrated that deregulated genes in the monocytes of patients with primary antiphospholipid syndrome were linked to mitochondrial function, oxidative stress, and inflammation. Gene enrichment analyses indicated that these alterations were significantly associated with both early atherosclerosis and thrombosis, highlighting their potential role as predictive biomarkers for vascular disease in people with antiphospholipid syndrome.

Knight and colleagues[40] reported an activated proinflammatory gene expression signature using RNA sequencing in neutrophils from nine patients with primary antiphospholipid syndrome. Overexpressed

<!-- page 4 -->
genes linked to interferon signalling, cellular defence, intercellular adhesion, and metabolic processes such as glycolysis were identified. In addition, overexpression of PSGL-1 in neutrophils has been shown in patients with antiphospholipid syndrome, whereas PSGL-1 inhibition effectively reduced the size of thrombi in mouse models.[40] In a whole-blood transcriptome study including 62 patients with thrombotic primary antiphospholipid syndrome and 29 age-matched and sex-matched healthy controls, type I and II interferon-regulated genes better differentiated patients with thrombotic primary antiphospholipid syndrome from healthy controls and subgroups at high risk for a worse outcome.[41]

A platelet transcriptome analysis from patients with antiphospholipid syndrome indicated an upregulation of the mTORC2-Akt signalling pathway.42 Sin1 (also known as MAPKAP1) deficiency disrupted the integrity of the mTORC2 complex, highlighting the role of Sin1 as a potential therapeutic target.

In the first tissue-level transcriptome study, RNA sequencing on archival formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded kidney biopsy samples from patients with primary antiphospholipid syndrome revealed whole-transcriptome expression profiles distinct from healthy controls, but shared similarities with expression profiles from patients with SLE.43 The study identified significant upregulation of complement, type I and II interferons, and neutrophil extracellular trap (NET)-related genes in antiphospholipid syndrome. Using publicly available RNA sequencing data of neutrophils from nine patients with primary antiphospholipid syndrome and eight healthy controls, a weighted gene co-expression network analysis was conducted to assess co-expression modules and intermodule relationships, which revealed the role of complement and complement receptors in the activation of neutrophils.44

Taken together, these data support the complement cascade, mTOR, interferons, neutrophils, and NETs as major points of interest in antiphospholipid syndrome pathogenesis and potential treatment targets. Antiphospholipid antibodies activate endothelial cells and monocytes, and—via the complement cascade and platelet activation—induce neutrophil activation and NET formation.[3,4] Neutrophils from patients with antiphospholipid syndrome express an interferon gene signature, whereas evidence suggests that mTOR regulates type I interferon production in plasmacytoid dendritic cells.[40,45,46]

# Non-coding RNAs and splicing profiles

The role of regulatory non-coding RNAs, such as microRNAs (miRNAs), is pivotal in the regulation of prothrombotic factor expression. Early studies have shown deregulated hsa-miR-19b and hsa-miR-20a as novel regulators directly influencing procoagulant molecule expression in monocytes.[47] Subsequent studies

investigated a variety of miRNAs in monocytes and neutrophils and identified correlations between hsa-miR-125a-5p, hsa-miR-125b-5p, hsa-miR-124a-3p, hsa-miR-146a-5p, hsa-miR-155-5p, and hsa-miR-222-3p and oxidative stress, immune responses, cardiovascular manifestations, and early atheroma plaque formation in patients with antiphospholipid syndrome.48 Of note, antiphospholipid antibodies were involved in the deregulated expression of miRNAs related to cardiovascular disease and miRNA biogenesis proteins in leukocytes from patients with antiphospholipid syndrome. Specifically, in vitro studies where neutrophils, monocytes, and human umbilical vein endothelial cells were activated with antiphospholipid IgG antibodies demonstrated downregulation of endoribonuclease Dicer, the central protein involved in miRNA biogenesis, with a subsequent reduction in the expression of miRNAs.

Additionally, a distinct circulating miRNA profile has been identified as a potential biomarker of thrombotic risk in patients with antiphospholipid syndrome, including hsa-miR-34a-5p, hsa-miR-15a-5p, hsa-miR-145-5p, hsa-miR-133b-3p, hsa-miR-124-3p, hsa-miR-206, hsa-miR-20a-5p, hsa-miR-19b-3p, hsa-miR-210-3p, hsa-miR-296-5p, and hsa-miR-374a-5p. These profiles were stable over time and correlated with antiphospholipid antibody titers, whereas in vitro treatment with antiphospholipid antibody altered miRNA secretion in monocytes and endothelial cells, reinforcing their pathophysiological effect. A review of 45 relevant studies demonstrated an association between hsa-mir-124-3p, hsa-mir-125b-5p, hsa-mir-125a-5p, and hsa-mir-17-5p with antiphospholipid syndrome and arterial thrombosis, whereas hsa-mir-106a-5p, hsa-mir-146b-5p, hsa-mir-15a-5p, hsa-mir-222-3p, and hsa-mir-451a were associated with antiphospholipid syndrome and venous thrombosis.

Other studies have revealed impaired splicing regulation in leukocytes as a clinically significant post-transcriptional mechanism in antiphospholipid syndrome pathophysiology. Lopez-Pedrera and colleagues[51] examined the expression of 45 splicing machinery components in immune cells from 45 patients with antiphospholipid syndrome. Specific splicing machinery component alterations in patients with antiphospholipid syndrome compared with healthy controls were identified in monocytes, lymphocytes, and neutrophils. Notably, the downregulation of some splicing machinery components in neutrophils, such as SKIP-SNW1, SND1, and CUGBP, was associated with an increased risk of thrombosis in patients with antiphospholipid syndrome.

# Proteomic profiles

Proteomic analyses identified protein profiles associated with thrombosis in patients with antiphospholipid syndrome. A study using two-dimensional gel electrophoresis in monocytes from patients with thrombotic $\mathrm{APS}^{54}$ showed clinically significant changes in the expression of annexin A1, annexin A2, protein disulfide isomerase (PDI), ubiquitin-like protein NEDD8, transforming protein RhoA, and heat shock protein 60. Proteins associated with pregnancy morbidity were also significantly deregulated.

Hell and colleagues[55] used two-dimensional difference gel electrophoresis to explore platelet proteome alterations in LA-positive patients with antiphospholipid syndrome and identified 25 proteins related to platelet activation and coagulation. Notably, two PDIs were altered, potentially contributing to thrombus formation by cleaving disulfide bonds in $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ . Conversely, leukocyte elastase inhibitor was decreased, potentially enhancing NET formation, which is associated with thrombosis development in antiphospholipid syndrome. In antiphospholipid syndrome, PDI can contribute to $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ oxidation, which unmasks the crucial B-cell epitope in domain 1 of $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ , making it immunogenic and potentially increasing the thrombotic risk.[56] Molecular alterations involving oxidative stress in APS[57,58] might further contribute to thrombotic risk.

Luminex technology identified altered circulating protein profiles in patients with antiphospholipid syndrome-related thrombosis, including cytokines (IFN $\gamma$ , IL-1 $\beta$ , IL-2, IL-6, IL-7, IL-8, IL-12, and IL-17) and growth factors (vascular endothelial growth factor, fibroblast growth factor, and granulocyte colony-stimulating factor). A clot proteome analysis by mass spectrometry revealed various amounts of clot-bound proteins, such as complement components, lipoproteins, platelet-derived proteins, and NET components eg. myeloperoxidase and histones. Araujo and colleagues used mass spectrometry to identify upregulation of proteins known to play a role in thrombosis and inflammation pathways (eg, fibrinogen $\alpha$ chain, $\alpha$ -1-acid glycoprotein 1 [AGP-1], and Apo-CIII).

Urine proteomics, using isobaric tags for relative and absolute quantification labelling combined with liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry analysis technology, allowed for the identification of potential non-invasive markers to differentiate primary thrombotic antiphospholipid syndrome from obstetric antiphospholipid syndrome, including CXCL12 (also known as SDF-1) and platelet-derived growth factor subunit B. Urinary CXCL12 concentrations were higher in thrombotic antiphospholipid syndrome than in obstetric antiphospholipid syndrome and correlated with IgG anti $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies. These findings underscore the complex molecular fingerprint in antiphospholipid syndrome (figure 3) and suggest that distinct protein

markers in blood and urine could serve as diagnostic and therapeutic targets.

# Molecular signatures in obstetric antiphospholipid syndrome identified by advanced technologies

# Transcriptomic profiles

Microarray analysis studies in monocytes isolated from patients with antiphospholipid syndrome with either pregnancy morbidity or vascular thrombosis revealed that upregulated genes were involved in cellular stress response, MAPK signalling modulation, cell adhesion, extracellular matrix dynamics, and embryonic and skeletal development.63 Another study using microarrays in human umbilical vein endothelial cells revealed a complex gene expression response to primary antiphospholipid syndrome-derived anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies, including multiple pro-inflammatory, pro-thrombotic, and pro-adhesive genes.64

A single-cell RNA sequencing study[65] on the first-trimester decidua from patients with obstetric antiphospholipid syndrome revealed: increased chemokine expression and heightened responses to IFN $\gamma$ and TNF by vascular endothelial cells; enhanced phagocytic activity, increased complement activation, and greater reliance on glycolysis by macrophages; and increased IFN $\gamma$ production from activated decidual natural killer cells.

A study aiming to measure the prevalence of the type I interferon signature in peripheral blood mononuclear cells of patients with primary antiphospholipid syndrome using real-time quantitative PCR demonstrated an association between the elevated type I interferon gene signature (including six genes: DNAJA1, IFI27, IFI6, IFIT5, MX1, and TYK2) and pre-eclampsia.[66] Another study that evaluated the interplay of type I and type III (or IFN $\lambda$ ) interferon signatures in antiphospholipid syndrome found a significantly higher IFN score to IFN $\lambda$ 1 ratio in patients with primary antiphospholipid syndrome versus healthy controls, and an association between low IFN $\lambda$ 1 expression and antiphospholipid syndrome-related pregnancy morbidity.[67]

# Advanced bioinformatics enhance diagnostic accuracy and patient stratification

Advanced bioinformatics have been used in antiphospholipid syndrome for the analysis and interpretation of large-scale biological datasets towards clinical and molecular phenotyping (figure 5). de Laat-Kremers and colleagues[75] developed and trained neural networks that could diagnose patients with antiphospholipid syndrome from a control group with antiphospholipid syndrome-like conditions (both not receiving anticoagulation treatment), with an accuracy of $87\%$ and positive and negative predictive values over $90\%$ . Input variables for the neural network included four thrombin generation and three thrombin dynamics parameters, and the inhibition of peak height by thrombomodulin. Another study from the same group examined the performance of five artificial neural networks for differentiation between patients with coagulated antiphospholipid syndrome and control individuals, incorporating six thrombin generation and six thrombin dynamics parameters into one prediction model.[76] The two best performing neural networks were further validated in an independent cohort; one of them performed better and showed an overall accuracy of $91\cdot 6\%$ .

In a study of patients with SLE,[7] the whole-blood RNA-sequencing database was split into a training set and a validation set and, based on model accuracy, a set of 100 genes was selected. The 100-gene random Forests model differentiated patients with SLE-antiphospholipid syndrome from healthy individuals with $76\%$ accuracy, $80\%$ sensitivity, $71\%$ specificity, and an area under the

Several differentially expressed genes were associated with functions relevant to antiphospholipid syndrome pathogenesis: venous thrombotic events in SLE-antiphospholipid syndrome were primarily driven by an enhanced myeloid and neutrophilic response, whereas arterial thrombosis was associated with an aberrant DNA damage response and altered metabolic pathways.

Incorporation of artificial intelligence models to help in the diagnosis of catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome and obstetric antiphospholipid syndrome are also needed. Huang and colleagues[79] developed a predictive model for the early detection of catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome, incorporating hypertension, anaemia, elevated lactate dehydrogenase concentrations, and proteinuria, which showed high precision in identifying patients with catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome, with an AUC of 0.97. The strength of this predictive model lies in the use of variables that are commonly obtained in clinical practice. Banos and colleagues[80] evaluated demographic and clinical variables, treatments, and laboratory parameters before pregnancy and during the first trimester as potential predictors of adverse pregnancy outcomes in patients with antiphospholipid syndrome and SLE with or without antiphospholipid antibodies or antiphospholipid syndrome, using a stepwise regression approach. A model that included non-White ethnicity, smoking, pregestational hypertension, and corticosteroid treatment

at the beginning of pregnancy successfully identified pregnancy complications with an AUC of 0.78. These studies could serve as a proof-of-principle for applying machine learning models in the field of catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome and obstetric antiphospholipid syndrome to facilitate diagnosis.

# Future directions

Discoveries of molecular pathways involved in antiphospholipid syndrome pathogenesis hold promise for personalised treatment approaches and the development of novel therapies. The potential role of targeted treatments (eg, the anti-CD20 antibody rituximab) has been shown in case series with catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome and has been tested in a pilot trial with patients with microvascular features such as skin ulcers, antiphospholipid syndrome nephropathy, and haematological manifestations.[81] Subsequently, an effect of anti-B-lymphocyte stimulator (BLyS; also known as B-cell-activating factor) monoclonal antibody belimumab has been shown in a case series of recurrent diffuse alveolar haemorrhage and refractory skin ulcers.[82] Ongoing trials in patients with primary antiphospholipid syndrome with thrombotic, microvascular, or non-thrombotic manifestations are examining the efficacy of other B-cell or plasma cell inhibitors, including anti-BLyS agents, BLyS-APRIL inhibitors, BLyS-APRIL and anti-CD20 antibodies, Bruton's tyrosine kinase inhibitors, anti-CD38 agents, and chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapies (table). New generation B-cell targeting agents, including better designed anti-CD20 antibodies and antibodies targeting plasma cells (eg, anti-CD38), and innovative strategies, such as bispecific antibodies inhibiting B cells and plasmablasts (eg, binding CD19 and co-engaged FcγRIIb [IgG Fc receptor II-c]), or a T helper-B cell interaction targeting[83] might represent future approaches.

# Search strategy and selection criteria

The aim of this Review was to provide an overview of the current state of knowledge, identifying major themes and trends for exploring new or emerging topics of personalised medicine in antiphospholipid syndrome. Increasing data have also shown the interrelationship between these molecules,[43,44,96-98] supporting potential future treatment targets in antiphospholipid syndrome against individual components or networks with synergistic actions. Future directions also include approaches that restore immune homoeostasis and retain immune tolerance towards drug-free remission and cure, a challenging mission in most systemic autoimmune disorders.[99]

# Conclusion

Advances in clinical and molecular stratification with the evolution of cutting-edge technologies and the development of sophisticated data analysis algorithms over the past years facilitated a precision medicine approach in antiphospholipid syndrome. Fostering interdisciplinary collaborations among experts from diverse fields and interactions among scientific, pharmaceutical, and biotechnological stakeholders will be instrumental in harnessing the full potential of these advancements and translating them into tangible benefits for patients with antiphospholipid syndrome.
