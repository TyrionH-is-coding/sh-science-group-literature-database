# Antiphospholipid Syndrome Diagnosed as a Result of the Occurrence of an Ischemic Stroke After a Successful Catheter Ablation of Atrial Fibrillation and Continuous Direct Oral Anticoagulants

Yumiko Haraguchi, $^{1*}$ MD, Kohki Nakamura, $^{1*}$ MD, Mitsuho Inoue, $^{1}$ MD, Takehito Sasaki, $^{1}$ MD, Kentaro Minami, $^{1}$ MD, Shingo Yoshimura, $^{1}$ MD and Shigeto Naito, $^{1}$ MD

# Summary

We report a case of an ischemic stroke after a successful catheter ablation of atrial fibrillation (AF) and continuous oral anticoagulation therapy with direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), which was the trigger for diagnosing antiphospholipid syndrome (APS). A 68-year-old woman underwent catheter ablation of persistent AF and continued oral anticoagulation with edoxaban at a dose of $30\mathrm{mg}$ once daily after the ablation procedure. An asymptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage was detected by brain computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging one month post-ablation. Oral anticoagulation with dabigatran at $110\mathrm{mg}$ twice daily was continued thereafter due to a high stroke risk profile of a $\mathrm{CHA}_2\mathrm{D}_2$ -VASc score of 3. Eight months after the procedure, the patient had multiple acute cerebral infarctions despite no apparent recurrence of atrial tachyarrhythmias and continuation of the DOAC. A blood examination revealed the presence of anti-cardiolipin-beta2-glycoproteion complex antibodies and lupus anticoagulants, and the patient was diagnosed with primary APS. The DOAC was changed to warfarin. The patient has remained free from any ischemic or hemorrhagic cerebral events for 11 months after the oral anticoagulants were changed. The ischemic stroke in the present case appeared to be associated with APS rather than AF. A diagnosis of APS may be extremely crucial in AF patients who have new-onset ischemic strokes under continuous administration of DOACs, because vitamin K antagonists are more effective for the prevention of APS-related ischemic strokes than DOACs.

(Int Heart J 2022; 63: 153-158)

Key words: Cerebral infarction, Intracerebral hemorrhage, Magnetic resonance imaging, Vitamin-K antagonist

A trial fibrillation (AF) is a well-known risk factor of ischemic strokes. Percutaneous catheter ablation has been recently reported to reduce the risk of ischemic strokes in patients with AF.[1,2] On the other hand, AF patients with a high risk of ischemic strokes are recommended to continue oral anticoagulation following AF ablation procedures, based on the patients' stroke risk profile and not on the perceived success or failure of the procedure.[3,4] We present a case with the occurrence of an ischemic stroke after a successful catheter ablation of AF and continuous oral anticoagulation therapy with direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), which was the trigger for diagnosing antiphospholipid syndrome (APS).

# Case Report

A 68-year-old woman with shortness of breath on exertion was referred to our institute for persistent AF with a duration of approximately one month. The laboratory data were as follows: hemoglobin, $14.2\mathrm{g / dL}$ ; platelet

count, $15.6 \times 10^{4} / \mu \mathrm{L}$ ; D-dimer, $0.4~\mu \mathrm{g / mL}$ ; prothrombin time international normalized ratio (PT-INR), 0.98; and activated partial thromboplastin time, 33.6 seconds. The patient had a past medical history of hypertension but neither strokes nor systemic embolisms. Thus, the $\mathrm{CHA}_2\mathrm{D}_2$ VASc score was 3, based on an age of 65-74 years, female gender, and presence of hypertension. Her body weight and creatinine clearance were $54~\mathrm{kg}$ and $61.2~\mathrm{mL}/$ minute (estimated by the Cockcroft-Gault equation), respectively, and she received oral anticoagulation with edoxaban at a dose of $30~\mathrm{mg}$ once daily. After receiving oral anticoagulation for one month and verifying the absence of any intracardiac thrombi by transesophageal echocardiography, the patient underwent a radiofrequency catheter ablation of AF, including an extensive pulmonary vein isolation and linear ablation along the cavotricuspid isthmus, guided by the Rhythmia system (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA) (Figure 1). Multiple silent cerebral embolisms were detected on brain magnetic resonance (MR) imaging on the day after the ablation procedure (Figure 2), which was performed as routine practice. On repeat MR imaging one month after the procedure, the cerebral embolic lesions were undetectable but a new-onset intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) was detected, which was also verified on brain computed tomography (Figure 3). Therefore, the patient was diagnosed with primary APS. The oral anticoagulant was changed from dabigatran to warfarin, and the warfarin dosage was adjusted to achieve a therapeutic international normalized ratio of $2.0 - 3.0$ .[6,7]

She has remained free from any ischemic or hemorrhagic cerebral events and atrial tachyarrhythmia recurrences for 11 months after the oral anticoagulant was changed from dabigatran to warfarin.

# Discussion

This case report describes the occurrence of an ischemic stroke without any apparent recurrence of atrial tachyarrhythmias and under continuous oral anticoagulation with DOACs after an AF catheter ablation. The patient was diagnosed with primary APS after the occurrence of an ischemic stroke, and the ischemic stroke was likely to be associated with APS rather than AF.

Previous studies reported that $23.8 - 65\%$ of patients had asymptomatic AF after AF catheter ablation.[8,9] More post-ablation patients may have asymptomatic AF, which is undetectable by repeat 12-lead electrocardiograms (ECGs), Holter-ECG monitoring, and continuous event monitors. The patient may also have experienced asymptomatic AF recurrences that were missed during a routine follow-up method with repeat 12-lead ECGs and Holter-ECG monitoring to detect atrial tachyarrhythmia recurrences.

Patients with a high stroke risk profile, such as a $\mathrm{CHA}_2\mathrm{D}_2$ -VASc score of $\geq 2$ are recommended to continue oral anticoagulation, regardless of the apparent success or failure of the AF ablation procedure.34) However, AF is not always the cause of ischemic strokes among AF patients. The present study suggested that predisposing factors of ischemic strokes other than AF should be fully examined in AF patients with new-onset ischemic strokes during continuous administration of DOACs. # Conclusions

The present case was diagnosed with primary APS as a result of the occurrence of an ischemic stroke after no apparent recurrence of atrial tachyarrhythmias after AF catheter ablation and continuous oral anticoagulation with DOACs. The ischemic stroke appeared to be associated with APS rather than AF. The diagnosis of APS may be extremely crucial in AF patients who have new-onset ischemic strokes during the continuous administration of DOACs, because vitamin K antagonists are more effective for the prevention of APS-related ischemic strokes than DOACs. Further, APS patients undergoing AF catheter ablation require oral anticoagulation with warfarin and strict maintenance of a therapeutic PT-INR during the periprocedural period, and probably should continue warfarin with or without an AF recurrence.
