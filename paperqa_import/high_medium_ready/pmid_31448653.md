# Post-translational modifications of proteins in antiphospholipid antibody syndrome

Brigitta Buttari<sup>a</sup>, Elisabetta Profumo<sup>a</sup>, Antonella Capozzi<sup>b</sup>, Luciano Saso<sup>c</sup>, Maurizio Sorice<sup>b</sup> and Rachele Rigano<sup>a</sup>

$^{a}$ Department of Cardiovascular and Endocrine-Metabolic Diseases and Aging, Istituto Superiore di Sanità, Rome, Italy; $^{b}$ Department of Experimental Medicine, Sapienza University of Rome, Rome, Italy; $^{c}$ Department of Physiology and Pharmacology, "Vittorio Erspamer", Sapienza University of Rome, Rome, Italy

# ABSTRACT

Antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (APS) is a systemic autoimmune disease characterized by arterial and venous thrombosis and/or pregnancy morbidity. The incidence is around five new cases/100,000 persons per year and the prevalence is around 40-50 cases/100,000. The prevalence is higher (about $30\%$ ) among patients with systemic lupus erythematosus. APS is associated with circulating antiphospholipid antibodies (aPLs), a heterogeneous group of autoantibodies directed against negatively charged molecules and a combination of protein-complexed phospholipids. The predominant protein antigen in this disorder is beta 2-glycoprotein I ( $\beta$ 2GPI). Despite the discovery of "new" antigenic targets and development of "new" methodological approaches, the laboratory diagnosis of APS is still an evolving field and studies to identify further antigenic target(s) as potential diagnostic markers and risk predictors are in progress. In particular, recent studies were aimed at analyzing the pathogenic role of post-translational modifications (PTMs) of proteins induced by inflammation and/or oxidative stress as modulators of protein structure and function and possibly as a source of antigenic epitopes. The present review is focused on PTMs of self-proteins responsible for autoimmune reactions in patients with APS. At present, the known PTMs in APS involve $\beta$ 2GPI. In particular, the PTM of $\beta$ 2GPI via thiol-exchange reactions is a highly specific phenomenon that makes the protein more antigenic. Other PTMs, including sialylation and acetylation, may affect $\beta$ 2GPI antigenicity. Moreover, the addition or loss of carbohydrate chains affects $\beta$ 2GPI immunoreactivity since carbohydrates are determining factors for $\beta$ 2GPI conformation. In addition to $\beta$ 2GPI, PTMs of other self proteins such as vimentin and annexins may play a role in the immune response during APS. The study of PTMs is useful to clarify the role of modified proteins in the pathogenesis of APS as well as to design more efficient diagnostic/prognostic tools and more targeted therapeutic approaches.

# Antiphospholipid syndrome

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), a systemic autoimmune disease characterized by arterial and venous thrombosis and/or pregnancy morbidity, is associated with circulating antiphospholipid antibodies (aPLs). Such manifestations can present in primary APS or can be associated with an autoimmune systemic disease such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) (secondary APS) [1].

The incidence of APS is around five cases/100,000 persons per year and the prevalence is around 40-50 cases/100,000, with a disease onset between the ages of 20-40 years (female/male ratio $>3:1$ ). The prevalence is higher among patients with SLE (about $30\%$ ), deep vein thrombosis $(30\%)$ , stroke in young subjects $(25\%)$ , and recurrent fetal loss $(10\%)$ .

This syndrome was first described in the 1980s by Hughes [2,3], while the immunological diagnosis has

<!-- page 3 -->
witnessed as a significant evolution over the past 30 years. Indeed, in 1990, three laboratories identified $\beta 2$ -glycoprotein I ( $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ ) as the primary antigen detected by specific autoantibodies in this syndrome [4-6]. In 1999, clinical and laboratory criteria for the definition of APS, known as the Sapporo criteria, were developed by international consensus; these criteria were subsequently updated in 2006 [7,8]. Laboratory criteria include a positive laboratory test for aPL on two or more occasions at least 12 weeks apart. The aPLs include anticardiolipin (aCL) antibodies (IgG or IgM) exceeding 40 IgG or IgM IU/mL (based on WHO IRPs), anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ (a $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ ) antibodies (IgG or IgM) at titers exceeding the 99th percentile and/or lupus anticoagulant. However, aPLs are a heterogeneous group of autoantibodies directed against negatively charged molecules and a combination of phospholipid/protein complexes, including not only $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ [9,10] but also other proteins or phospholipid/protein complexes such as prothrombin/phosphatidylserine [11], vimentin/cardiolipin [12], protein S [13], protein C [14], annexin A5 [15], annexin A2 [16], and oxidized low-density lipoproteins (ox-LDL) [17]. In addition, a $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ antibodies comprise a family of antibodies that recognize different epitopes of the protein. Among these, antibodies to domain I, particularly those specific for the epitope Gly40-Arg43, show good correlation with thrombosis and pregnancy morbidity [18,19]. Interestingly, autoantibodies react to $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ only when the protein is in its open conformation and not when it is in its soluble circular form [20]. Anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ antibodies react with their target antigen, likely in association with annexin A2 and toll-like receptor (TLR)-4, in endothelial cells [21] or on the monocyte [22] plasma membrane. Anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ binding triggers interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase phosphorylation and nuclear factor-kappa B translocation, leading to a proinflammatory and procoagulant monocyte phenotype characterized by the release of cytokines and procoagulant factors (tissue factor).

In the last few years, new technical approaches that utilize different supports for the detection of aPLs, including chemiluminescence [23,24], multiline dot assay [25], and thin-layer chromatography immunostaining, have been developed [26]. They may be useful to better analyze the antigen specificities of aPLs, since in all these systems the epitopes of proteins and/or of phospholipid/protein complexes exposed to autoantibodies are different from standard ELISAs [27]. However, despite discovery of "new" antigenic targets and "new" methodological approaches for refining the laboratory diagnosis of APS, it is still possible to find patients who present with clinical manifestations characteristic of APS,

but who are persistently negative for all the aPL tests (the so-called "seronegative" APS patients) [28,29]. This clinical presentation suggests that APS is still an evolving field and studies are in progress to identify further antigenic targets [30]. Indeed, a combination of aPL tests may represent a risk stratification tool in these patients. In addition, autoantibody presence may help to subclassify APS according to the association of these antibodies with clinical manifestations and/or to identify patients who need prophylactic treatment during pregnancy.

In general, the formation of autoantibodies against self-proteins involves post-translational modifications (PTMs) of the self-proteins that are induced mainly by oxidative stress and/or inflammation. With this aim, recent studies were conducted to identify PTMs of self proteins that are responsible for autoimmune reactions in patients with APS. The present review focuses on the state of the art for the main PTMs involved in APS.

# Oxidative stress and inflammation in APS

Clinical and experimental studies have largely demonstrated the association of oxidative stress with APS. Under physiological conditions, pro- and antioxidant processes are well balanced. When there is an overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and/or a deficiency of the antioxidant machinery, a biochemical imbalance occurs and causes tissue damage [31,32]. Clinical studies have demonstrated that patients with primary APS have increased levels of the oxidative stress markers, isoprostanes [33-35], and that circulating concentrations of these compounds are associated with the presence of aPLs [36]. Isoprostanes are produced by a non-cyclooxygenase mechanism involving arachidonic acid oxidation by ROS and represent a quantitative measurement of oxidative stress in vivo [37]. The link between oxidative stress and APS is also sustained by the observation that patients with aPLs have decreased levels of paraoxonase activity, an enzyme involved in antioxidant mechanisms [38]. Furthermore monocytes and neutrophils from APS patients are characterized by an alteration of the oxidative balance because they produce increased levels of $\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}_2$ and have reduced activities of antioxidant enzymes [32]. Pro-inflammatory mechanisms, which are closely related to oxidative stress, are also associated with APS, which may be considered a chronic low-grade inflammatory disease. In a small observational study, it has been demonstrated that APS patients have significantly higher plasma levels of the proinflammatory markers, C reactive protein, serum amyloid A, and prostaglandin E2, when compared with

<!-- page 4 -->
healthy subjects [34,35]. Circulating levels of the proinflammatory cytokines, IL-6, IL-2, and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-alpha), are also up-regulated in these patients [39-41]. Furthermore, a clinical study demonstrated that increased plasma levels of the immune activation markers, neopterin and soluble CD14, were associated with primary APS, particularly in conjunction with single- or multiple-vessel occlusion [42]. As neopterin and CD14 are produced by monocytes/macrophages, and their expression is increased upon activation of these immune cells [43,44], their association with APS and thrombotic events supports the concept that innate and adaptive immunity are involved in the pathogenic mechanisms APS.

It has been suggested that the main factor contributing to oxidative stress in APS is aPL [45]. Autoantibodies from patients with APS are able to impair the functional activity of mitochondria in monocytes and neutrophils, thus promoting the generation of intracellular ROS and sustaining oxidative stress [46]. In particular, aβ2GPI antibodies stimulate neutrophils from both APS patients and healthy subjects to produce $\mathsf{H}_2\mathsf{O}_2$ , and aCL antibodies induce NO and $\mathsf{O}_2$ production, causing enhanced levels of plasma peroxynitrite [45,47]. Antiphospholipid autoantibodies also have a major role in promoting inflammation. Affinity-purified antibodies from patients with APS have been shown to activate monocytes and endothelial cells [48-50]. In particular, aPLs are able to up-regulate the expression of adhesion molecules and pro-coagulant factors such as tissue factor, thus promoting the adhesion of immune cells on endothelium and the activation of inflammatory responses. These autoantibodies interact with multiprotein complexes, including annexin A2 and TLR-4 expressed on the endothelial cell surface [35], and promote the activation of nuclear factor kB [51], a transcription factor that plays a major role in the activation of pro-inflammatory genes. In a previous study, we demonstrated that aβ2GPI antibodies from patients with APS interacted with their target antigen expressed in association with annexin A2 and TLR-4 on the monocyte plasma membrane, leading to a pro-inflammatory monocyte phenotype characterized by the release of TNF-alpha [22]. Following their up-take into endosomes, aPLs also promote NADPH oxidase activation, superoxide production, and the up-regulation of TLR-7 and TLR-8 expression in plasmacytoid dendritic cells and monocytes [52]. All these data strongly demonstrate the ability of aPLs to activate innate immune cells that are fundamental for the activation of immune responses and inflammatory reactions.

Of note, oxidative stress and inflammation promoted by aPL have in turn a pathogenic role in APS and its complications. In particular, in clinical and experimental studies, the involvement of oxidative stress has been demonstrated in miscarriage. By binding to trophoblasts and activating the complement cascade, aPLs attract leukocytes, particularly neutrophils. These cells produce ROS, which causes trophoblast injury and fetal death [53-55]. Inflammation is another process involved in APS complications as it helps to promote thrombotic events. Pro-inflammatory conditions induce the activation of endothelial cells and the interaction of endothelium with leukocytes. Leukocytes, particularly monocytes/macrophages, lymphocytes and neutrophils, produce pro-inflammatory cytokines and coagulation factors that promote the expression of adhesive and pro-coagulant molecules, thus sustaining the inflammatory process and increasing the risk of thrombus formation [56-60].

The pathogenic roles of oxidative stress and inflammation in APS are also related to their ability to effect protein structural modifications [61,62]. It is well documented that pro-oxidant conditions can cause conformational changes in protein structures by promoting PTMs [63]. ROS may interact with and modify any amino acid of the protein target. Most proteins contain some sequences and structures ("hot spots") that are particularly susceptible to oxidative damage [64]. Depending on the amino acids involved, oxidation induced PTMs give rise to different adducts. Interaction of hydroperoxides with cysteine and methionine causes the formation of alcohols, whereas oxidation of arginine, proline, or lysine gives rise to carbonyl derivatives that are utilized as common markers of oxidative stress and protein damage. Other oxidative stress-induced modifications are nitrosilation of tyrosine and tryptophan and the formation of methionine-sulfoxide. Interaction with ROS may also promote the formation of covalent cross-links among amino acid side chains, for example, disulfide bonds between cysteine residues [64,65]. Oxidative stress, together with inflammation, is also involved in nonenzymatic glycosylation (glycation of lysine residues). These products accumulate in inflamed tissues and, by interacting with the receptor for advanced glycation end products, RAGE, that is expressed on the surface of vascular and immune cells, participate in the progression of inflammatory and immune-mediated diseases [66]. The modifications of self structures may cause the appearance of neo or cryptic epitopes (neo epitope, epitope that appears following structural modification of the molecule; cryptic epitope, epitope that is present under physiological

conditions but is hidden to the immune system due to its position within the structure of the molecule and that becomes visible following structural modification) that can be recognized by immune cells, thus promoting the activation of autoimmune responses. Therefore, oxidative stress and inflammation contribute significantly to the pathophysiology of APS by promoting the appearance of neoantigens that sustain the activation of autoimmune reactions and the occurrence of complications in this disease.

# Post-translational modifications in autoimmunity

PTMs are defined as covalent modifications that take place in specific amino acids. They occur by enzymatic modifications or spontaneously, and they may alter protein structure and functions [67,68]. PTMs occur after the protein is synthesized and can be reversible. In general, cells use PTMs to control protein function by regulating protein stability, activity and localization as well as interaction with other proteins [69,70]. The absence, deficiency, or excess of PTMs of a specific protein may

be a potential trigger for autoimmune responses. One hypothesis suggests that PTMs that occur during inflammation are a possible source of autoimmunity, since PTMs emerge in the periphery and are not exposed to the developing naive T cells in the thymus. These modified proteins can be taken up by antigen presenting cells to be processed and presented to autoreactive T and B cells and eventually they induce an autoimmune response. PTMs have been found in autoimmune conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA), SLE, and APS, and several lines of evidence suggest that they have a role in the generation of autoantibodies [71].

# Carbamylation

Carbamylation is a nonenzymatic process in which homocitrulline residues are generated by the reaction of cyanate with the primary amine of lysine residues of proteins. Although carbamylation has been related to protein aging and other pathological conditions, recent studies that have identified carbamylated IgG proteins in patients with RA suggest that the presence of these antibodies may be useful to predict higher disease activity and may be related to inflammatory biomarkers [79-85].

# Sumoylation

Sumoylation is the addition, in a covalent and reversible manner, of small ubiquitin-like modifiers (SUMOs) to specific targets; these small proteins are similar to ubiquitin. The binding is between a C-terminal glycine residue of SUMO and an acceptor lysine residue present in the target protein [71]. Sumoylation has been reported to have crucial roles, such as control of nucleo-cytoplasmic signaling and transport, replication, and regulation of gene expression, in different autoimmune diseases, including RA, in regulatory pathways in nucleated cells [86].

# Phosphorylation

Phosphorylation, a common PTM, consists of the attachment of a phosphoryl group to amino acids. Several autoantibodies are directed to post-translational phosphorylated glycoproteins. In particular, a protein susceptible to phosphorylation in SLE is the small nuclear ribonucleoprotein U1-70k (also known as 68k), an autoantigen selectively phosphorylated on the serine and arginine domains during apoptosis associated with cellular stress. Other proteins phosphorylated in SLE and in Sjogren's syndrome are Ro/SSA and La/SSB; these are complex ribonucleoproteins found in the nucleus and cytoplasm [87,88].

# Methylation

Methylation, the addition of methyl groups to proteins, typically occurs on arginine or lysine residues. Another PTM associated with the pathogenesis of SLE is the

methylation of the Sec1/Munc18 (Sm) protein, which is part of the structure of small nuclear ribonucleoproteins U1, U2, U4, and U5. There are seven Sm proteins, B/B', D1, D2, D3, E, F, and G, that may be targets of an anti-Sm immune response; in SLE, autoantibodies to Sm have been shown to be highly specific [89-92].

# Acetylation

Acetylation of lysine by acetyltransferases is one of the most frequent PMTs in proteins. Lysine acetylation has also been showed to play a key role in immune system regulation. Indeed, after lysine acetylation, the majority of proteins, including $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ , are in an open conformation [93,94]. In addition, H2B and H4 histones undergo apoptosis-induced acetylation in SLE and autoantibodies to these proteins have been reported in SLE patients [95-97].

# Isoaspartylation

Isoaspartylation is the conversion of aspartic acid to iso-aspartic acid, which can occur spontaneously under physiological and pathological conditions. Recently, H2B has been identified as an autoantigen in SLE patients after isoaspartylation at position 25 [98].

# Sialylation

Sialylation introduces a sialyl group onto a molecule. A reduced level of sialylation has been described in patients with APS [99]. It occurs in the glycan structures of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ and can induce the exposure of an otherwise cryptic epitope, leading to an intermolecular interaction between amino acid residues Arg39-Arg43 (in the first domain of the protein) and autoantibodies [99].

# Glycosylation

Glycosylation adds a sugar to the side chains of amino acids such as asparagine, serine and threonine. Partial or complete removal of the carbohydrate chains (deglycosylation) is a PTM that could easily trigger access of aβ2GPI antibodies to the cryptic epitope, thereby increasing not only antibody reactivity [100] but also conferring to aβ2GPI/β2GPI complexes the ability to activate monocytic cells [101].

# Glycation

Glycation is a nonenzymatic process that leads to the formation of early, intermediate, and advanced glycation end products; such products are able to modify

self molecule structure and function. Elevated levels of antibodies to glucose modified $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ were reported in APS patients [102].

# Self antigens associated with APS and their PTMs

Many PTMs of self proteins are involved in APS as in vivo triggers of autoimmune reactions or as sources of antigens to improve the performance of immunodiagnostic tests. Table 1 reports the self antigens that have been associated with APS and their PTMs up to now.

# Beta 2-glycoprotein I

Beta 2-glycoprotein I, one of the most abundant proteins in human serum, is present at variable levels (50-500 $\mu$ g/mL or 1-10 $\mu$ mol/L) [6]. $\beta$ 2GPI is predominantly synthesized in hepatocytes, although mRNA coding for $\beta$ 2GPI has been found in endothelial cells, placental cells, neurons and astrocytes [10]. $\beta$ 2GPI levels increase with age and are reduced in pregnant women and in patients with stroke and myocardial infarction. Interestingly, $\beta$ 2GPI levels seem to be slightly higher in patients with APS.

$\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ is a single chain polypeptide (molecular weight approximately $50\mathrm{kDa}$ ) that contains four N-linked carbohydrate side chains and consists of 326 amino acids organized in five repeating sequences called complement control protein (CCP) domains or "sushi" domains [115,116]. In particular, domain I contains the major epitopes for $\alpha\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ autoantibodies in

APS patients [117-119]. In domain V, a short beta-hairpin that is rich in lysine confers a highly positive charge to this domain. This region forms the binding site for a large number of negatively charged compounds, including cardiolipin, DNA, heparin, oxLDL, apoptotic cells, activated platelets, platelet factor 4, apolipoprotein E receptor 2, glycoprotein Ibα, annexin A2, TLR-2, and TLR-4 [16,120-126].

The biological functions displayed by $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ include involvement in triglyceride metabolism, blood coagulation, and homeostasis [16,61,63,127-129]. The mechanisms by which $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ exerts its functions are not yet clearly identified. It has been proposed that transitions between conformational states of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ directly correlate with its functional properties. Evidence exists that the structure of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ in solution may be different from the crystal structure, the former being less elongated and more bulky [130]. Differences in the overall shape between crystal and solution structures are attributed to the carbohydrate units and to the flexibility of the CCP domains [124]. Cleavage of the protein at Lys317-Thr318 by plasmin or factor Xla may be one of the mechanisms by which $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ regulates fibrinolysis or possibly coagulation. $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ proteolytically cleaved by plasmin (nicked $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ ) is unable to bind to phospholipids [5]. The finding that plasmin-nicked $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ promotes angiogenesis by interacting with plasmin-generated angiostatin [4-6] suggests that the nicked form of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ plays a role in the regulation of angiogenesis at the site of thrombosis.

Several studies have suggested the presence of a pathogenic structure of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ in patients with APS. Giannakopoulos et al. The presence of covalent modification of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ with oxidation products of cardiolipin (oxCL) has been demonstrated and this modification makes the protein more antigenic [135]. Autoantibodies against oxLDL/ $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ complexes in patients with APS correlate with arterial thrombosis and thus represent a risk factor and/or a useful biomarker for atherothrombotic complications. Moreover, the oxidized forms of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ were covalently coupled by imine bonds to form dimers and oligomers [136]. A study by Sheng et al. [137] showed that a $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ autoantibodies from patients with APS bound to $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ with low affinity, whereas dimerization of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ induced a significant increase in antibody affinity. In line with this finding, Arvieux et al. [103] demonstrated that in vitro oxidative modification of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ by the hydroxyl radical altered phospholipid binding and modulated autoantibody recognition in APS patients. Recently, Raimondo et al. [104] demonstrated a specific association between IgG anti-domain I antibodies and increased oxidation of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ in patients with APS. Our group, in a previous study, showed that only the oxidized form of the protein was able to

activate immature monocyte-derived dendritic cells and in parallel to release Th1-promoting cytokines [61].

Overall, these data support PTMs of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ via thiol-exchange reactions as a highly specific phenomenon in the setting of APS thrombosis [63]. These findings may be of clinical relevance not only for the generation and/or reactivity of a $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ antibodies but also for the functional properties of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ . Further protein structural studies are needed to clarify the effects of antibodies on the redox state of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ .

It is known that the addition or loss of carbohydrate chains affects the immunoreactivity of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ because carbohydrates are determining factors of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ conformation. A partial or complete removal of the carbohydrate chains (deglycosylation) is a PTM that could easily trigger access of a2GPI antibodies to the cryptic epitope, thereby leading to an autoimmune reaction and APS symptoms. Deglycosylation not only increases the antibody reactivity [100] but also confers to a2GPI/ $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ complexes the ability to activate and differentiate U937 into proinflammatory monocytes [101]. In patients with APS, Kondo et al. [99] showed an altered distribution of glycans in $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ , with a reduced number of negatively charged sialic acid units in the glycan structure at Asn143 (desialylation), an event that may lead to conformational instability and exposure of the disease-related epitope to the circulating autoantibody. The conformational changes acquired by $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ after complete deglycosylation lead to a specific auto-association of the $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ molecules in which the dimeric quaternary structure formation increases the density of cryptic epitopes recognized by aCLs [99].

The addition of carbohydrate chains to proteins is another mechanism responsible for modifying self molecule structures and functions, thus generating neoepitopes or exposing cryptic structures. Even though glycation is a physiological process and is modulated by numerous factors, systemic autoimmune diseases associated with inflammation and oxidative stress, such as APS, may favor the formation and accumulation of glycated proteins. In previous studies, we demonstrated the presence of several potential glycation sites within the primary structure of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ [102,105]. Our studies demonstrated that glycated- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ is a target antigen of humoral immune response in patients with APS and suggested a possible role for antiglycated- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ in improving the diagnosis of APS. In addition, our in vitro experiments demonstrated that conformational changes induced by glycation of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ conferred to this protein the ability to act as an inflammatory stimulus that was able to activate immunogenic dendritic cells and lead to T-cell responses.

Lysine residue acetylation, one of the most common PTMs in proteins, has also shown an impact on immune system regulation. Recently, Buchholz et al. [94] showed in vitro that specific and complete chemical acetylation of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ shifted the equilibrium of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ from the closed to open conformation. Further studies are needed to assess whether this modification may affect the $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ conformation in vivo and whether targeting acetylation may be a new strategy for treating APS.

Biotinylation of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ is another event which enhances dimer formation and detection by aCL antibody EY2C9 [138].

Misfolded cellular proteins are generally eliminated by the process of endoplasmic reticulum-associated degradation [139] and are not exposed to the immune system. Recently, Tanimura et al. [46] found that misfolded proteins were rescued from degradation and transported to the cell surface without processing to peptides when they associated with the peptide-binding groove of HLA class II molecules in the endoplasmic reticulum. Interestingly, they also found that $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ complexed with HLA class II molecules was a target for autoantibodies in APS and that more than $80\%$ of patients possessed autoantibodies against these complexes in the absence of phospholipids.

Thus, there are many aβ2GPI antibodies that recognize different epitopes of the protein. The role of oxidative PTMs of β2GPI has been described widely [67], and antibodies to oxidized β2GPI have been reported in APS patients, since pathogenic aβ2GPI antibodies recognize domain I of β2GPI only after a conformational change [100]. Recently, antiglucose modified β2GPI was correlated significantly with several clinical manifestations of APS, including venous thrombosis and seizure [101].

# Vimentin

The presence of autoantibodies directed against the vimentin/cardiolipin complex has been demonstrated in a high percentage of APS patients and these antibodies have been associated with both arterial and venous thrombosis [108]. Various studies have described these autoantibodies as a new marker of APS and a useful tool for diagnosis of seronegative APS patients. These results demonstrated that vimentin is a new cofactor protein for cardiolipin [12,26].

Vimentin is a $57\mathrm{kDa}$ protein expressed in cells and tissues of many different organisms; it is an intermediate filament protein of the cytoskeleton that is localized mainly in the cytoplasm, but that is also found in the nucleus and on the cell surface [140,141]. Thus, it is a

ubiquitously expressed protein that regulates mechanical and spatial properties of cells, cellular interactions and the functioning of the immune system [142].

Vimentin has a tertiary structure consisting of a central rod of alpha-spiral linked to N- and C-terminal domains [143,144]. This is a dynamic protein with an important role in cellular and tissue functions, but its role in the pathogenesis of inflammation and several autoimmune diseases also appears to be important [106,145-147]. Studies have shown that PTMs of the protein are responsible for the induction of specific antibodies in various diseases such as RA, sarcoidosis, SLE, APS, Crohn's disease, ankylosing spondylarthritis, and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis [143]. The main PTMs found for vimentin are phosphorylation, sumoylation, citrullination, carbamylation, and acetylation [106].

In particular, intermediate filament assembly changes in the conformation and solubility of the protein are associated with modifications by phosphorylation and sumoylation [107,148], whereas O-linked glycosylated vimentin is important to nutrient sensing and stress responses [149]. Moreover, citrullination, carbamylation, and acetylation are involved in the negative regulation of filament stability and are known to be responsible for inducing an immune response specific for modified vimentin [109,111,112].

It has been shown that citrullination can induce modifications in the vimentin conformation as well as in its binding with phospholipids, and it can facilitate the exposure of neoepitopes to the immune system. Therefore, conformational changes of the protein have a role in the induction of anticitrullinated vimentin antibodies in APS. In fact, antibodies to mutated citrullinated vimentin have been described in $26.6\%$ of patients with APS, and they are strongly association with arthritis [108]. These findings indicate a role for citrullination in the pathogenesis of APS and in the development of joint involvement.

Indeed, citrullination and/or other PTMs of intracellular proteins, including vimentin, provide an explanation for their expression on the plasma membrane in association with anionic phospholipids, and antibodies specific for mutated citrullinated vimentin may become an important diagnostic and prognostic marker for APS patients.

# Annexins

Annexins are a group of 12 highly conserved proteins characterized by their ability to bind phospholipids, predominantly in a calcium-dependent manner. These proteins are involved in various regulatory functions of

<!-- page 11 -->
cellular activities such as vesicle trafficking, calcium signaling, cell division, cell growth regulation, and apoptosis. Moreover, they appear to have a role in human diseases such as cancer, cardiovascular disorders, diabetes, and inflammatory and autoimmune diseases including autoimmune rheumatic diseases [110]. Antiannexins antibodies have been found in patients with APS [113]. Since PTMs are usually necessary to make a protein immunogenic, high circulating annexin A1 levels cannot explain per se the development of autoantibodies. A previous study hypothesized that annexin A1 undergoes PTM as a part of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) that are produced in response to viral, bacterial, and/or inflammatory triggers [150]. In particular, the focus is on the process of citrullination of annexin A1, which takes place within NETs and that mimics, to some extent, other autoimmune conditions such as RA that are characterized by the presence of anticitrullinated peptides in circulation [150].

# Conclusions

Overall, the findings described above strengthen the concept that, in a stress-inducing environment, posttranslationally modified proteins may serve as antigens for antibodies that can break tolerance and induce the autoimmune spiral. Although many parts of this puzzle are now in place, several pieces are still missing. The study of post-translationally modified proteins is an evolving and interesting field and further research is needed to clarify the participation of modified proteins in the pathogenesis of APS and/or other autoimmune disorders. Further analyses on the accumulation of modified/misfolded proteins due to defective protein catabolism or protein transport, and a better understanding of NETs as a source of modified antigens, will help to better understand the pathogenesis of APS and to design more efficient diagnostic/prognostic tools and more targeted therapeutic approaches.
