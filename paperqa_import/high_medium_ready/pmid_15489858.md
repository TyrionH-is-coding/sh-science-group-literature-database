# Heparin prevents antiphospholipid antibody-induced fetal loss by inhibiting complement activation

Guillermina Girardi, Patricia Redecha & Jane E Salmon

The antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is defined by thrombosis and recurrent pregnancy loss in the presence of antiphospholipid (aPL) antibodies and is generally treated with anticoagulation therapy. Because complement activation is essential and causative in aPL antibody-induced fetal injury, we hypothesized that heparin protects pregnant APS patients from complications through inhibition of complement. Treatment with heparin (unfractionated or low molecular weight) prevented complement activation in vivo and in vitro and protected mice from pregnancy complications induced by aPL antibodies. Neither fondaparinux nor hirudin, other anticoagulants, inhibited the generation of complement split products or prevented pregnancy loss, demonstrating that anticoagulation therapy is insufficient protection against APS-associated miscarriage. Our data indicate that heparins prevent obstetrical complications in women with APS because they block activation of complement induced by aPL antibodies targeted to decidual tissues, rather than by their anticoagulant effects.

The APS, characterized by thrombosis and pregnancy loss that occur in the presence of aPL antibodies, is a leading cause of miscarriage and maternal and fetal morbidity $^{1-3}$ . In vivo and in vitro studies have demonstrated that aPL antibodies trigger activation of endothelial cells, monocytes, neutrophils and platelets, and produce inflammation, thrombosis and tissue damage $^{4-8}$ . But the pathogenic intermediates of injury are not completely understood. Using a mouse model of APS induced by passive transfer of human aPL antibodies, we previously showed that complement activation is essential and causative in pregnancy loss and fetal growth restriction $^{9,10}$ . We proposed that aPL antibodies, preferentially targeted at decidua and placenta, activate complement through the classical pathway, leading to the generation of potent anaphylatoxins and mediators of effector-cell activation. The recruitment of inflammatory cells accelerates local alternative pathway activation and creates a proinflammatory amplification loop that enhances complement component 3 (C3) activation and deposition, generates additional C3a and C5a, and results in further influx of inflammatory cells into the placenta $^{10}$ . Depending on the extent of damage, either death in utero or fetal growth restriction ensues.

Therapy for pregnant women with APS focuses on preventing thrombosis $^{3,11}$ , but anticoagulation is only partially successful in averting miscarriage, and histopathologic findings in placentas from women with APS argue that proinflammatory factors may contribute to tissue injury $^{12,13}$ . Notably, heparin, the standard anticoagulant treatment used to prevent obstetric complications in patients with $\mathrm{APS}^{3,11}$ , was shown to have "anti-complementary effects" as early as $1929^{14}$ . Subsequent studies identified several possible mechanisms by which heparin can inhibit complement activation at various points in the classical, alternative and terminal pathways $^{15-22}$ .

Given the importance of complement split products as mediators of aPL antibody-induced fetal injury, we considered the possibility that heparin can prevent miscarriage as a result of its capacity to block the activation of complement, rather than its anticoagulant activity. We tested the hypothesis that heparin treatment protects mice from fetal loss caused by aPL antibodies by preventing complement activation on trophoblasts and that anticoagulation, in and of itself, is not sufficient to prevent pregnancy complications in APS. Our results suggest that the mechanism by which heparin exerts its beneficial effects is more complicated than simple inhibition of thrombin generation. These results underscore the potential value of developing and testing targeted complement-inhibitory therapy for people with APS.

# RESULTS

# Heparins protect from pregnancy loss in APS

We examined the effects of heparin on pregnancy loss induced by aPL antibodies in a mouse model of APS in which pregnant mice were injected with human IgG containing aPL antibodies (aPL-IgG) or IgG from healthy individuals $(\mathrm{NH - IgG})^{4,9,10}$ . We compared the consequences of treating pregnant mice with aPL-IgG in the presence or absence of unfractionated heparin (UFH) (10 U or 20 U subcutaneous injection twice daily) or low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) (enoxaparin). Passive transfer of aPL-IgG caused a $43.3 \pm 3.5\%$ frequency of fetal resorption $(\mathrm{P} < 0.001$ versus NH-IgG). To determine whether anticoagulation, in and of itself, can prevent aPL antibody-associated fetal injury, we treated mice with the anticoagulants fondaparinux sodium or hirudin. Fondaparinux, a synthetic pentasaccharide composed of the minimal antithrombin-binding subunit of heparin, is a specific inhibitor of factor Xa, and lacks N- and O- sulfation, which is required for the anticomplement activity associated with heparin20. A direct inhibitor of thrombin, hirudin acts independently of antithrombin and other plasma proteins and has no known effects on complement23. Although fondaparinux $(10\mu \mathrm{g})$ and hirudin $(4\mu \mathrm{g}$ and $8\mu \mathrm{g})$ showed the expected anticoagulant activities (factor Xa activity 87 $\pm 3\%$ inhibited and partial thromboplastin times $59\pm 3s$ and $57\pm$ 5, respectively, $P < 0.05$ versus control), neither treatment protected mice from aPL antibody-induced pregnancy loss (Fig. 1b). # Heparins inhibit complement activation in vivo

Given our finding that complement activation, involving both the alternative and classical pathways, is a necessary intermediate step for fetal injury and pregnancy loss caused by aPL antibodies $^{10}$ , we considered the possibility that heparin protected pregnancies by inhibiting activation of complement. To assess the effect of heparin on complement activation in vivo, we focused on C3, a pivotal member of the complement system. Three complement activation pathways converge at the C3 protein, a nexus for effector functions, whose cleavage generates two fragments: C3a and C3b. C3a is an anaphylatoxin that recruits and activates leukocytes. If not bound to its receptor, C3a is rapidly digested by plasma carboxypeptidases to C3a desArg $^{24}$ . C3b and its cleavage fragments, iC3b and C3d, are ligands for complement receptors 1 and 2 and for the $\beta 2$ integrins, CD11b/CD18 and CD11c/CD18, that are present on inflammatory and immune accessory cells. C3b attaches covalently to targets, followed by assembly of C5 convertase and the subsequent cleavage of C5 to C5a, a potent anaphylatoxin and cell activator, and C5b, which leads to formation of the C5b-9 membrane attack complex.

We studied the products of aPL-IgG-initiated C3 cleavage: C3a in plasma (measured as C3a desArg) and C3 activation fragments bound to trophoblasts. Mice treated with aPL-IgG showed a marked increase in plasma levels of C3a desArg 60 min after intraperitoneal injection, compared to mice treated with NH-IgG (Table 1); the increase in plasma C3 desArg persisted for $4\mathrm{h}$ and returned to baseline by $10\mathrm{h}$ . This increase in C3a desArg was completely abolished in the presence of UFH (10 U or 20 U) or LMWH (Table 1). Neither fondaparinux nor hirudin affected C3 activation as detected by plasma C3a desArg.

As the second approach to confirm that heparin inhibits complement, we focused on C3 cleavage products bound to trophoblasts in aPL-IgG-treated mice. We performed immunohistochemical analyses of decidua from day 8 of pregnancy in mice pretreated with anticoagulants and those that were not. Deciduas from aPL-IgG-treated mice showed extensive C3 deposition and embryo destruction (Fig. 2a), whereas mice that received UFH and LMWH in addition to aPL-IgG showed minimal staining for C3 activation fragments in the deciduas and the embryos had normal morphology (Fig. 2b-d). As expected, there was no decrease in decidual C3 deposition or tissue damage in mice treated with aPL-IgG and fondaparinux or hirudin (Fig. 2e,f).

# DISCUSSION

Heparin is used routinely to treat and prevent thrombotic disorders, and the dose is adjusted to induce anticoagulation as determined by laboratory parameters. Notably, heparin administered in subanticoagulant doses (UFH 5,000 U subcutaneous injection twice daily) has been found to improve pregnancy outcomes in some trials of APS patients[11]. Here we show that heparin has strong protective effects in an experimental model of APS in which complement activation is a proximal effector of fetal injury, that prevention of pregnancy loss is not related to anticoagulation and that heparin blocks activation of complement initiated by aPL-IgG in vivo and in vitro. Although we cannot exclude the possibility that blockade of P- and L-selectin by UFH contributes to heparin's therapeutic effects, the finding that LMWH (unlike UFH, a poor inhibitor of P- and L-selectin binding) $^{30,31}$ had similar benefits on pregnancy outcome argues that heparins do not prevent fetal loss in APS by this mechanism.

Heparin has long been known to possess anticomplement activity. Early studies identified several mechanisms for this effect, including inhibition of C1q binding to immune complexes, interference with interactions of C4 with C1s and C2, blockade of the formation of the C3 amplification convertase by the alternative pathway, and inhibition of the formation of the membrane attack complex $^{14-18,20-22}$ . In our experimental model of APS, aPL-IgG-induced fetal damage requires classical, alternative and terminal pathway components $^{10}$ . The present work emphasizes the capacity of UFH and LMWH to inhibit classical pathway activation, but blockade at multiple points in the cascade may enhance the efficacy of this intervention. Indeed, subanticoagulant doses of heparin have therapeutic benefits in other conditions in which complement activation is thought to have a role, including reperfusion injury and coronary artery disease $^{33,34}$ . It would be important to know if these effects also require blockade of complement activation, a finding that would imply that fondaparinux is a poor therapeutic choice in these disorders.

Our data suggest that heparins prevent obstetrical complications in some women with APS because they block activation of complement induced by aPL antibodies targeted to decidual tissues, not because they prevent placental thrombosis. Our findings provide a framework for understanding how subanticoagulant doses of heparin exert beneficial effects in antibody-mediated tissue injury and emphasize the importance of developing and testing targeted complement-inhibitory therapy for people with APS.

# METHODS

Preparation of aPL and other antibodies. We obtained APL-IgG from patients with APS (characterized by a high titer of aPL antibodies (>140 GPL units), thromboses, and/or pregnancy losses) and NH-IgG from healthy individuals. The generation and specificity of the human monoclonal antibody aPL IgG1 (monoclonal antibody 519) was previously described[35]. We purified IgG by affinity chromatography using Protein G Sepharose chromatography columns (Amersham Pharmacia Biotech) and treated it to deplete endotoxin with Centriprep ultracentrifugation devices (Millipore Corporation) and determined it to be free of endotoxin using the Limulus amebocyte lysate assay. For experiments to measure binding to deciduas by western blotting, we biotinylated aPL-IgG preparations as previously described[36].

Mouse pregnancy model. On days 8 and 12 of pregnancy, 6-8-week-old BALB/c females (Taconic Farms) were treated with intraperitoneal injections of aPL-IgG $(10\mathrm{mg})$ , human aPL mAb $(1\mathrm{mg})$ or NH-IgG $(10\mathrm{mg})^{4,9,10}$ . All protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and performed in compliance with institutional guidelines. To study the effects of heparins in aPL antibody-induced pregnancy loss, mice were treated from day 7 to day 13 with either UFH $(10\mathrm{U}$ and $20\mathrm{U}$ subcutaneous injection twice per day) or LMWH (enoxaparin $15\mu \mathrm{g}$ subcutaneous injection daily) (Aventis Pharmaceuticals Inc.). To study the effects of other anticoagulants, mice were treated from day 7 to day 13 of pregnancy with hirudin $(4\mu \mathrm{g}$ or $8\mu \mathrm{g}$ subcutaneous injection twice daily) (Sigma Chemical Co.) or fondaparinux sodium $(10\mu \mathrm{g}$ subcutaneous injection daily) (Arixtra, Organon Sanofi-Synthelabo LLC). Doses of anticoagulants were calculated based on standard therapy of APS patients, manufacturers' suggested doses for mouse studies and our observation that higher doses (UFH $50\mathrm{U}$ , hirudin $15\mu \mathrm{g}$ , fondaparinux $20\mu \mathrm{g}$ ) caused vaginal bleeding[37,38]. Anticoagulation was confirmed and monitored by PTT on day 8 in heparin- or hirudin-treated mice and by chromogenic anti-factor Xa activity (Pharmacia Hepar) in LMWH and fondaparinux-treated mice. We obtained plasma $60\mathrm{min}$ after aPL-IgG injection on day 8 for measurement of C3a desArg levels by ELISA (Cedarlane Laboratories Ltd.). Mice were killed on day 15 of pregnancy, uteri dissected, fetuses weighed and fetal resorption rates calculated (number of resorptions/total number of formed fetuses and resorptions). Resorption sites are easily identified and result from loss of a previously viable fetus. For immunohistochemistry studies, we removed deciduas on day 8, $60\mathrm{min}$ after treatment, froze them in O.C.T. compound and cut them into $10 - \mu \mathrm{m}$ sections. We stained the sections for mouse C3 or human IgG as previously described[10] and scored the intensity of staining on a semiquantitative scale $(0 - 4+)$ by three observers who were blinded to the experimental condition. As an alternative approach to quantify aPL-antibody binding to decidual tissue, we treated pregnant mice with biotinylated aPL-IgG, removed uterine contents and harvested proteins by homogenizing tissue in NP-40 lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors. Proteins $(20\mathrm{mg / lane})$ were separated by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotted with streptavidin HRP-conjugate to detect biotinylated aPL-IgG.

In vitro assay of complement activation. We assessed the effect of heparins and other anticoagulants on complement activation in vitro using BeWo cells, a trophoblast-like human choriocarcinoma cell line (ATCC). Phosphatidylserine, externalized on BeWo cells as on human villous cytotrophoblasts, is recognized by aPL antibodies[39]. BeWo cells $(10^{6} / \mathrm{ml})$ were incubated with aPL-IgG $(500\mu \mathrm{g} / \mathrm{ml})$ at $37^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for $30\mathrm{min}$ to form an opsonized, complement-activating surface. Analysis by flow cytometry, using goat antibody to human IgG F(ab')2 (Jackson ImmunoResearch), showed that after incubation with aPL-IgG, $57\pm 13\%$ of BeWo cells bound human IgG compared to $9\pm 2\%$ of BeWo cells incubated with NH-IgG. We incubated washed aPL-IgG-opsonized BeWo cells with $10\%$ mouse or $10\%$ human serum (sources of complement) for $30\mathrm{min}$ and $15\mathrm{min}$ , respectively, at $37^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ in the presence or absence of UFH (5 U/ml or $10~\mathrm{U / ml}$ ), LMWH $(8\mu \mathrm{g} / \mathrm{ml})$ , hirudin $(2\mu \mathrm{g} / \mathrm{ml}$ or $4\mu \mathrm{g} / \mathrm{ml})$ or fondaparinux $(5\mu \mathrm{g} / \mathrm{ml})$ . Concentrations of anticoagulants for in vitro studies were based on calculated peak concentrations from mouse in vivo experiments. Incubation of aPL-IgG-opsonized BeWo cells with anticoagulants did not alter binding of human IgG, and we monitored binding of aPL-IgG to BeWo cells under each experimental condition. To assess the effects of anticoagulants on C3 activation, we measured generation of C3a and cell-bound C3b.
