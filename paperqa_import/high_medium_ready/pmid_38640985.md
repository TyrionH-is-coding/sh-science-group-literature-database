Maria G. Tektonidou*, Nikolaos I. Vlachogiannis, Petros P. Sfikakis

First Department of Propaedeutic Internal Medicine and Joint Academic Rheumatology Program, National and Kapodistrian University of Athens, Medical School, Athens, Greece

# ABSTRACT

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a systemic autoimmune disorder characterized by arterial and venous thrombosis, and obstetric complications in the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL), including lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin and anti- $\beta 2$ -glycoprotein I antibodies. APS manifests as single, often as recurrent events, and rarely as a catastrophic condition. Most studies of APS pathogenesis to date have focused on the prothrombotic role of aPL, while innate immune responses such as monocyte, complement and neutrophil activation have been also recognized as part of the thrombo-inflammatory cascade in APS. While the presence of autoreactive T cells against $\beta 2$ -glycoprotein I has been long known, less data are available on their pathogenetic role in APS. In this review, we summarize current knowledge on the involvement of T cells in APS pathophysiology, alterations of T cell subsets in peripheral blood, and clinical associations. We also highlight potential therapeutic opportunities by targeting T helper-B cell interactions in these patients.

# 1. Introduction

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a rare autoimmune disease presenting mainly with thrombotic and/or pregnancy complications in association with persistently positive antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL), namely lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin (aCL) and anti- $\beta2$ -glycoprotein 1 (anti- $\beta2$ GPI) antibodies [1,2]. APS can occur either as primary APS (PAPS) or in association with other systemic autoimmune diseases (secondary APS, SAPS), most commonly systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE/APS) [3]. Thrombosis in APS can affect literally any blood vessel. Deep vein thrombosis and stroke comprise the most frequent venous and arterial vascular events, respectively [3]. APS has a 10-year mortality rate of approximately $10\%$ , with the majority of deaths being related to severe vascular events, such as stroke and myocardial infarction [3]. The currently recommended therapeutic approach to APS includes risk modification by lifestyle changes to avoid cardiovascular and venous thrombosis risk factors, low-dose aspirin, and vitamin K antagonists, while in some cases, immunomodulatory drugs may be considered [4-6].

The pathogenesis of APS is complex and involves thrombotic and immune mechanisms. The interaction of prothrombotic mechanisms with aberrant innate immune responses including endothelial cell activation [7], secretion of multiple proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, monocyte activation and tissue factor expression [8,9],

dendritic cell activation [10] and type I interferon production [11], complement C3a and C5a activation [12], and an interplay between neutrophils/neutrophil extracellular traps [13,14] and platelets, comprise the thromboinflammatory environment of APS [15,16]. Most mechanistic studies to date were focused on the role of aPL antibodies, and their effect on target cells. While the role of T helper cells in producing aPL antibodies has been long recognized [17], less data are available on the role of T cells in APS development. Herein, we aim to review the current literature on the pathogenetic role of T cells in APS, alterations of T cell subsets and function in APS patients and their clinical associations, and potential targeted treatments for the disruption of T helper-B cell interactions to reduce aPL antibody formation and subsequent thrombotic events.

# 2. Insights on the role of T cells in APS pathogenesis: lessons from animal models

The role of T cells in the production of aPL autoantibodies and the development of APS has been shown in a series of animal studies. In an early study by Blank and colleagues, APS was induced in naive mice by immunization with aCL monoclonal antibodies. Bone marrow transplantation from experimental primary APS mice into total body-irradiated naive BALB/c recipient mice resulted in high serum titers of aCL antibodies in the latter, and clinical/laboratory features of APS,

such as thrombocytopenia, prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time and frequent fetal resorptions (the equivalent of fetal loss in human). Of interest, transplantation of T cell-depleted bone marrow cells did not result in APS disease, highlighting the indispensable role of T cells in APS development in this model [18]. In another model of experimental SLE-APS induced by immunization with MIV-7 anti-DNA monoclonal antibodies, administration of anti-CD4 antibodies 2 months before immunization prevented clinical features of SLE-APS including leukopenia, proteinuria, and fetal resorptions. Similarly, anti-CD4-treated mice had significantly lower titers of aPLs. Of note, therapeutic administration of anti-CD4 antibodies two months after induction of SLE-APS resulted in sustained reduction of aCL titers and prevented the development of clinical manifestations [19]. Moreover, engraftment of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from an SLE-APS patient into BALB-Rag $2^{- / - }$ IL2Rgc $^{- / - }$ mice that lack T, B and NK cells resulted in an increased CD8/CD4 ratio compared to mice receiving PBMCs from healthy controls (HC). SLE-APS receiver mice produced high aCL antibody titers, developed micro-thrombi and had $\mathrm{CD3^{+}}$ $\mathrm{CD8^{+}}$ T cell and B cell infiltration in their kidneys [20]. On the other hand, injection of $\mathrm{CD8^{+}}$ T cells with immunosuppressive properties derived from mice previously treated with bromocriptine, a dopamine agonist with immunomodulatory properties, prevented disease development in experimental SLE and APS mice suggesting also a central role of $\mathrm{CD8^{+}}$ T cells in SLE-APS pathogenesis [21]. Finally, orally-induced $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ tolerance ameliorated in vivo T cell responses to $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ , and tolerance could be adoptively transferred by $\mathrm{CD8^{+}}$ T cells from tolerized into naive mice [22,23].

The above-mentioned studies supported the role of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -reactive T cells in the production of anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ antibodies and the development of clinically-evident APS in animal models. Eliminating autoreactive T cells or inducing a suppressive/tolerant state showed promising results as a therapeutic option in experimental APS. In a more recent ex vivo approach, Shoenfeld and colleagues showed that IL-10/TGF- $\mathbf{b}1$ generated tolerogenic dendritic cells against $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ were able to induce $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -specific-unresponsiveness in effector memory $\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ T cells derived from APS patients, while maintaining the proliferative response to an unrelated candidin [24]. The potential therapeutic role of tolerant DCs in APS immunity and inflammation needs further evaluation.

Whether the associations between T-cell activity and aPL production are reflective of an independent T-cell activity rather than a supportive role of T-cells on B-cell activation, a driving pathogenetic force in the disease, remains to be further investigated. Both T-cell dependent and Tcell independent mechanisms are likely to play a role in the induction of aPL but additional research is needed to elucidate their role. Of interest, MHCII-null mice, practically lacking $\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ T cell-dependent activation of B cells, still developed aPLs, albeit at reduced titers and speed of induction and with limited epitope spreading [25]. However, a limitation of this aPL mouse model is that it uses heterologous $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ as the antigen to induce APS.

# 3. Thrombogenic and proinflammatory effects of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -reactive T cells

A first hint that T cells are involved in the pathogenesis of human APS comes from the association of certain major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II alleles with APS, as well as with a class switch of aPL antibodies into an IgG class, which requires B cell activation by $\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ (helper) T cells [17]. APL recognize a number of autoantigens, but the primary target in both APS and SLE is $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ [1,17], a phospholipid-binding plasma protein with antithrombotic properties. Autoreactive $\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ T cells to $\beta 2$ -GPI have been shown to promote pathogenic anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ antibody production in APS patients [26]. $\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ T cells autoreactive to $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ in APS preferentially recognize a cryptic peptide encompassing amino acid residues 276-290 (p276-290), which is located in domain V of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ and contains the major phospholipid

binding site [26,27]. Of interest, these autoreactive $\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ T cells have been shown to react only against reduced or recombinant $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ , but not against native $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ (Fig. 1), and their clones are HLA-DR restricted (DR53) [26-32]. Moreover, $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -reactive T cell responses are associated with epitope spread to SLE-related autoantibodies [33].

Binding of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ on phospholipids is indispensable to generate reactive $\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ T cells through antigen processing by dendritic cells or macrophages, while $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ or phospholipids alone do not induce a T cell response [34]. B2GPI-specific T-cell clones isolated from APS patients were able to stimulate the production of anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ antibodies by autologous peripheral blood B cells ex vivo in the presence of recombinant $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ . However, this T-cell helper activity was exclusively found in clones producing interleukin 6 (IL-6). Of interest, IL-6 blockade and antibodies against CD40 ligand were able to inhibit anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ antibody production [27]. Whether anti-IL6 or anti-CD40/CD40L therapeutics may hold therapeutic value in APS remains to be examined in future clinical trials. In line with this, exposure of wild-type C57BL/6 mice to human $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ in the presence of lipopolysaccharide led to the development of anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ autoantibodies and the activation of splenic T cells of the proinflammatory Th1 phenotype. This suggests that exposure of immune cells to native $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ in the presence of a strong systematic inflammatory response may be sufficient to generate an anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ response [35]. Peripheral mononuclear cells isolated from apparently healthy individuals and incubated with aCL antibodies were skewed towards a Th2 and Th17 phenotype, while Th1 and Treg frequencies were decreased [36]. APL antibodies targeting the D1 domain of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ have also been shown to activate $\mathrm{CD8^{+}}$ T cells in vitro further inducing the expression of CD69 [37].

Oxidized $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ causes bone marrow-derived dendritic cells to mature in vitro stimulating the secretion of multiple proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1, IL-6, IL-8, and tumor necrosis factor $\alpha$ . These $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -activated dendritic cells in turn, primed naive T lymphocytes towards a proinflammatory Th1 polarization [38]. Of interest, we recently showed that oxidative stress levels are significantly higher in peripheral blood mononuclear cells of patients with primary thrombotic APS compared to age- and sex-matched healthy individuals and asymptomatic aPL positive individuals [39]. Whether anti-oxidants may result in lower anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ titers and subsequent prevention of thrombotic events remains to be further elucidated.

# 4. Altered circulating T cell subsets in APS patients

A number of studies have described alterations in T cell subsets in the peripheral blood of APS patients, with the majority pointing towards upregulated proinflammatory Th1/Th17 responses and downregulated anti-inflammatory T regulatory (Treg) cell responses, although conflicting results have been reported (Table 1) [40].

# 4.1. Associations of altered T cell subsets with distinct APS phenotypes

Several studies have examined the association of altered T cell subsets with distinct clinical phenotypes of APS, including patients with primary or secondary APS, patients with either single or recurrent thrombosis, obstetric APS alone or patients with both thrombotic and obstetric APS history, or aPL+ patients with systemic autoimmune disease not fulfilling criteria for APS, pointing towards a functional role of these cells in disease pathogenesis.

# 4.1.1. T cell subset alterations in PAPS vs SAPS patients

A recent study examining 12 patients with PAPS, 55 patients with SAPS (mostly SLE- and Sjogren's Syndrome-associated APS) and 40 healthy individuals, revealed significant increase in the absolute number of total T cells and $\mathrm{CD4^+}$ T cells in PAPS vs SAPS patients. Of interest, all APS patients showed increased Th1, decreased Th2, and decreased Treg levels, as well as increased Th17/Treg ratio compared to HC. # 4.1.2. T cell subset alterations in thrombotic APS patients

In a study including 38 thrombotic PAPS patients and 52 HC, PAPS patients had higher percentages of activated $\mathrm{CD4^{+}HLA - DR^{+}}$ and of $\mathrm{CD8^{+}HLA - DR^{+}}$ T-cells than healthy controls, but similar to SLE counterparts $(n = 16)$ or $\mathrm{aPL^{+}}$ patients with other systemic autoimmune diseases $(n = 14)$ [49]. On the other hand, using a deconvolution analysis in the peripheral blood cell transcriptome of 62 thrombotic PAPS patients and 29 age- and sex-matched healthy individuals, we did not observe differences in the examined T cell subsets $(\mathrm{CD8^{+}T}$ cells, naive $\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ T cells, resting and activated $\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ memory T cells, Tfh cells, Tregs and $\gamma \delta$ T cells) [11].

# 4.1.3. T cell subset alterations in obstetric APS patients with or without thrombosis

In a study of obstetric APS patients with or without thrombosis, nonthrombotic obstetric APS patients had higher percentages of central memory helper T cells $\left(\mathrm{CD4}^{+} \mathrm{CD45RA}^{-} \mathrm{CCR7}^{+}\right)$ and activated

$\mathrm{CD4^{+}CD25^{+}}$ T cells, but lower percentages and absolute counts of effector memory helper T cells $(\mathrm{CD4^{+}CD45RA^{-}CCR7^{-}})$ compared to healthy non-parous women. On the other hand, patients with thrombotic obstetric APS had higher percentages of activated $\mathrm{CD4^{+}HLA - DR^{+}}$ and $\mathrm{CD4^{+}CD25^{+}HLA - DR^{+}}$ T cells vs all controls, increased percentage of activated $\mathrm{CD8^{+}HLA - DR^{+}}$ T cells vs non-thrombotic obstetric APS and vs healthy non-parous/parous women, as well as increased percentage of $\mathrm{CD4^{+}CD38^{+}HLA - DR^{+}}$ T cells vs healthy non-parous women [50]. In another study including 36 female patients with obstetric APS and 30 healthy women, both Cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA4)+Tfh and $\mathrm{CTLA4^{+}}$ Tfr cells were decreased, while $\mathrm{CTLA4^{+}}$ Tfr/Tfh ratio was increased in obstetric APS. Tfh and Tfr cells were skewed towards a Th17 subtype, while both Th1 and Th2 subtypes were decreased in obstetric APS. Regarding the activation status of Tfh and Tfr cells, effector memory subtypes were increased, while central memory subtypes were decreased in obstetric APS [51].

# 4.2. Association of activated T cell subsets with increased risk of thrombosis and atherosclerosis in APS

Evidence has shown that higher levels of activated $(\mathrm{HLA - DR}^{+})$ T cells in the peripheral blood are associated with an increased risk of thrombosis. In obstetric APS patients, a $10\%$ increase in the percentage of activated $\mathrm{CD8^{+}HLA - DR^{+}}$ T cells conferred a 2.5-fold higher risk of thrombosis (relative risk 2.43, $95\%$ confidence interval: $1.1 - 5.4$ $p = 0.02)$ during a mean follow-up of 7 years, which remained significant after adjustment for anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ titers in multivariable regression analysis [50]. In line with this, another study showed that higher percentages of $\mathrm{CD8^{+}HLA - DR^{+}}$ T cells were associated with APS-related complications including recurrent thrombosis and neuropsychiatric complications [49].

Evidence from mechanistic [7,15] and clinical studies [52-55] supports an increasing risk of atherosclerosis in APS, which seems to involve local activation and proinflammatory transformation of T cells. # 4.3. Association of altered T cell subsets with aPL titers

In a study examining the frequency of T cell subsets in the peripheral blood of PAPS patients, the Th17/Treg ratio positively correlated with aCL and anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ antibody titers [41]. Of interest, another study showed that Treg cells of PAPS patients with triple aPL positivity and concomitantly high aPL IgG titers showed a decreased expression of the latency associated peptide that binds TGF- $\beta$ , and a trend for decreased TGF $\beta 1$ production in in vitro assays [44], supporting a hypothesis of reduced immune regulation in APS subgroups with more severe phenotype.

Another study in APS showed a positive correlation of Tfh cells and their subsets with new memory B cells and plasmablasts as well as with anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ aCL and lupus anticoagulant titers, while Tfr cells and their subsets showed a negative correlation [48]. Among obstetric APS patients, increased $\mathrm{CTLA4^{+}}$ Tfr/Tfh ratio positively correlated with anti $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ IgM titers and C4 levels [51]. Moreover, in a study including 36 obstetric APS patients (with or without a history of thrombosis) and 108 controls, the percentage of activated $\mathrm{CD4^{+}CD38^{+}HLA - DR^{+}}$ T lymphocytes positively correlated with the aCL IgG titers [50]. Finally, in our dataset using RNA-sequencing and deconvolution analysis, we did not observe differences in the examined T cell subsets $(\mathrm{CD8^{+}}$ T cells, naive $\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ T cells, resting and activated $\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ memory T cells, Tfh cells, Tregs and $\gamma \delta$ T cells) between triple-aPL-positive and non-triple-aPL-positive thrombotic PAPS patients [11].

# 5. Targeting T cell responses and B cell-T cell interactions in APS treatment

Finally, modulation of T cell activity may be partly responsible for

the efficacy of immunosuppressive treatments used in previously defined non-criteria APS manifestations [5,6] or rarely in its catastrophic form. Sirolimus, an inhibitor of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), has shown a protective effect against renal vascular complications in APS patients (APS nephropathy), which has been mainly attributed to the prevention of endothelial cell activation and proliferation [64]. However, the mTOR protein complex, which includes mTORC1 and mTORC2, has also been shown to directly affect the activation of T cells in rheumatic diseases [65,66]. Sirolimus has been shown to inhibit antigen-induced T cell proliferation [67], which could reduce the number of autoreactive $\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ T cells in APS patients. On the other hand, Sirolimus has also been shown to increase the number of Tregs and restore the Th17/Treg balance [68], which is disturbed in APS patients (see Table 1). Further research is needed to elucidate the efficacy of Sirolimus in APS and the importance of T cell modulation in the clinical response.

Moreover, B cell activating factor (BAFF) blockade prevented disease onset and significantly prolonged survival in a murine model of spontaneous APS, although it did not affect aPL [69], which is in line with our post-hoc analysis of two SLE trials that did not show any significant effect of Belimumab treatment on aPL titers, independently of hydroxychloroquine use [70]. On the other hand, BAFF-blockade in the APS murine model prevented kidney damage compared to controls, which is dependent on both renal immune complexes and effector cells [69]. Indeed, treated mice had fewer activated T cells in their spleen [69]. In our previous work in SLE patients treated with anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies, mRNA levels of the costimulatory/activation T cell molecule CD40L were profoundly reduced. More importantly, these changes persisted in those patients with sustained clinical remission but were reversed in patients who relapsed [71]. Taken together, these results suggest that the effect of B cell-targeting therapies may partly rely on the alteration of T cell activation. Future clinical trials targeting B cell-T cell interactions in PAPS and SLE-APS are warranted.

Recently, a CRISPR-Cas9/12a-based genome-editing platform was developed to incorporate $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ into the T cell receptor-CD3 complex of human T cells [72]. The $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -CATCR-T cells eliminated anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ domain I B cells, while sparing normal B cells [72], holding promise for targeted low-infection risk treatments in APS and other systemic autoimmune disorders.

# 6. Conclusions

Experimental evidence supports an integral role of autoreactive T cells against $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ in the production of aPL antibodies and the development of APS manifestations. Altered T cell subsets in the peripheral blood of APS patients suggest an expansion of activated, proinflammatory T cells and numerical or functional reduction of suppressive T cells, mainly Tregs. T cell-B cell interactions comprise a promising therapeutic target in a subset of APS patients and warrant further exploration, as do the autoantigen-specific T-cell therapies.
