Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a systemic autoimmune disease characterized by recurrent vascular thrombosis and pregnancy morbidities associated with the persistent presence of autoantibodies against phospholipid-binding proteins (aPLs), such as $\beta 2$ glycoprotein I ( $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ ) (1). In addition to their role in the acquired procoagulant diathesis, aPLs were associated with accelerated atherosclerosis to explain cardiovascular manifestations of the syndrome (2-4).

Atherosclerosis is a multifactorial disease for which a number of pathogenic mechanisms have been proposed. In addition to classical risk factors, in the last two decades the attention has focused on inflammatory processes (5). Observations in humans and animals suggest that atherosclerotic plaques derive from specific

cellular and molecular mechanisms that can be ascribed to an inflammatory disease of the arterial wall, the lesions of which consist of activated macrophages and T lymphocytes. If inflammation continues unabated, it results in more plaque-infiltrating macrophages and T cells, which contribute to the remodeling of the arterial wall, eventually favoring plaque instability and rupture (6). Within the T cell population infiltrating the plaque, most cells are activated $\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ Th1 and Th17 cells expressing HLA-DR and IL-2R (CD25) (7, 8). Current evidence indicates that autoimmunity can be detected within the atherosclerotic lesions (9). Accordingly, self-phospholipids, such as oxidized low-density lipoproteins (oxLDL) and human heat shock proteins, drive T cell inflammation in atherosclerotic patients (10, 11). However, the

It was suggested that the development of anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -specific responses in the target organ may contribute to atherothrombosis in APS. This hypothesis is largely based on the presence of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ in human atherosclerotic plaques (12) and on the enhanced fatty streak formation in transgenic atherosclerosis-prone mice immunized with $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ (13, 14). Moreover, $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -reactive T cells have also been found to promote early atherosclerosis in low-density lipoprotein (LDL) receptor-deficient mice (15). In contrast, there is no clear evidence for accelerated atherosclerosis in patients, because increased numbers of plaques or arterial wall thickness are not found in primary APS patients with no underlying systemic autoimmune diseases (3, 4, 16). Despite that, aPLs predict adverse arterial outcomes (4, 17-19).

In this study, we demonstrate that, in primary APS patients, $\beta 2$ GPI-specific Th1 cells infiltrate atherosclerotic plaques and that $\beta 2$ GPI drives a local Th1 inflammatory response that can be responsible for plaque instability and rupture, eventually favoring atherothrombosis. Collectively these findings explain why an atherothrombotic phenomenon rather than an increased plaque formation can contribute to arterial events in APS patients.

# Materials and Methods

# Reagents

Human $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ was purified by perchloric acid treatment of pooled normal human sera obtained from blood donors, followed by affinity purification on a Heparin column (HiTrap Heparin HP) and ion-exchange chromatography (Resource-S; both from GE Healthcare, Mila, Italy) (20). Human rIL-2 and tetanus toxoid (TT) were provided by Novartis (Siena, Italy). PHA was purchased from Life Technologies (Carlsbad, CA). $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ domains (DI and DIV-DV) were obtained as previously described (21).

Fluorochrome-conjugated human mAbs anti-CD3, anti-CD4, anti-CD8, anti-CXCR5, anti-IFN- $\gamma$ and isotype-matched control mAb were purchased from BD Biosciences (San Jose, CA). The fluorochrome-conjugated anti-CXCR3 mAb was purchased from R&D Systems (Minneapolis, MN), and the fluorochrome-conjugated anti-IL-17 mAb was obtained from eBioscience (San Diego, CA). PMA, ionomycin, and brefeldin A were purchased from BD Biosciences.

# Patients

Upon approval of the local Ethical Committee, four patients (two males and two females, mean age 52 y, range 43-56 y) with APS and four aPL-negative patients (two males and two females, mean age 52 y, range 44-56 y), all with arterial occlusive disease of the lower extremities, were included in the study. The four APS patients with arterial occlusive disease of the lower extremities underwent open vascular surgical treatment of their disease by thromboendarterectomy: two had aortoiliac stenosis, and two suffered from femoral-iliac stenosis due to atheroma. The four aPL-negative patients with arterial occlusive disease of the lower extremities were also treated with thromboendarterectomy; two had aortoiliac stenosis, and two had femoral-iliac stenosis due to atheroma.

APS patients and aPL-negative patients were eligible for vascular surgery. APS patients were triple positive for aPL, with high titers of serum anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ , anti-cardiolipin (aCL) Abs, and positivity for lupus anticoagulant, whereas aPL-negative patients were triple negative (Fig. 1). All four APS patients in this study satisfied Myiakis's criteria for primary APS; they were on oral anticoagulation with vitamin K antagonists and were switched to low m.w. heparin a few days before surgery (22). None of them displayed traditional risk factors for atherosclerosis and were not receiving any anti-lipidemic drugs. All aPL-negative patients were on low-dose aspirin at the time of surgery and received therapy with vasodilators or statins.

# Anti-phospholipid Ab detection

For the detection of aCL and aβ2GPI in patient sera, commercially available solid-phase ELISAs using purified human cardiolipin and human β2GPI were used (Inova Diagnostics). Sera were considered positive when their concentration exceeded the cut-off of $10\mathrm{U / ml}$ for IgG and IgM. All samples were tested by respective in-house assays, as described elsewhere (18, 22). The results of the two techniques were comparable.

Analysis of lupus anticoagulant was performed in accordance with the international recommendations, as described recently (22, 23).

# Generation and characterization of T cell clones from atherosclerotic plaques' inflammatory infiltrates

Aortoiliac or femoral-iliac artery wall specimens, obtained by thromboendarterectomy, were investigated in APS and in aPL-negative patients under the same experimental conditions. Plaque fragments were cultured for 7 d in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with IL-2 (50 U/ml) to expand in vivo-activated T cells. Specimens were then disrupted, and single T cells were cloned under limiting dilution, as described (7, 8). To assess their phenotype profile, T cell clones were screened by flow cytometry with fluorochrome-conjugated anti-CD3, anti-CD4, and anti-CD8 on a BD FACSCanto II (BD Biosciences), using FACS Diva 6.1.3. software.

T cell clones were analyzed for their responsiveness to $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ by measuring $\left[^{3}\mathrm{H}\right]$ thymidine uptake after $60\mathrm{h}$ of coculture with irradiated autologous PBMCs in the presence of medium, $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ $(10~\mathrm{nM})$ or TT $(0.3~\mathrm{nM})$

In parallel, each T cell clone was also screened for responsiveness to PHA $(1\%, \mathrm{v} / \mathrm{v})$ , used as positive control, by measuring $\left[^{3}\mathrm{H}\right]$ thymidine uptake after $36\mathrm{h}$ of coculture with irradiated autologous PBMCs. Sixteen hours before harvesting, $0.5\mu \mathrm{Ci}$ of $\left[^{3}\mathrm{H}\right]\mathrm{dT}$ (Amersham Pharmacia Biotech, Piscataway, NJ) was added, and the radionuclide uptake was measured. The mitogenic index was calculated as the ratio of mean values of cpm obtained in stimulated cultures/those obtained in the presence of medium alone. A value $>5$ was considered positive.

# Assessment of T cell clones' cytokine profile

To assess the cytokine production of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -specific T cell clones upon Ag stimulation, $5\times 10^{5}$ T cell blasts of each clone were cocultured for $48\mathrm{h}$ in $0.5\mathrm{ml}$ of serum-free medium with $5\times 10^{5}$ irradiated autologous PBMCs in the absence or presence of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ $(10\mathrm{nM})$ . At the end of the culture period, duplicate samples of each supernatant were assayed by ELISA (BioSource International, Camarillo, CA) for IFN- $\gamma$ , TNF- $\alpha$ , IL-4, and IL-17 production (6). To verify whether these cells were Th or T follicular helper T cell clones (24), $\mathrm{CD4}^+$ T cell blasts were polyclonally stimulated with Leukocyte Activation Cocktail with BD GolgiPlug containing PMA, ionomycin, and brefeldin A. After $5\mathrm{h}$ of stimulation, external staining with anti-CXCR3 and anti-CXCR5 was performed. Then T cells were fixed in formaldehyde and permeabilized to perform intracellular staining with anti-IFN- $\gamma$ and anti-IL-17.

# Submolecular specificity of plaque-derived T cell clones reactive to $\beta 2GPI$

To further investigate the submolecular specificity of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -reactive T cell clones, T cell blasts from each $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -specific T cell clone were stimulated with medium, $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ (10 nM) or $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ domains, such as DI (10 nM) and DIV-DV (10 nM), in the presence of autologous APCs for 48 h in ELISPOT microplates coated with anti-IFN- $\gamma$ or anti-IL-17 Ab, respectively (eBioscience). At the end of culture period, the number of IFN- $\gamma$ and IL-17 spot-forming cells (SFC) were counted as described (8).

# T cell clone-mediated cytotoxicity and Fas/Fas ligand-mediated proapoptotic activity

T cell clones' cytolytic activity was assessed as reported (7). T cell blasts of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ specific T cell clones were incubated at ratios of 10:1, 5:1, and 2.5:1 with ${}^{51}\mathrm{Cr}$ -labeled autologous EBV-transformed B cells preincubated with $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ $(10~\mathrm{nM})$ or TT $(0.3~\mathrm{nM})$ . After centrifugation, microplates were incubated for $8\mathrm{h}$ at $37^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ , and $0.1\mathrm{ml}$ of supernatant was removed for the measurement of $[{}^{51}\mathrm{Cr}]$ release, as reported (7). The ability of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -specific T cell clones to induce Fas/Fas ligand (FasL)-mediated apoptosis was assessed using $\mathrm{Fas}^{+}$ Jurkat cells as target. T cell blasts from each clone were cocultured with ${}^{51}\mathrm{Cr}$ -labeled Jurkat cells at an E:T ratio of 10:1, 5:1, and 2.5:1 for $18\mathrm{h}$ in the presence of PMA $(10~\mathrm{ng / ml})$ and ionomycin $(1\mu \mathrm{mol / ml})$ , as reported (8).

# Assay for T helper function for monocyte TF and matrix metalloproteinase-9 production

# Results

Autoreactive $CD4^{+}$ T cell clones specific for $\beta 2GPI$ are present in atherosclerotic lesions of atherothrombotic APS patients

Atherosclerotic plaque-infiltrating in vivo-activated T cells were expanded in vitro in an human rIL-2-conditioned medium, cloned, and studied for their phenotypic and functional profile (Fig. 1). A total of $115\mathrm{CD4}^+$ and $19\mathrm{CD8}^+$ T cell clones was obtained from atherosclerotic lesions of four APS atherothrombotic patients. For each patient, $\mathrm{CD4}^+$ and $\mathrm{CD8}^+$ atherosclerotic lesion-derived T cell clones were assayed for proliferation in response to medium, $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ , TT, or PHA. None of the $\mathrm{CD8}^+$ T cell clones showed proliferation to $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ or TT, although they proliferated in response to mitogen stimulation. None of the $\mathrm{CD4}^+$ T cell clones showed proliferation to TT, whereas they proliferated in response to mitogen stimulation (Fig. 2A). We also investigated the amount of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -specific T cells present in peripheral blood and compared it with the amount found in atheromas. The proportion of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -specific $\mathrm{CD4}^+$ T cell clones generated from atherosclerotic plaques of atherothrombotic APS patients ranged from 22 to $30\%$ , which is remarkably higher compared with the frequency of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -specific T cells found in the peripheral blood of the same patients (between 1:1800 and 1:3500).

A total of 32 of the 115 $(28\%)$ $\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ T cell clones generated from atherosclerotic plaque-infiltrating T cells proliferated significantly in response to $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ (Fig. 2A). Each APS patient displayed a comparable percentage of $\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ T cell clones that were responsive to $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ . In contrast, a total of $263\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ and $45\mathrm{CD8^{+}}$ T cell clones was obtained from atherosclerotic lesions from the four atherothrombotic patients who were negative for aPL. For each patient, $\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ and $\mathrm{CD8^{+}}$ atherosclerotic lesion-derived T cell clones were assayed for proliferation in response to medium, $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ , TT, or PHA. None of the $\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ or $\mathrm{CD8^{+}}$ T cell clones derived from the atherosclerotic lesions of patients negative for aPL showed proliferation in response to $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ or to TT, although they proliferated in response to mitogen stimulation (Supplemental Table I).

$\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ T cell clones obtained from the peripheral blood of APS and aPL-negative atherothrombotic patients displayed comparable proliferative responses to TT and PHA (Supplemental Table II).

# Discussion

Several experimental models suggest a role for aPLs in accelerating atherosclerotic plaque formation, although there are controversial findings in clinical studies. In contrast, there is growing evidence that aPLs represent a risk for arterial thrombosis, supporting their pathogenic role in cardiovascular events (1, 3, 4, 16). We report, for the first time to our knowledge, that a proinflammatory and procoagulant $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -specific Th1 infiltrate in human atherosclerotic lesions of patients with primary APS may represent a key pathogenic atherothrombotic mechanism.

A number of self-antigens may theoretically be involved in APS atherosclerosis; among them, oxLDLs are good candidates, and oxLDL-specific peripheral blood-derived T cells displaying a Th1 profile were reported in APS patients (26). However, there is no information on whether these cells are actively involved in atherosclerotic tissue lesions. In addition, $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ was found to bind oxLDLs (27), raising the issue of whether the immune response is against oxLDLs or $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ itself.

The relevance of the data presented in this article lies in the demonstration that all four APS patients with clinically severe atherothrombosis harbored in their target tissues, such as the atherosclerotic lesions, activated, in vivo, $\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ T cells that reacted to $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ . With the experimental procedure used in this study, the proportion of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -specific $\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ T cell clones generated from atherosclerotic plaques of atherothrombotic APS patients is remarkably higher than the frequency of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -specific T cells found in the peripheral blood of the same patients.

A reasonable objection may be that the high proportion of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -specific $\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ T cell clones merely reflects the IL-2-induced selective expansion of one or a few $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -specific T cells. However, using the same experimental approach, a high number of $\mathrm{CD8^{+}}$ T cell clones was obtained in different immunopathological conditions, such as acute kidney graft rejection, toluene diisocyanate-related bronchial asthma, Graves' ophthalmopathy, and Job-like syndrome (28-31).

The presence of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -reactive T cells is in agreement with the earlier demonstration of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -specific T cells with restricted TCR $\beta$ -chain usage in the peripheral blood of APS patients (32). The submolecular specificity study of T cells, obtained from the inflammatory infiltrate of atherosclerotic lesions of APS patients, showed that the majority of Th clones specific for $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ recognized their epitopes within the DI domain. The data obtained from the current study indicate that the DI domain is the major T cell stimulus that is able to keep the predominant B cell reactivity to DI in APS patients (33). Just a few $\mathrm{CD4^+}$ T cell clones recognized their specific epitope within DIV-DV domains. Anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ IgA Abs against DIV-DV associated with thromboembolic events were described in systemic lupus erythematosus patients (34), whereas $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -reactive $\mathrm{CD4^+}$ peripheral T cells recognizing DV 244-264 or 276-290 epitopes and exhibiting Th0-Th2-type responses were found in APS/ systemic lupus erythematosus patients (35-37). The apparent discordance with our findings is not surprising; it is well known that T cells specific for the same Ag may display different epitope specificities and effector functions when circulating in the peripheral blood or after their homing into the target tissues (38-40). Moreover, the fact that cell reactivity may change, depending on the presence or absence of domains other than DI, fits well with the known conformational changes in the molecule due to interactions among domains. It was suggested that DI is complexed with DV and hindered in the circular form of blood $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ , but it can be exposed in the open J form after the molecule's binding to anionic structures (41). Accordingly, a human mAb specific for the DI epitope reacts better with tissue $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ than with the molecule in fluid phase (42). Thus, it is not surprising that autoantigen-specific T cells derived from the target organ recognize such an immunodominant epitope that is available at the inflammatory site, in contrast to peripheral blood cells, which are mainly in contact with the circular form of the molecule.

T cell recognition of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ resulted in proliferation and production of IFN- $\gamma$ and TNF- $\alpha$ , whereas the production of IL-17 and IL-4 was limited to a few clones. These data suggest that $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ represents a powerful signal that is able to activate the functional program of Th1 cells in the arterial wall of APS patients with atherothrombosis. We considered the possibility that this outcome does not reflect the real attitude of plaque-infiltrating T cells in vivo but is the result of in vitro artifacts due to the culture in IL-2 and the cloning protocol. However, using the same culture and cloning procedure, T cell clones with a predominant Th17 profile were obtained from atherosclerotic lesions of patients with Chlamydia pneumoniae-associated atherosclerosis (8), from the gastric mucosa of Helicobacter pylori-associated gastric adenocarcinoma (43), and from synovial fluid of patients with Lyme disease (44). Using the same method, T cell clones with predominant Th2 profile were obtained from the mucosa of patients with allergic diseases or helminth infections (31, 45), as well as from the skin of patients with systemic sclerosis or HIV-1 infection (31, 40, 45).

Our findings are in agreement with the ex vivo data indicating a preferential expression of IFN- $\gamma$ and its pivotal role in atherosclerotic plaques (7, 9, 46, 47). In fact, aortic lesion size, lipid composition, and macrophage accumulation in plaques, as well as progression of the process, were significantly diminished in IFN- $\gamma^{- / - }$ mice and were reduced significantly compared with wild-type ones (47, 48).

In addition, upon appropriate Ag stimulation, the majority of atherosclerotic plaque-derived $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -specific clones induced

perforin-mediated cytolysis and Fas/FasL-mediated apoptosis in target cells. It is possible to speculate that Ag-presenting macrophages within the plaque become targets of the cytotoxic and proapoptotic activity displayed by activated Th1 cells and are involved in the necrotic cores that are characteristic of complicated atherosclerotic lesions. Indeed, local IFN- $\gamma$ and TNF- $\alpha$ production by Th1 cells can increase the expression of MHC class II molecules by endothelial cells, which, in turn, enables these cells to present $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ to T cells that further differentiate into Th1 effector cells (49). Our results support the notion that cytotoxic and proapoptotic mechanisms are involved in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis (8, 50, 51).

Based on these findings, it is tempting to hypothesize that, in the inflammatory setting of the APS atherosclerotic plaque, endothelial cells may acquire APC function and, together with professional APCs, can themselves become targets of the cytotoxic and proapoptotic activity of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -specific Th cells. The outcome of this process would be expansion of the plaque and the formation of necrotic cores that are characteristic of complicated and unstable atherosclerotic lesions. Apoptosis of smooth muscle cells may also contribute to the process (52, 53).

Bearing in mind that CD40L is the primary T cell-derived driver of TF, we showed that activated $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -specific Th1 cells contributed to the upregulation of TF production by monocytes, thus further contributing to the thrombogenicity of lesions (51, 54).

Several studies provided a clear indication of the important role for MMPs in atherothrombosis, whereas different signals may affect different enzymes (8, 55). It was suggested that ischemic complications may, in fact, depend more on plaque composition than on the degree of stenosis. The composition of the extracellular matrix may affect plaque progression and also be a key determinant of plaque vulnerability/instability (55-59). The infiltration of inflammatory cells into the atherosclerotic lesion results in a major increase in the MMPs' activity (8, 55-59). $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ specific T cell clones contributed to stimulate the in vitro production of MMP-9 from patients' monocytes.

Autoreactive $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ peripheral $\mathrm{CD4^{+}}$ T cells were reported to promote aPL production via T cell-dependent helper activity (36). Although T cells that actually provide help within B cell follicles are T follicular helper cells, and not Th1 or Th2 cells (60), we found, using in vitro assays, that $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -specific T cell clones, derived from plaques' inflammatory infiltrates, were able to enhance Ab production.

The identification of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ , and especially of the DI domain, as the major driving Ag for atherothrombosis-associated T cell responses may be of importance for designing novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies aimed at targeting the identified molecular players in APS.

The most relevant observation in the current study is that a crucial component of the atherosclerotic plaque infiltrate is represented by $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -driven Th1 autoimmune responses, which may result in a breakdown of tolerance associated with chronic inflammation, plaque instability, and, ultimately, atherothrombosis in predisposed individuals. This finding may balance the lack of evidence for surrogate markers of arterial atherosclerosis, such as number of plaques and increased arterial wall thickness, in primary APS patients with a high rate of arterial events.
