# Calprotectin Impairs Platelet Survival in Patients With Primary Antiphospholipid Syndrome

Claire K. Hoy, $^{1}$ Somanathapura K. NaveenKumar, $^{1}$ Sherwin A. Navaz, $^{1}$ Kavya Sugur, $^{1}$ Srilakshmi Yalavarthi, $^{1}$ Cyrus Sarosh, $^{1}$ Tristin Smith, $^{1}$ Katarina Kmetova, $^{2}$ Emily Chong, $^{1}$ Noah F. Peters, $^{1}$ Christine E. Rysenga, $^{1}$ Gary L. Norman, $^{3}$ Gabriel Figueroa-Parra, $^{4}$ Dava Nelson, $^{1}$ Jennifer Girard, $^{1}$ Asra Z. Ahmed, $^{1}$ Jordan K. Schaefer, $^{1}$ Johann E. Gudjonsson, $^{1}$ J. Objective. While thrombosis and pregnancy loss are the best-known clinical features of antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), many patients also exhibit "extra-criteria" manifestations, such as thrombocytopenia. The mechanisms that drive APS thrombocytopenia are not completely understood, and no clinical biomarkers are available for predicting antiphospholipid antibody (aPL)-mediated thrombocytopenia. Calprotectin is a heterodimer of S100A8 and S100A9 that is abundant in the neutrophil cytoplasm and released upon proinflammatory neutrophil activation. Here, we sought to evaluate the presence, clinical associations, and potential mechanistic roles of circulating calprotectin in a cohort of primary APS and aPL-positive patients.

Methods. Levels of circulating calprotectin were determined in plasma by the QUANTA Flash chemiluminescent assay. A viability dye-based platelet assay was used to assess the potential impact of calprotectin on aPL-mediated thrombocytopenia.

Results. Circulating calprotectin was measured in 112 patients with primary APS and 30 aPL-positive (without APS criteria manifestations or lupus) patients as compared to patients with lupus (without APS), patients with unprovoked venous thrombosis (without aPL), and healthy controls. Levels of calprotectin were higher in patients with primary APS and aPL-positive patients compared to healthy controls. After adjustment for age and sex, calprotectin level correlated positively with absolute neutrophil count $(r = 0.41$ , $P < 0.001)$ , positively with C-reactive protein level $(r = 0.34$ , $P = 0.002)$ , and negatively with platelet count $(r = -0.24$ , $P = 0.004)$ . Mechanistically, we found that calprotectin provoked aPL-mediated thrombocytopenia by engaging platelet surface toll-like receptor 4 and activating the NLRP3-inflammasome, thereby reducing platelet viability in a caspase-1-dependent manner.

Conclusion. These data suggest that calprotectin has the potential to be a functional biomarker and a new therapeutic target for APS thrombocytopenia.

# INTRODUCTION

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a thromboinflammatory disease that often results in devastating, sometimes life-threatening outcomes for patients. APS classification criteria require the

persistent presence of one or more antiphospholipid antibodies (aPLs) as defined by tests for lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin antibodies (aCLs) (IgG and/or IgM, $\geq 40$ IgG phospholipid/IgM phospholipid [GPL/MPL] units), and anti- $\beta_{2}$ glycoprotein I (anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI) antibodies (IgG and/or IgM, $\geq 40$ GPL/MPL units) in

<!-- page 2 -->
the setting of either vascular thrombosis or obstetric morbidity. $^{1}$ APS may exist as a primary standalone disease or be associated with other autoimmune diseases, such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). Clinically, APS represents a complex disease spectrum. For example, various "extra-criteria" clinical manifestations can be found in patients with APS, including thrombocytopenia, cardiac valve disease, cognitive dysfunction, and livedo reticularis or racemose. $^{1}$ Although aPL testing is useful for identifying traditional APS clinical manifestations, a clinically actionable biomarker for extra-criteria manifestations is not yet available.

Thrombocytopenia is a common APS extracriteria manifestation, occurring in $16\%$ to $53\%$ of patients with APS.[2,3] Although aPL-mediated platelet consumption and the presence of antiplatelet glycoprotein antibodies are thought to drive thrombocytopenia in some patients with APS, the exact cause has not been fully revealed, and there are likely multiple contributing mechanisms.[3] Interestingly, modest thrombocytopenia appears to identify patients with APS who are at a higher risk of future thrombosis.[4]

Recent studies suggest that neutrophils and their formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) (prothrombotic and proinflammatory tangles of DNA and proteins) play an important role in APS pathophysiology. For example, patients with primary APS have elevated levels of circulating NET remnants, and their neutrophils appear primed for spontaneous NET release. Purified aPL can trigger neutrophil activation in healthy control neutrophils, whereas high-risk patients with APS and recurrent thrombosis tend to have increased expression of neutrophil activation genes. Within neutrophils, calprotectin is a heterodimer of the S100A8 and S100A9 calcium-binding proteins and is abundantly found in the cytoplasm. Upon neutrophil activation or NET formation, calprotectin is released extracellularly, where it promotes inflammation by interacting with various innate receptors such as toll-like receptors (TLRs) and the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE). Although its potential as a clinical biomarker has been suggested in various rheumatic diseases, such as SLE and rheumatoid arthritis, neither the clinical nor mechanistic role of calprotectin in APS has been previously studied. We aimed to evaluate the presence, clinical associations, and potential mechanistic roles of circulating calprotectin in a large cohort of primary APS and aPL-positive patients.

# MATERIALS AND METHODS

Human samples and quantification of circulating calprotectin. More detailed protocols can be found in the Supplementary Methods. This study complied with all relevant ethical regulations and was approved by the University of Michigan Institutional Review Board (IRB) (HUM00122519, HUM00044257, and HUM00151834) and the Mayo Clinic IRB (site IRB: 20-000476). Sodium citrate plasma samples were collected from 112 patients with primary APS, 30 patients with persistent aPL but without “criteria” APS manifestations or lupus,

38 patients with SLE without APS, 11 patients with a history of unprovoked venous thrombosis without aPL, 5 patients with idiopathic thrombocytopenia (ITP) without aPL or systemic rheumatologic conditions, and 18 healthy controls (Supplementary Table 1). Plasma samples were stored at $-80^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ and used for calprotectin and aPL measurements. Circulating calprotectin levels in human plasma were quantified by QUANTA Flash chemiluminescent immunoassay (701365; Inova Diagnostics, Werfen) using the BIO-FLASH system (Inova Diagnostics, Werfen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Results were interpreted using the suggested positive/negative cutoff.

Human platelet isolation and recombinant calprotectin. Venous blood samples from healthy donors were collected in anticoagulant (sodium citrate) tubes and centrifuged for 15 minutes at $200g$ . EDTA $(0.5\mathrm{mM})$ and prostaglandin $\mathsf{E}_1$ $(\mathsf{PGE}_1;1\mu \mathsf{M})$ were added to the isolated platelet-rich plasma and centrifuged at $700g$ for six minutes. Platelet pellets were then washed with Tyrode's buffer (HEPES [20 mM], NaCl [135 mM], $\mathsf{Na}_2\mathsf{HPO}_4$ [0.34 mM], KCl [2.9 mM], $\mathsf{NaHCO}_3$ [12 mM], $\mathsf{MgCl}_2$ [1 mM], and glucose [5 mM]; pH 6.5) in the presence of EDTA and $\mathsf{PGE}_1$ . After centrifugation, the supernatant was aspirated, and platelet pellets were resuspended in Tyrode's buffer of pH 7.35 with $0.3\%$ bovine serum albumin. Platelet count was determined using an automated cell counter (DeNovix-CellDrop) and was adjusted to $1\times 10^8$ cells/mL with Tyrode's buffer (pH 7.35). All steps were performed at $37^{\circ}\mathbf{C}$ . Recombinant calprotectin used in all the experiments was purchased from Bioligand (Bioligand, no. 753406). According to the manufacturer, it is greater than $95\%$ pure for S100A8/A9 heterodimer and devoid of any S100A9 homodimer or endotoxin contamination.

Platelet viability assay. Using calcein AM. Platelets $(1 \times 10^{8}$ cells/mL) were treated with various concentrations of recombinant calprotectin (5-25 μg/mL) for two hours at $37^{\circ}C$ . In some experiments, platelets were treated with inhibitors, such as paquinimod (5-20 μM), CU-CPT22 (0.5-5 μM), and MCC-950 (1-10 μM), for 15 minutes before treatment with either calprotectin (10 μg/mL) or 100 μg/mL of control and APS IgG. To check platelet viability in the presence of patient serum samples, platelets were incubated with 5% serum samples of 12 healthy controls, 12 patients with APS who had low (0-2 μg/mL) calprotectin levels, and 12 patients with APS who had high calprotectin (2-10 μg/mL) levels at $37^{\circ}C$ for one hour. In other experiments, either purified recombinant human S100A8/A9 heterodimer or serum samples from patients with APS who had high calprotectin (2-10 μg/mL) levels were treated with a 1:1 ratio of 27E10 (AB17050, Abcam)/isotype control IgG (AB170190, Abcam) for one hour before being treated with purified healthy platelets. After the serum incubation, platelets were incubated with calcein AM (0.5 μM) for 15 minutes, and cells were analyzed in a BioRad-ZE5 flow cytometer analyzer.

<!-- page 3 -->
Using annexin V fluorescein isothiocyanate. The levels of phosphatidyl serine externalization on plasma membrane were measured using an annexin V fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) or a PE annexin V apoptosis detection kit (BD Biosciences). After the initial incubation, annexin V-binding buffer was added to the treated platelets. Then the platelets were incubated with annexin V-FITC, gently mixed, and again set in the dark for 15 minutes at $37^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ . Excess dye was removed by centrifugation, and the platelets were resuspended in an annexin V-binding buffer and analyzed by a BioRad-ZE5 flow cytometer analyzer.

# Evaluation of platelet-derived microparticle generation.

PE-conjugated CD61 stained platelets $(1 \times 10^{8} / \mathrm{mL})$ were pretreated with paquinimod $(20\mu M)$ and MCC-950 $(10~\mu M)$ for 15 minutes and then were treated with calprotectin $(10~\mu g / mL)$ for two hours. The upper and outer limits of the microparticles gate were established just below the size distribution of the platelet population in a forward and side scatter setting (log scale) using the autogate function in the FlowJo software. The percentage of CD61-positive events within the microparticle gating was analyzed and presented as a percentage of platelet-derived microparticle (PDMP) generation.

Estimation of caspase-1 activity by luminescence assay. A Caspase-Glo 1 inflammasome assay kit (Promega) was used to measure the caspase-1 activation in platelets $(1 \times 10^{8}$ cells/mL) treated with paquinimod $(20\mu M)$ and MCC-950 $(10\mu M)$ for 15 minutes before treatment with either calprotectin $(10\mu g / mL)$ or $100~\mu \mathrm{g} / \mathrm{mL}$ of control and APS IgG for two hours at $37^{\circ}C.$ In another experiment, platelets pretreated for 15 minutes with or without paquinimod $(20\mu M)$ , and MCC-950 $(10\mu M)$ were incubated with $5 \%$ serum samples of 12 healthy controls or 12 patients with APS who had low $(0 - 2\mu g / mL)$ and high calprotectin $(2 - 10\mu g / mL)$ levels for one hour at $37^{\circ}C.$ After incubation, the platelets were processed, and the caspase-1 activity was measured as described by the manufacturer.

Statistical analysis. Normally distributed data were analyzed by two-sided $t$ -tests, and skewed data were analyzed by the Mann-Whitney $U$ test. Comparisons of more than two groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance with correction for multiple comparisons by Dunnett's method. Correlations were tested by using Spearman's partial correlation coefficient adjusted for age and sex. Univariable logistic regression models adjusted for age and sex were used to determine the associations between a positive calprotectin level and various extra-criteria APS manifestations among patients with primary APS or positive aPL without criteria APS manifestations. Data analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism software version 8 or SAS statistical software version 9.4M6 (SAS Institute, Inc). Statistical significance was defined as $P < 0.05$ unless stated otherwise.

# RESULTS

Circulating calprotectin levels in patients with primary APS and aPL-positive patients. Using a chemiluminescence-based assay, we measured circulating calprotectin levels in 112 patients with primary APS, 30 patients with positive aPL but without criteria APS manifestations or lupus, 38 patients with SLE without APS, 11 patients with a history of unprovoked venous thrombosis without aPL, 5 patients with ITP without aPL, and 18 healthy controls. The clinical characteristics of these patients are described in Supplementary Table 1. Calprotectin levels were higher in patients with primary APS and aPL-positive patients compared to healthy controls. Elevated levels were also seen in patients with SLE without APS (Figure 1A). After adjustment for age and sex, high calprotectin levels correlated with absolute neutrophil count ( $r = 0.41$ , $P < 0.001$ , $n = 90$ ) and C-reactive protein level ( $r = 0.34$ , $P = 0.002$ , $n = 89$ ) among patients with primary APS and aPL-positive patients. Interestingly, a significant negative correlation was found between circulating calprotectin level and platelet count ( $r = -0.24$ , $P = 0.004$ , $n = 137$ ). This negative correlation persisted when we only considered patients with primary APS ( $r = -0.21$ , $P = 0.027$ , $n = 110$ ). Circulating calprotectin did not correlate with decreased platelet count among patients with SLE without aPL ( $r = -0.11$ , $P = 0.2$ , $n = 33$ ). When we assessed its association with aPL, circulating calprotectin correlated with the IgG isotypes of anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI ( $r = 0.34$ , $P < 0.001$ , $n = 142$ ) and aCL ( $r = 0.37$ , $P < 0.001$ , $n = 132$ ) but not with the IgM isotypes of anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI ( $r = -0.02$ , $P = 0.778$ , $n = 142$ ) and aCL ( $r = -0.10$ , $P = 0.26$ , $n = 132$ ). We evaluated whether the presence of an elevated calprotectin level (as defined by $>2 \mu g/mL$ ) would add to traditional aPL in the risk stratification of aPL-positive patients. We found that an elevated calprotectin level was significantly associated with thrombocytopenia after adjustment for age and sex (odds ratio 4.51 [95% confidence interval 1.44–14.11]) (Figure 1B).

The impact of calprotectin on platelet viability via activation of the TLR4-inflammasome axis. We evaluated the potential role of calprotectin in platelet viability by using calcein AM dye and annexin V-based assays, which quantify platelet survival based on cellular esterase activity and the levels of phosphatidylserine externalization, respectively. We found that recombinant calprotectin decreased platelet viability in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 2A and B and Supplementary Figure 1A). This effect on viability was attenuated by 27E10 monoclonal antibody, a specific inhibitor of the calprotectin or S100A8/A9 heterodimer (Supplementary Figure 1B-C). # DISCUSSION

In this study, we found high levels of circulating calprotectin in patients with primary APS and aPL-positive patients. Clinically, calprotectin levels correlated with increased absolute neutrophil count, increased C-reactive protein levels, and decreased platelet count. In addition, elevated calprotectin levels were able to identify patients with extra-criteria manifestations, such as thrombocytopenia. Mechanistically, calprotectin potentiated aPL-mediated thrombocytopenia by engaging platelet surface TLR4 and activating the NLRP3-inflammasome, thereby inducing caspase-1-dependent platelet nonviability.

Calprotectin constitutes up to $60\%$ of neutrophil cytosolic protein and has been found to be a main component of NETs. $^{13}$ During neutrophil activation, calprotectin can be released extracellularly, where it acts as a danger-associated molecular pattern by activating TLR4 and RAGE, inducing the expression of proinflammatory cytokines, promoting leukocyte adhesion to the endothelium, and exerting its intrinsic cytotoxic properties on target cells. $^{14,15}$ As a functional biomarker of NETs and inflammation, the clinical utility of calprotectin has been evaluated with hints of usefulness in a variety of different rheumatic diseases. $^{14,15}$ For example, a high circulating calprotectin level is associated with active rheumatoid arthritis and can predict disease relapse in SLE. $^{14}$ Here, we have now demonstrated the presence of elevated calprotectin levels in both classifiable patients with APS and aPL-positive patients who do not have classifiable APS features. Our study suggests that circulating calprotectin not only hints at the untreated inflammatory burden of some patients with APS and aPL-positive patients but may also help identify patients at risk for developing APS extra-criteria manifestations, such as thrombocytopenia.

In APS, thrombocytopenia is a common extra-criteria manifestation. Despite its high prevalence, there is no consensus on the pathophysiology of APS thrombocytopenia, possibly because of the interplay of complex overlapping mechanisms. Such mechanisms include the presence of antibodies targeting platelet membrane glycoproteins, such as those seen in immune thrombocytopenia; the direct action of aPL against platelets resulting in destruction and consumption; and for a subgroup of patients with severe APS, the consequence of thrombotic microangiopathy.3 Interestingly, a recent study demonstrated that neutrophil-derived calprotectin induces TLR4-mediated platelet pyroptosis in models of sepsis.12 Here, we found that calprotectin activated TLR4 and the inflammasome axis, resulting in reduced platelet viability. This mechanism is likely another key player in the pathophysiology of APS thrombocytopenia. Furthermore, because paquinimod effectively restored viability of calprotectin- and aPL-treated platelets, targeting calprotectin-mediated TLR activation is a potential intervention for APS thrombocytopenia that warrants further evaluation.

In the general population, a low platelet count is often associated with increased bleeding risk. Recent research in APS suggests that thrombocytopenia is paradoxically associated with a two-fold increased risk of future thrombosis. What factors drive this increased thrombosis risk have yet to be evaluated. Lately, increasing attention has been paid to a subset of activated platelets with procoagulant properties driven by the exposure of anionic phosphatidylserine on the activated platelet surface where it interacts with various coagulation factors to promote thrombin formation. Such activated procoagulant platelets can also release phosphatidylserine-positive microparticles, which are a known risk factor for venous thrombosis. Here, we found that, at least in vitro, calprotectin increased phosphatidylserine externalization on the platelet surface and promoted platelet microparticle generation. Our findings provide new insights into the mechanisms that may drive the prothrombotic risk of APS patients with thrombocytopenia.

Although we speculate that aPL-mediated NETosis is a major source of circulating calprotectin in APS, we recognize that, as danger-associated molecules, S100A8, S100A9, and calprotectin could also be derived from a variety of other cells, including platelets. Indeed, platelets from patients with acute coronary syndrome revealed increased surface S100A9, which might predict myocardial infarction.[19] Furthermore, a recent study demonstrated that activated platelets from patients with SLE can express surface calprotectin and that high levels of circulating calprotectin were associated with cardiovascular complications among those patients with SLE.[20] The relationship between platelet-derived calprotectin and APS thrombocytopenia warrants further evaluation.

In summary, our data suggest a fascinating synergy between calprotectin and aPL, wherein their combined action appears to contribute to decreased platelet viability. Although the mechanisms underlying this collaboration warrant further investigation, the emerging understanding of their synergistic effects could open new avenues for therapeutic interventions targeting these molecular pathways for APS thrombocytopenia. Circulating calprotectin has the potential to be a mechanism-informed biomarker for APS thrombocytopenia.
