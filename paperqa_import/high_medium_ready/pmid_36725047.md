## Abstract

We experienced a 36-year-old man with lupus nephritis and antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) who received a donor kidney from his father. Twenty-two months after transplantation, at a time of poor adherence to immunosuppressants and warfarin, the patient developed sudden graft loss due to hemolytic uremic syndrome with rapid deterioration of renal function, thrombocytopenia, and hemolytic anemia. A kidney biopsy showed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) related to platelet thrombus formation; however, there was no recurrence of lupus and no findings suggestive of post-transplant rejection, so acute TMA associated with APS was thought to be the cause of the graft loss. This case highlights the importance of instructing patients with lupus nephritis to adhere to treatment with warfarin, a therapeutic drug for APS.

## Introduction

Recent advances in immunosuppressive agents have improved the post-transplant renal prognosis in patients with proliferative lupus nephritis (LN) who undergo renal transplantation to treat end-stage renal failure ( 1 ). However, patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and concomitant antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) have a high risk of early graft loss after renal transplantation.

The renal histology of APS is known to include chronic lesions due to fibrous thickening of the interlobular arteries and acute lesions of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), but only a few cases of acute TMA lesions as a cause of graft loss after renal transplantation in patients with SLE have been reported ( 2 , 3 ). The efficacy of anticoagulation therapy with prophylactic warfarin or low-molecular-weight heparin has not been established ( 4 ).

We herein report a patient with acute TMA lesions due to APS who experienced a rapid post-transplant decline in the renal function because of poor compliance with medications, including warfarin.

## Case Report

A 36-year-old Japanese man was admitted to our institute for the evaluation of rapid renal dysfunction.

At 13 years old, the patient had developed a fever and generalized erythema. SLE had been diagnosed, and prednisolone had been started at a dose of 30 mg. At 14 years old, proteinuria had appeared, and LN class III (World Health Organization classification) had been diagnosed by a kidney biopsy. At 27 years old, the platelet count had dropped to 100,000 /μL, and tests for antiphospholipid antibodies, including anti-cardiolipin antibody, had been positive. At 28 years old, proteinuria had increased to 7.3 g/day, ds-DNA antibodies had increased to 22.0 U/mL (normal level, ＜10 U/mL), and complement with CH50 had decreased to 26 U/mL, so a second kidney biopsy had been performed. LN class IV-G with active and chronic lesions (International Society of Nephrology/Renal Pathology Society classification) was diagnosed at that time ( Fig. 1 ) based on endocapillary, extracapillary, and mesangiocapillary proliferation on light microscopy ( Fig. 1a ); positive findings of a full-house pattern of immunoglobulin G (IgG; Fig. 1b ), IgA ( Fig. 1c ), IgM, C1q ( Fig. 1d ), C3 ( Fig. 1e ), C4 ( Fig. 1f ), and kappa and lambda in immunofluorescence microscopy; and the presence of electron-dense deposits in the subendothelial, subepithelial, and mesangial areas in electron microscopy ( Fig. 1h ). However, the LN in this patient differed from active LN because of changes related to long-term steroid treatment. Arteriolar hyalinosis was noted ( Fig. 1g ), but thrombosis was not detected. Steroid pulse therapy was started with methylprednisolone (0.5 g/day) for 3 days, the prednisolone dose was increased from 5 mg to 30 mg, and 4 doses of intravenous cyclophosphamide therapy were administered at a dose of 350 mg/day. Thereafter, proteinuria decreased, but the renal function deteriorated, and the platelet count gradually declined. At 35 years old, the patient suffered a cerebral infarction, and hemodialysis was started.

The patient requested transplant of a donor kidney from his father, but his platelet count was below 50,000 /μL. Therefore, he was treated by steroid pulse therapy with methylprednisolone (0.5 g/day) for 3 days, tacrolimus (3 mg/day), and mycophenolate mofetil (MMF; 2,000 mg/day). Splenectomy was performed, but the platelet count did not increase. One year later, when concomitant treatment with warfarin was started and the prothrombin time international normalized ratio (INR) was maintained at 2.0 to 3.0, the platelet count rose to 200,000 /μL, and renal transplantation was performed.

The patient's blood type was O/Rh(+), and his human leukocyte antigen (HLA) typing was HLA-A (A24, A26), HLA-B (B35, B39), and HLA-DR (DR15, DR-). The blood type of the donor (his father) was O/Rh(+), and the HLA typing was HLA-A (A2, A26), HLA-B (B39, B67), and HLA-DR (DR4, DR15).

Pre-transplant plasma exchange was not performed because the transplant was blood-group-matched between a parent and child. A one-hour protocol transplant kidney biopsy showed mild acute tubular injury but no evidence of native glomerulonephritis or acute rejection.

For 1 year and 10 months after transplantation, the INR was maintained at 2.0 to 3.0 by treatment with warfarin (2 to 3 mg), and the renal function was maintained (serum creatinine concentration, 1.8 mg/dL) by treatment with prednisolone (5 mg), tacrolimus (3 mg/day), and MMF (1,500 mg/day). However, in the 3 months before admission, the patient repeatedly failed to attend his scheduled outpatient visits. Because the amount of drug prescribed was sufficient only for the number of days until the next outpatient visit, the patient was assumed to have had an inadequate medication intake. At the same time, his compliance with drug treatment decreased, so the INR decreased to 1.3, the serum creatinine level worsened from 1.8 to 2.8 mg/dL, and the patient was admitted urgently to our hospital.

At the current admission, the patient was 168 cm tall and weighed 58 kg. His blood pressure was 135/80 mmHg, and his body temperature was 37.6°C. Edema was observed in the lower legs, but no abnormalities of the heart, lungs, or abdomen were noted. The laboratory findings were as follows ( Table ): serum creatinine, 2.8 mg/dL; platelet count, 200,000 /μL; hemoglobin, 7.1 g/dL; haptoglobin, 150 mg/dL; INR, 1.9 (i.e. increased); lupus anticoagulant, 1.76 (normal value, ＜1.2); and β2-glycoprotein I-dependent anticardiolipin antibody, 16.4 U/mL (normal value, ＜3.5 U/mL). Tests for anti-ds-DNA and anti-Sm antibodies were negative, but the anti-nuclear antibody titer was 1:40 (normal value, 1:＜40). Total complement activity (assessed as CH50) was 35 U/mL (normal value, ＞30 U/mL); complement 3, 91 mg/dL (reference range, 86-160 mg/dL); and complement 4, 19 mg/dL (reference range, 17-45 mg/dL). The level of a disintegrin-like and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin type 1 motifs 13 (ADAMTS13) was 50.8%, and that of the ADAMTS13 inhibitor was less than 0.5 Bodansky unit/mL. Urinary protein excretion was 0.22 g/gCr, and red blood cell sediment was ＜1/high-power field.

Four days after the patient was switched from warfarin to heparin, the serum creatinine value worsened to 5.6 mg/dL, and the platelet count and haptoglobin level decreased to 36,000 /μL and 20 mg/dL, respectively. Therefore, a kidney biopsy was performed.

### Kidney biopsy and immunohistological examination findings

Light microscopy showed global sclerosis in 2 out of 13 glomeruli. Approximately 30% of the tubulointerstitium in the cortical area was affected by chronic fibrosis and tubular atrophy, and acute tubular injury findings, such as tubular epithelial swelling and vacuolar degeneration, were also seen in the preserved tubular region. In one glomerulus, thrombus formation with fibrin deposition was present in the vessels from the afferent arteriole to the vascular pole (preglomerular arteriole), and the peripheral glomerulus was collapsed ( Fig. 2a-c ). In the glomerulus without visible thrombus formation, endothelial cell swelling with prominent mesangiolysis close to the vascular pole had caused proximal glomerular capillary occlusion, resulting in collapse of the peripheral glomerulus ( Fig. 2d ). In the other glomeruli, endothelial cell damage with mesangiolysis had caused dilation of the glomerular lumen and doubling and wrinkling of the glomerular basement membrane ( Fig. 2e ).

An immunohistological examination showed anti-CD61 antibody staining-positive platelet and megakaryocyte infiltration in the affected glomerular area ( Fig. 2f ). Phosphotungstic acid hematoxylin (PTAH) staining, which indicates fibrin deposition, was negative. Immunofluorescence microscopy showed no staining for IgG, IgA, IgM, C3, C4, C4d, or C1q. Electron microscopy showed thrombosis containing numerous platelets ( Fig. 2g ) but without fibrin-like material or endothelial cell swelling and subendothelial edema with mesangiolysis ( Fig. 2h ). Fibroelastic intimal thickening (hyperplasia) of the interlobular arteries, which is considered a typical chronic lesion in APS, was absent. Fibroelastosis of the interlobular arteries ( Fig. 2i ) was present. The Banff classification was i0, t0, v0, g0, ptc0, ti0, i-IFTA0, t-IFTA0, ci2, ct2, cg3, cv2, ptcml0, and pvl0 ( 5 ).

Although marked endothelial cell swelling with prominent mesangiolysis was noted, tests for blood-type match, histologic criteria (microvascular inflammation and tubulitis), immunohistologic criteria (C4d staining in peritubular capillaries), and serum donor-specific anti-HLA antibody were negative. Antibody-mediated and T-cell-mediated rejection were deemed unlikely, and there was no recurrence of lupus. Consequently, TMA with platelet thrombus formation was diagnosed, and TMA associated with APS was considered to have caused the rapid graft loss.

### Clinical course

Hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) associated with acute TMA and APS nephropathy was diagnosed, along with rapid deterioration of the renal function, thrombocytopenia, and hemolytic anemia. The patient was treated with intensified warfarin anticoagulation therapy, 4 sessions of plasma exchange therapy with 30 units of fresh-frozen plasma per session, and steroid pulse therapy with methylprednisolone (1 g/day) for 3 days; in addition, treatment was changed from tacrolimus to cyclosporine because tacrolimus was also considered a cause of the HUS. The platelet count rose to 200,000 /μL, but the renal function did not improve, and hemodialysis was restarted ( Fig. 3 ).

## Discussion

We experienced a case of HUS that developed after renal transplantation in a patient with a 23-year history of SLE. Because we hypothesized that acute TMA associated with APS was involved in the pathogenesis of this syndrome, we reviewed previous publications on this subject.

Stone et al. studied renal outcomes after renal transplantation in 96 patients with SLE and found that among the 15 patients with APS, 10 (10.4%) either died of or had an APS-related clinical event within 3 months of transplantation, with details as follows: thrombotic arteriolar microangiopathy, 2 patients; stroke, 4 patients; ocular ischemia, 7 patients; deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism, 6 patients; renal artery or vein thrombosis, 4 patients; peripheral ischemia, 1 patient; and fetal wastage, 3 patients. The authors did not present detailed renal biopsy results showing acute TMA ( 6 ).

Ducloux et al. studied 178 renal transplant recipients without SLE and reported that 28.1% of them had positive tests for anticardiolipin antibodies and lupus anticoagulant and that these patients had a shorter transplant period and greater frequency of venous thrombosis than those with negative tests for anticardiolipin antibodies and lupus anticoagulant ( 7 ). Gauthier et al. studied 247 renal transplant patients who underwent antiphospholipid antibody tests, including tests for anticardiolipin antibodies and lupus anticoagulant, and reported that patients with positive results had a significantly lower estimated glomerular filtration rate at 1 year after transplant than those with negative results ( 8 ). Canaud et al. studied 37 patients with a positive lupus anticoagulant test and reported that early thrombosis of graft vessels and deep venous thrombosis occurred more frequently in these patients than those with a negative lupus anticoagulant test and that 4 patients died early after transplantation ( 9 ). Morales et al. reported that a positive IgA anti-β2-glycoprotein-1 antibody test is the main independent risk factor for graft thrombosis and early graft loss after renal transplantation ( 10 ). Taken together, these reports indicate that the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies is an important risk factor for graft loss.

Appel et al. and D'Agati et al. summarized vascular lesions in LN ( 11 , 12 ). They reported that chronic thrombotic occlusion of the renal arterioles is the main renal histology in SLE with APS that presents with HUS/thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura and that acute lesions with TMA are rare. Direct damage to the vascular endothelium by APS or endothelial cell damage related to thrombus formation in APS is thought to be the mechanism by which APS affects the kidneys ( 11 - 14 ).

The clinical course of the patient described in the present report suggests that the first half of the disease was caused by LN but that the pathogenesis leading to end-stage renal failure was more likely related to APS than LN. With the advent of immunosuppressive drugs, such as MMF, tacrolimus, and cyclosporin, the success rate of renal transplantation in dialysis patients with LN has improved, and because these drugs are also successful in treating LN, recurrence of LN is rarely seen. However, we learned from this case that APS, a common complication of LN, does not respond to immunosuppressive drugs and is a risk factor for rejection of transplanted kidneys. Our experience highlights the importance of instructing patients with LN to adhere to treatment with warfarin, a therapeutic drug for APS.

Reports have described a condition in which patients with APS antibody rapidly develop thrombocytopenia, hemolytic anemia, and organ involvement due to acute TMA; the name catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome has been proposed for this disease ( 15 ). In the present case, in addition to HUS, which is considered to be a case of catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome, histological examinations showed TMA lesions in the kidney. Although various combinations of anticoagulant therapy, including warfarin, immunosuppression, plasma exchange, and intravenous immunoglobulins, have been reported to improve the prognosis, some cases show no response. In the present case, thrombocytopenia and hemolytic anemia improved with the above treatment, and the prognosis was good; however, the transplanted kidney did not recover. We speculate that the renal function responds to early intervention but that once a certain threshold of renal dysfunction is exceeded, thrombocytopenia improves, while the renal function does not. Failure of anticoagulant therapy for APS is associated with renal allograft failure and is reported as the cause of graft loss after renal transplantation in patients with APS.

The normal thrombus formation process begins with platelet thrombus formation in the acute phase, followed by fibrin thrombus formation in the chronic phase. In the present case, PTAH staining, which indicates fibrin deposition, was negative, whereas platelet staining was positive, suggesting that thrombus formation was in the acute phase ( 14 ).

We speculate that APS nephropathy occurred first because the threshold for developing APS nephropathy is lower than that for transplant rejection, which explains why the transplant was not rejected despite the poor adherence to immunosuppressive drugs.

Thrombocytopenia is a hallmark of patients with APS, but a platelet count below 50,000 /μL is rare. Based on the present findings, a rapid drop in platelet count to below 50,000 /μL and the presence of HUS, another characteristic of APS, should be considered atypical ( 16 ).

The development of APS nephropathy suggests that the prognosis of transplanted kidneys is poor, even when TMA symptoms improve. This may be because transplanted kidneys are more susceptible to endothelial cell damage than native kidney; for example, there have been reports of a poor renal prognosis of APS nephropathy in ABO-incompatible cases of renal transplantation. Alternatively, warfarin alone may not be sufficient to dissolve microthrombi in the glomerulus, resulting in progressive renal failure even when platelet counts increase.

During the period of renal failure before renal transplantation, the patient was diagnosed with chronic LN because of serologically positive findings of decreased complement and ds-DNA antibodies, as well as mild immune deposits on a kidney biopsy. After renal transplantation, there was no serological evidence of decreased complement or positive findings of ds-DNA antibodies, suggesting that APS was involved in the rapid decline in the renal function. However, it should be noted that some reports on patients with LN have described vascular lesions with TMA that did not involve immune deposits ( 11 , 13 ).

In conclusion, we experienced a 36-year-old man who underwent renal transplantation with a donor kidney from his father after 23 years of treatment for SLE and then hemodialysis. Twenty-two months after transplantation, nonadherence to treatment resulted in rapid graft loss, which was found to be due to acute TMA related to APS and not to recurrent LN or graft rejection. Subsequently, the patient was treated with a steady course of medications, and the platelet count normalized; however, his renal function did not improve.
