# Abstract

Lupus anticoagulant (LAC) is a well-known laboratory test used to explore potential reasons for the prolongation of phospholipid-dependent coagulation tests. An extended clotting time in a coagulation test typically suggests a bleeding tendency, as the plasma takes longer to clot. However, a positive LAC result, defined as normalization of prolonged clotting time by adding anionic phospholipids in the system, does not necessarily imply this. In fact, quite the opposite is true: a positive LAC often strongly correlates with an increased risk of thromboembolic events. Therefore, despite being conceptually counterintuitive, LAC remains extremely valuable in routine clinical practice for identifying individuals at risk for thromboembolic events. Over the years, various factors have been recognized as potential inducers of LAC, with antiphospholipid antibodies associated with antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) playing a significant role. Today, research indicates that, among antiphospholipid antibodies, those targeting plasma proteins $\beta_{2}$ -glycoprotein I and prothrombin are central to LAC. This article offers a historical perspective on LAC, emphasizing recent developments in antiprothrombin antibodies, their connection to LAC, and novel detection methods. Our premise is that a deeper understanding of how antiprothrombin antibodies contribute to LAC and the identification of subpopulations of these antibodies potentially responsible for it in thrombotic APS patients could lead to transformative advancements, offering new strategies for risk stratification and personalized treatments for patients with APS and beyond.

# 1 | INTRODUCTION

It was the late 1970s when, as a resident in internal medicine at the University of Padova, I came across a middle-aged woman with multiple recurrent thrombotic events and a prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time. Puzzled, my tutor surprised me by saying, "This is a lupus

anticoagulant (LAC)." On that day, I realized that thromboembolic events can be paradoxically associated with an "in vitro" anticoagulant phenomenon. That very day also marked the beginning of a long scientific journey, shared with many colleagues, to unravel this complex phenomenon, which is vital for clinical decisions yet still deeply mysterious.

# 2 | ANTI- $\beta_{2}$ -GLYCOPROTEIN I ANTIBODIES AND TRIPLE POSITIVITY

The term "lupus anticoagulant" was first introduced in 1972 by Feinstein and Rapaport [1] to describe an acquired inhibitor of blood coagulation in the plasma of patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (Figure 1). At that time, it was known that the prolonged clotting times in these patients could be corrected by incubating their plasma with anionic phospholipids (PLs) but not through mixing studies. However, the inhibitor's source was unknown. Because LAC was noted in all PL-dependent coagulation tests and because of the positive correlation between the presence of LAC and reactivity against cardiolipin (CL) measured by solid-phase immunoassays [2], most research in the field gravitated toward antibodies against anionic PLs, especially CL. It was the early days of antiphospholipid antibody research.

Chasing the idea that antibodies directed to PLs may be responsible for LAC, in the mid-1980s, we successfully used liposomes containing CL to purify immunoglobulin (Ig) G with LAC activity from citrated plasma [3]. This showed that IgG isolated using an anionic lipid, specifically anti-CL antibodies, can cause LAC. However, we also stumbled across the unexpected observation that LAC-positive IgG could not be eluted from the liposomes if the patient's total IgG was used instead of plasma. This implied that a substance in the plasma was needed to bridge the IgG to the liposomes. Soon after, in 1990, 3 groups showed that most anti-CL antibodies are not directed to CL but rather a plasma protein binding to anionic PLs [4-6]. This protein

was identified as $\beta_{2}$ -glycoprotein-I ( $\beta_{2}$ GPI). This discovery marked a turning point in antiphospholipid antibody research. Along with anti-CL, antibodies targeting $\beta_{2}$ GPI (anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies) soon became a hallmark of antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) and were included in the classification criteria in 2006 [7].

Since these early discoveries, our efforts and those of many others have led to several advancements in the diagnosis and management of APS patients. Most notably, we contributed to the introduction and validation of a simple and effective stratification scheme based on test positivity, along with a corresponding clinical management plan. Specifically, in 2005, we reported that positivity in LAC, anti-CL antibodies, and anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies, which we defined as triple positivity, was significantly associated with thromboembolic events [8]. We also discovered that the risk progressively decreases in patients who test positive for 2 and 1 of these markers, referred to as double and single positivity, respectively. Clinically, we observed that unlike double- and single-positivity, triple-positive patients do not respond well to direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs); therefore, it is recommended that these patients be treated with vitamin K antagonists instead of DOACs [9].

In addition to providing clinical guidance, these findings started to reveal the complex nature of antiphospholipid antibodies and suggested potential new mechanisms linking antiphospholipid antibodies to LAC. For example, the existence of single- and double-positive APS profiles indicated that anti-CL and anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies are not identical, potentially operating via different mechanisms in relation to

It is now accepted that anti-CL and $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies can differ significantly [10,11], although distinguishing between them remains challenging as today's tests tend to obscure their differences. Furthermore, it was found that not all $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies are the same. In fact, within anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies, those targeting the N-terminal domain of the molecule, called domain I (DI), often cause LAC when spiked in healthy plasma [12]. This is unlike anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies targeting other domains, such as domain IV and domain V [13]. Importantly, anti-DI antibodies are frequently observed in APS patients who have a history of thrombosis [12,14], and we found them especially in triple-positive patients [15]. Furthermore, they are pathogenic when injected into animals [16], though how these results translate to humans remains to be clarified. Consequently, in recent years, anti-DI antibodies have frequently been viewed as the primary cause of LAC in APS patients at high risk of thrombosis. However, it is important to remember that a recent study found that LAC positivity remains significantly correlated with thrombosis and pregnancy morbidity even after excluding anti-DI-positive patients [17]. This demonstrates that (1) anti-DI antibodies, while clearly significant for APS pathogenesis, are only a part of the pathogenic pool of antiphospholipid antibodies, and (2) anti-DI antibodies are not the only contributors to LAC; anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies targeting domains other than DI as well as antiphospholipid antibodies with other specificity, might play a role.

# 3 | ANTIPROTHROMBIN ANTIBODIES AND TETRA POSITIVITY

Though a key antigen of antiphospholipid antibodies, $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ is not the only one. More than 50 years ago, pioneering studies led by the groups of Loeliger [18] and Rapaport et al. [19], followed by Bevers et al. [20] and Horbach et al. [21], among others, identified prothrombin (PT) as a potential antigen of antiphospholipid antibodies. In these early studies, it was proposed that antibodies directed against PT could be responsible for LAC [20-22]. Furthermore, antibodies causing LAC were obtained by absorbing plasma into "insolubilized" PT [22]. Yet, despite these encouraging results, several issues remained, dampening clinical interest. First, the detection rate of antiprothrombin antibodies was too low, as antiprothrombin antibodies were found in only about half of the patients with APS [23]. Second, there was no correlation between antiprothrombin antibodies and thrombotic events [24], and the levels of antiprothrombin antibodies failed to correlate positively with LAC [20-22]. Finally, though autoantibody binding to PT, the precursor of the clotting protease thrombin would predict reduced levels of this coagulation factor and a bleeding diathesis; most patients with antiprothrombin antibodies do not show substantial hypoprothrombinemia nor hemorrhagic manifestations, raising questions about the robustness and specificity of the detection methods.

Despite these concerns, research on antiprothrombin antibodies continued. PT is a vitamin K-dependent plasma protein [25]. Similar

to $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ , PT binds to anionic PLs, most notably, phosphatidylserine (PS) [25]. However, unlike $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ , PT requires calcium to bind PS, as this interaction is mediated by 10 gamma-carboxyglutamic acid residues (Gla) located in the N-terminal Gla-domain of PT. In 1994, Permpikul et al. [26] reported that the binding of IgG preparations with LAC to immobilized PS was enhanced when PT was mixed with calcium ions. Building on this evidence, Atsumi et al. [27], in the early 2000s, developed and deployed a new test in which PT was bound to PS rather than immobilized to plastic plates as done before. Quite remarkably, antiprothrombin antibodies detected with this new enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay test, named hereafter antiphosphatidylserine (aPS)/PT antibodies, not only positively correlated with thrombosis but were frequently found in patients with LAC [27]. This indicated, for the first time that aPS/PT antibodies could serve as a suitable biomarker for identifying thrombotic APS patients and that aPS/PT antibodies may be important contributors to LAC.

In follow-up studies, Sciascia et al. [28] independently verified, extended, and consolidated the clinical significance of aPS/PT antibodies, as we and others did. Specifically, in 2020, we first established that levels of aPS/PT antibodies are persistently high in triple-positive APS patients with a history of thrombosis [29], indicating that these high-risk patients, in addition to LAC, anti-CL, and anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI, almost always contain aPS/PT antibodies. Based on this evidence, we renamed these patients tetra-positive. We then provided evidence in 2023 for a strong positive correlation between LAC potency and aPS/PT antibody titers [30]. These findings led us to hypothesize a causative relationship between LAC and aPS/PT antibodies, which we confirmed using IgG aPS/PT antibodies isolated from a handful of APS patients [29]. This boosted our interest in their potential use in clinical practice.

# 4 | CLINICAL UTILITY OF DETECTING ANTIPROTHROMBIN ANTIBODIES

A significant challenge with LAC is that, despite its strong diagnostic value, determining it is not straightforward, as various preanalytical and analytical factors can influence the results [31,32]. Consequently, confidence in LAC results can be low, leading to uncertainty in diagnosis and treatment. For example, as part of a recent survey on LAC diagnosis, 302 plasma samples labeled as LAC-positive by specialized thrombosis centers were reevaluated in an APS reference laboratory. Out of these 302 samples, 71 (23%) were found to be false positives, resulting in misdiagnosis. Most errors were associated with (a) patients on oral anticoagulant treatment, (b) borderline LAC—defined as a test value slightly (5%-15%) higher than the cutoff for the specific reagent/analizer or provided by manufacturer, and (c) operator inexperience with this test [33]. Given the strong positive association between LAC potency and aPS/PT antibody titers, we, therefore, wondered whether detecting aPS/PT antibodies could serve as a surrogate for LAC, especially when its determination is unfeasible, unpractical, or the results are not so easily interpretable.

<!-- page 4 -->
The idea of using aPS/PT as a surrogate for LAC was investigated in a meta-analysis [34] and research involving tetra-positive APS patients [30]. The meta-analysis supported this idea quite well, revealing a combined prevalence of aPS/PT IgG/IgM in patients who tested positive for LAC at $84.6\%$ . Considering the previously mentioned false positive discovery rate of $23\%$ for LAC, the prevalence could be as high as $100\%$ . Likewise, we observed a strong positive correlation between the total IgG/IgM aPS/PT titers and LAC potency, suggesting a close relationship between these 2 variables in tetra-positive APS patients [30]. Finally, in contrast to a confirmed positive LAC, which is always positive for aPS/PT, double-positive aPL profiles with a negative LAC (ie, $\alpha \mathrm{CL}+$ , anti- $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}+$ , and LAC-) exhibit aPS/PT titers below the cutoff value [35]. Based on this evidence and several more published studies, a discussion was recently initiated among members of the APS research and medical community to address the question of whether testing for aPS/PT in patients with suspected APS would provide additional value to currently available testing in terms of diagnosis or risk stratification. For now, the consensus is NO if LAC is strong, as in these cases, LAC is unlikely to be affected by technical issues. However, we think it could be YES when LAC is uncertain or suspected to be false positive in tested plasma due to the presence of anticoagulants or other confounding agents, such as elevated levels of C-reactive protein typically seen in acute settings.

For example, a situation in which aPS/PT detection could be extremely important involves young patients presenting with confirmed or suspected unprovoked venous thromboembolism. Given that these patients are often treated promptly with anticoagulants in the emergency department, testing for LAC poses challenges. Although heparin neutralization reagents in coagulation tests effectively neutralize prophylactic doses of heparin, administering an intravenous bolus of heparin (5000-10 000 U) can result in peak heparin levels that exceed neutralization capacity, leading to false positive LAC results. In this situation, testing for aPS/PT antibodies in addition to traditional anti-CL and anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies could be highly beneficial. If all 3 tests are positive, long-term vitamin K antagonist treatment should be considered for secondary prophylaxis, even before confirming the tests' positivity after 12 weeks.

Another situation where testing aPS/PT might be highly beneficial is when DOACs are used for secondary prophylaxis. Neutralizing DOACs is advised to reduce their interference in LAC assays. However, measuring DOAC concentration should be performed in samples treated with activated carbon due to potential incomplete removal. Using activated carbon on samples containing vitamin K antagonists or heparin can result in misleading LAC results [36].

# 5 | LAC AND ANTIPROTHROMBIN ANTIBODIES: TYPE I AND TYPE II ANTIBODIES

In addition to its diagnostic appeal, the increasing recognition that antiprothrombin antibodies contribute to LAC has raised a variety of new mechanistic questions about their role in the pathogenesis of

APS. The observation that aPS/PT, but not antiprothrombin, detected by immobilizing PT into plastic plates strongly correlates with LAC, along with the fact that LAC is the most significant predictor of thrombosis in APS patients, serves as a solid starting point for research and suggests that there is considerable heterogeneity among antiprothrombin antibodies. One possibility is that some antiprothrombin antibodies may be more pathogenic than others. Under certain circumstances, some may even be protective.

In 2016, we discovered that PT adopts closed and open forms as it circulates in plasma [37] (Figure 2). Building on this conformational equilibrium and the idea of autoantibody heterogeneity, in 2019, we discovered the existence of 2 distinct subpopulations of IgG anti-prothrombin antibodies in triple-positive APS patients, which we called type I and type II [38]. We defined type I antibodies as those recognizing the open form of PT, whereas type II antibodies recognize both forms. Structural analyses suggested that type I antibodies are more likely to bind to regions in kringle-1 (aa. 65-143) and serine protease domains (aa. 285-579) that are hidden in the closed form but become more exposed in the open form. In contrast, type II antibodies were expected to interact with regions that remain accessible in both conformational states. In line with this premise, last year, we reported cryptic epitopes for the type I monoclonal antibodies, prothrombin open monoclonal antibody (POmAb) and 5B10, within kringle-1 and epitopes for the type II monoclonal antibody, anti-human prothrombin (AHP) monoclonal antibody, in kringle-2 (aa. 169-249) [39]. We have also identified epitopes for type II antibodies in the N-terminal region of PT (aa. 1-44) [38], but those remain less characterized. More recently, we have demonstrated that type I antibodies POmAb and 5B10 lead to a mild prolongation of clotting time that extra PLs could not completely reverse, signifying weak LAC activity [38-40]. In contrast, the type II antibody AHP resulted in a more significant prolongation of clotting time, which was entirely reversed upon the addition of extra PLs, indicative of strong LAC activity. For the first time, these studies highlighted functional differences between type I and type II antibodies, providing initial characterization of their divergent roles in LAC.

The identification of subpopulations of antiprothrombin antibodies presents an interesting parallel to anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies. Specifically, we have previously discussed how anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies targeting different domains may have distinct roles in APS, with anti-DI antibodies regarded as some of the most pathogenic. However, it is important to keep in mind that type I and type II antiprothrombin antibodies are defined not by the domain to which the antibody binds but by the specific conformational state of PT that they recognize. In fact, antibodies targeting kringle-1 could be of type I or type II; likewise, antibodies targeting kringle-2 could be of type I or type II. Moving forward, we thus recommend clearly defining the types of antibodies first (ie, type I or type II) and then the domain (or even better, epitope) to which they bind. For example, a type I/kringle-1 antibody signifies an antibody that binds to kringle-1 in a manner that favors the open conformation. In contrast, a type II/kringle-1 antibody binds to kringle-1 with minimal impact on the conformational equilibrium. We believe that this definition is essential to capture the complexity of antiprothrombin antibodies and ultimately

# 6 | A GLIMPSE INTO THE FUTURE

As we move forward, many questions remain. First, it will be important to further define the mechanisms underlying type I and type II activities in plasma and establish their exact roles in LAC. This will help us understand the complexities of LAC and interpret the results of this test more confidently. Several hypotheses have been proposed over the years, with the most notable ones advanced in the early 2000s by the groups of Bevers and De Groot [42] and more recently

revisited and expanded by the group of Urbanus [43]. The prevailing theory suggests that antiprothrombin antibodies induce LAC activity by competing with clotting factors for catalytic PL surfaces. However, it is likely that multiple mechanisms exist, with type I and type II antibodies operating through different pathways. This is because we showed that closed and open forms of PT are converted to thrombin using different pathways [25,39,44].

We shall also continue to delve deeper into antibodies' heterogeneity. It is possible that additional types of antiprothrombin antibodies exist, such as those recognizing epitopes present in the closed but not in the open form or those that show a strong preference for the closed form (Figure 2). These new antibodies may represent a novel functional class that could be called type III to distinguish it from existing type I and type II.

Exploring the synergy between antiprothrombin and anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies in promoting thrombophilia will be crucial for advancing the field. Current evidence indicates that both antiprothrombin and anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI autoantibodies can activate platelets, albeit through distinct mechanisms. For instance, anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI activates platelets via various pathways [45] but is mostly independent of the FcγRIIA receptor. Conversely, in one study, monoclonal antiprothrombin antibodies 3B1 and 28F4 with LAC activity (but not those without LAC activity) stimulated platelet aggregation in the presence of

<!-- page 6 -->
calcium by engaging the FcγRIIA receptor [46]. Furthermore, this study showed that reducing PT levels diminished antiprothrombin antibody-induced platelet aggregation [46]. Beyond their effects on platelets, both antiprothrombin and anti-β₂GPI autoantibodies might also activate endothelial cells and neutrophils, recruit complement, and cause activated protein C resistance [47]. A comprehensive understanding of the underlying mechanisms necessitates further investigation.

In addition to advancing basic knowledge, new insights from mechanistic studies underlying LAC could assist in designing strategies to neutralize pathogenic antiphospholipid antibodies and even develop innovative approaches to better regulate thrombin generation, thereby overcoming the limitations of current anticoagulants. For example, we recently showed that POmAb reduces but never abolishes thrombin generation because it acts via a noncompetitive mechanism with prothrombinase complex [39]. Reducing thrombin generation by manipulating PT conformation presents a novel mechanism that could lead to a safer approach to anticoagulation. The unique in vitro anticoagulant activity of POmAb also raises the provocative question of whether all antiprothrombin antibodies are pathogenic. Some, especially at low titers, might actually be protective.

While commercial availability has increased the accessibility and adoption of enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay aPS/PT tests, there remains significant room for improvement in the diagnostic arena. For example, aPS/PT tests suffer from platform-dependent variability due to the inherent instability of the reagents immobilized onto the plastic plate. In fact, PLs tend to oxidize when exposed to atmospheric oxygen [48], and PT can undergo autoproteolysis [49]. Additionally, the methods used to coat the wells with PS and PT may vary between tests, potentially affecting PT's conformational properties and complicating the interpretation and comparison of results across different platforms. Finally, like the anticardiolipin (aCL) assay, aPS/PT tests may not be specific to PT and could also detect other proteins that bind to PS.

To overcome some of the limitations, in 2021, we developed a new test called aPT-Bio [50]. Unlike the previous tests, aPT-Bio uses an engineered recombinant PT variant, ProTS525A-Biot, that is stably bound to avidin-coated plastic wells at a desired orientation and density. This design allows PT to retain its native structural configuration, which is critical for the identification of clinically relevant autoantibodies. It also avoids the detection of antibodies targeting PLs or other plasma proteins that bind to PS, reducing complexity in interpreting results. In our recent publication, we demonstrated that IgG aPT-Bio test displays a positive linear correlation with IgG aPS/PT test $(R^2 = .85)$ but a poor correlation with traditional IgG anti-prothrombin antibodies $(R^2 = .40)$ in which PT is directly immobilized onto plastic plates. This finding supports the current view that different subpopulations of antiprothrombin antibodies coexist in APS patients, and their detection depends on the platform used. Moreover, since IgG aPS/PT antibodies are frequently found in high-risk patients while traditional IgG antiprothrombin antibodies are not, our data indicate that IgG aPT-Bio antibodies may prove an effective and more cost-effective alternative to aPS/PT test for subphenotyping APS

patients. In fact, aPT-Bio is easy to prepare and perform, and plates can be stored for weeks at $4^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ , improving reproducibility. It also supports the use of plasma collected with a variety of anticoagulants, such as sodium citrate or EDTA, improving accessibility. Finally, the biotin tag allows for the immobilization of the antigen into magnetic beads and other surfaces of choice coated with neutravidin, which is ideal for multiplexing and other automated diagnostic formats that are likely to dominate the diagnostic landscape in the future [51].

In addition to being a promising alternative to the aPS/PT test, one of the most exciting directions of aPT-Bio is that it serves as a stepping stone toward developing next-generation tests to differentiate between antiprothrombin antibody subtypes, such as type I and type II. Given our biochemical data demonstrating the differing activity of type I and type II antibodies in LAC, these new tests are expected to be of high clinical significance and enhance our understanding of antiprothrombin antibodies, not just in APS but also in other diseases. For example, it has been long known that antiprothrombin antibodies are associated with LAC in the rare condition known as LAC/hypoprothrombinemia syndrome (LAHPS) [52]. In this situation, however, LAC is linked to bleeding rather than a thrombotic outcome. Early studies by Rapaport et al. [19] and Loeliger [18] proposed that bleeding in LAHPS is driven by very high levels of circulating antiprothrombin antibodies, which promote a rapid clearance of PT, lowering its plasma levels. Yet, it remains unclear how these antibodies bind to PT and how they clear PT from the circulation. Considering the high concentration of PT in plasma but its low levels in LAHPS, it is likely that most antiprothrombin antibodies in patients with LAHPS are of type II or type III. This is because type II or type III antibodies, unlike type I antibodies, can recognize the closed form, which predominates in blood. It is also possible that these antibodies have higher affinity compared with those found in thrombotic APS patients. In this context, it is interesting to note that in one case report of LAHPS, besides PT, another vitamin K-dependent coagulation factor (factor [F] IX) was markedly reduced [53]. Since PT and FIX share similarities in their Gla-domain, and type II antibodies targeting the Gla-domain of PT have been reported in APS patients [38], it is tempting to speculate that a possible mechanism by which PT levels are reduced in LAHPS is by type II antibodies targeting the Gla-domain.

Interestingly, cross-reactivity of type II antibodies targeting the Gla-domain of PT with other vitamin K-dependent coagulation factors could also explain some of the thrombotic manifestations in APS patients. For example, some reports describe an association between antiprothrombin antibodies and autoantibodies directed against protein C and protein S [54-57]. One could speculate that interference with these 2 key anticoagulant proteins could determine a thrombophilic state, as seen in APS patients.

# 7 | CONCLUSIONS

Strong clinical evidence exists of a tight association between LAC and antiprothrombin antibodies detected by the aPS/PT test. Biochemical and structural studies exploring the link between LAC and antiprothrombin antibodies are ongoing. Soon, clinical, biochemical, and

<!-- page 7 -->
structural evidence could provide a rationale for measuring anti-prothrombin antibodies with the aPS/PT test or other tests we are developing for all suspected APS patients and other disorders. Embracing these new technologies will likely increase our diagnostic confidence and unveil the complex set of molecular mechanisms contributing to LAC. Ultimately, these efforts might reveal new strategies for risk-stratifying and treating APS patients, advancing personalized medicine.
