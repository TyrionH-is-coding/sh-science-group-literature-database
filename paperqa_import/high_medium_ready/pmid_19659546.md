# Genetic risk factors of thrombosis in the antiphospholipid syndrome

Jesús Castro-Marrero, Eva Balada, Miquel Vilardell-Tarres and Josep Ordi-Ros

Autoimmune Diseases Research Laboratory, Vall d'Hebron University Hospital Research Institute, Barcelona, Spain

# Summary

The possibility of a genetic predisposition to develop anticiphospholipid syndrome (APS) and to produce anticardiolipin antibodies and lupus anticoagulant has been addressed by family studies and population studies. Various studies suggest a familial occurrence of anticardiolipin antibodies and lupus anticoagulant, with or without clinical evidence of APS. This familial tendency could be genetically determined. Multiple human leucocyte antigen-DR or -DQ associations with antiphospholipid antibodies have been described. Genetic studies of a representative antigen, beta2-glycoprotein-I $(\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI})$ , have been carried-out and a particular valine[247]/leucine polymorphism could be a genetic risk for presenting anti- $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ antibodies and APS. Many other thrombosis-related genetic factors have been investigated in APS, but no additional risk for thrombosis has been indicated in affected patients. Although the mechanisms and pathophysiology of thrombosis in APS are highly heterogeneous and multifactorial, different genes and acquired factors seem to be involved. In this review, we will focus on those genetic variants that could contribute to the development of thrombosis in APS.

Keywords: thrombosis, genetics, antiphospholipid syndrome, prothrombin, factor V Leiden.

# Antiphospholipid syndrome: phenotype assessment

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), also known as antiphospholipid antibody syndrome, is an autoimmune disease defined by the simultaneous presence of clinical and laboratory criteria that is characterized by excessive blood clotting, leading to life-threatening vascular thrombosis, as well as an increased risk of recurrent fetal loss in the presence of persistently elevated serum or plasma titres of antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL), such as lupus anticoagulant (LA),

anticardiolipin (aCL) antibodies (IgG and/or IgM isotypes) and anti-beta2 glycoprotein-I (anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI) antibody (IgG and/ or IgM isotypes) (Miyakis et al, 2006). The actual cause of APS is unknown. Several studies reported occurrences of aPL antibodies with or without clinical manifestations of APS (Matthey et al, 1989). APS can involve almost any organ system, including a wide range of neurological manifestations and valvular heart disease. Patients with this syndrome often have systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) or related autoimmune diseases. In this case, we refer to the disease as secondary APS. The syndrome may also occur in the absence of such diseases and it is then known as primary APS syndrome. In fact, there is little fundamental difference between the primary and secondary syndrome. This syndrome is characterized by a spectrum of heterogeneous autoantibodies directed against cellular phosphoprotein components, i.e. aPL antibodies. These include aCL, anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies and LA, which interfere with phospholipid-dependent coagulation cascade reactions.

# Genetics of antiphospholipid syndrome

# Family studies

A genetic basis for aPL antibodies was suggested for the first time when a familial clustering of chronic false-positive syphilis test individuals were detected (McGhee-Harvey & Shulman, 1966); in this case, aPL antibodies were observed many years before overt autoimmune disease developed. Familial occurrence of LA was first described in two pairs of siblings (Exner et al, 1980). Subsequent studies have reported that first-degree relatives of patients with SLE or primary APS have a higher incidence of aCL antibodies, suggesting a genetic predisposition to the development of these antibodies (Mackworth-Young et al, 1987; Goldberg et al, 1995). The identification of several pedigrees with an increased frequency of aPL antibodies and the associated clinical manifestations further support the familial form of APS. In one of these studies, a large kindred in which nine individuals had aPL antibodies was described. # Genes linked to antiphospholipid syndrome

<!-- page 4 -->
Human leucocyte antigen (HLA) studies. Multiple HLA-DR or -DQ associations with aPL antibodies have been described, but are difficult to interpret because of small patient sample size and questions regarding appropriate ethnically-matched control populations. In a study of 13 patients with the primary form of APS, HLA-DR4 and DRw53 were more frequently found, but no correction was made for multiple comparisons (Asherson et al, 1992). However, in a study of 20 patients with SLE and LA, an association with HLA-DQw7 $(HLA-DQB1^{*}0301)$ linked to HLA-DR4 and/or -DR5 haplotypes was found in $70\%$ of patients and was significantly increased compared with 139 race-matched normal control individuals $(33\%)$ $(P = 0.002)$ (Arnett et al, 1991). In this study, the authors found that several HLA-DQB1 alleles shared a common aminoacid sequence (positions 71-77) in the third hypervariable region of the outermost domain. This HLA-DQB1 sequence, called TRAELDT, is distinct from those found in the HLA-DQB1 alleles of HLA-DQw2, -DQw4 and -DQw5 and, as such, may represent an autoantibody predisposing 'candidate epitope' in patients with connective tissue disorders.

On the other hand, other authors have suggested that African-American patients with partial C4A or C4B null alleles are more likely to have aCL antibodies (Wilson et al, 1988). Subsequent studies from this group suggest that coexistence of C4 deficiency alleles with DQB1 alleles that contain the TRAELDT structural domain seems to be associated with aCL antibodies (Wilson et al, 1995). In contrast, patients in the Hopkin Lupus Cohort study who were homozygous for C4A deficiency had a lower frequency of aCL and LA than patients with SLE who did not have this deficiency (Petri et al, 1993).

Antigenic targets: phospholipid-binding plasma proteins. Among targets of aPL, beta2-GPI, which bears the epitopes for aCL antibody binding, has been extensively studied. It is a glycoprotein (about $50\mathrm{kDa}$ ), found in plasma at a concentration of approximately $200~\mu \mathrm{g / ml}$ , which makes it one of the most abundant proteins in human serum, second only to fibrinogen among the plasma proteins involved in clotting (McNeil et al, 1990). A member of the short-consensus- repeat protein family, beta2-GPI is characterized by five 'sushi-domains'. The fifth sushi-domain contains the binding site to phospholipid, and it attaches to activated cellular surfaces (Warm, 1984). Although its physiological role is not known, 'in vitro' data suggest that beta2-GPI may play a role in coagulation. It binds to anionic phospholipids and inhibits the contact phase of the intrinsic blood coagulation pathway (Schousboe, 1985), adenosine diphosphate-dependent platelet aggregation (Nimpf et al, 1987) and the prothrombinase activity of human platelets (Nimpf et al, 1986). Although these data imply an anticoagulant role for beta2-GPI, deficiency of this protein is not a clear risk factor for thrombosis.

The plasma level of this protein is under genetic control (Cleve, 1968). A study of familial thrombophilia demonstrated that heterozygous partial beta2-GPI deficiency is not associated with the risk of thrombosis (Bancsi et al, 1992). Patients with APS appear to have normal or somewhat elevated levels of beta2-GPI (De Benedetti et al, 1992; Galli et al, 1992). Neo- or cryptic epitopes expressed on beta2-GPI as a result of the beta2-GPI/phopholipids interaction could be a potential antigenic target for the autoimmune type of aCL antibodies. The human beta2-GPI gene (ITGA2B) is mapped on chromosome 17q23-qter with four major polymorphisms $(\mathrm{Ser}^{88} / \mathrm{Asn}, \mathrm{Leu}^{247} / \mathrm{Val}, \mathrm{Cys}^{306} / \mathrm{Gly}$ and $\mathrm{Trp}^{316} / \mathrm{Ser})$ . Alterations in beta2-GPI properties between these polymorphisms are not defined. The Leu $^{247} / \mathrm{Val}$ polymorphism locates in domain 5 of beta2-GPI which is a potential epitope site for anti- $\beta_{2}$ -GPI antibodies (Ichikawa et al, 1994). As a result, polymorphisms on or near the phospholipid binding site or the antigenic site can affect autoantibody production. Hirose et al (1999) found that the valine 247 allele was more frequently detected in Asian patients with APS than in matched normal individuals. Furthermore, it reported an association between the $\mathrm{Val}^{247} / \mathrm{Val}$ homozygous genotype and the presence of anti- $\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies only among Asian patients with APS. The authors found no evidence of an increased risk of thrombosis in this Asian population.

Additional genetic risk factors associated with thrombosis. Protein C (PC), protein S (PS) and prothrombin, like beta2-GPI, are phospholipid-binding plasma proteins. Recent data suggest phospholipid specificity for different phospholipid-binding plasma proteins, including beta2-GPI, prothrombin, PC and PS in APS. Results of early genetic studies have established that two types of genetic defects cause venous thrombosis: loss-of-function mutations (antithrombin [SERPINCI], PC [PROC] and PS [PROS1]) and gain-of-function mutations (Factor V Leiden [F5] G1691A and prothrombin [F2] G20210A) (Reitsma & Rosendaal, 2007). Combined, these loss and gain-of-function mutations account for about half of the genetic thrombosis risk. Therefore, there is every reason to believe that additional genetic causes of thrombosis remain to be discovered, and many attempts have been, and continue to be, made to identify them.

A number of genetic variants have been investigated as risk factors for the development of thrombophilia. These risk factors, although considerable and significant, are insufficient to cause thrombosis in every carrier. PC and PS deficiencies result in defects in the activated PC anticoagulant system. PC is activated after the binding of thrombin to its endothelial receptor (thrombomodulin). Activated PC cleaves and inactivates factors Va and VIIIa, thereby inhibiting clot formation. PS acts as a non-enzymatic cofactor for activated PC, optimising the efficiency of these reactions. Based on these functions, it is predictable that PC and PS deficiencies are linked to a hypercoagulable state and an increased risk for thrombosis. The prevalence of PC deficiency in the general population is estimated at approximately 1 in 300 (Miletich et al, 1987). Recent data on the prevalence of PS deficiency in the population yielded frequencies between $0\cdot 03\%$ and $0\cdot 13\%$ (Dykes et al, 2001). Activated PC resistance (APCR) is an inherited abnormality in the coagulation system that is frequently detected in patients suffering from thrombosis (Koster et al, 1993; Seligsohn & Zivelin, 1997). Nowadays, a total of 195 different PROC mutations (D'Ursi et al, 2007) (182 missense and 13 stop mutations) and 201 PROS1 mutations (Gandrille et al, 2000) (of which $48\%$ correspond to missense mutations) have been reported in the relevant databases (PROC mutations, http://www.itb.cnr.it/procmd; PROS1 mutations, http://www.isth.org).

Inherited APCR is, in most cases, the result of a gain-of-function point mutation in $F5$ . This point mutation was first described in 1994 and is currently referred to as factor V Leiden (FVL). FVL is highly prevalent in Caucasians with carrier frequencies in the population ranging from $1\%$ up to $15\%$ (Rees et al, 1995; Franco et al, 1999). APCR in the absence of FVL is also linked to an increase in the risk of deep venous thrombosis (Visser et al, 1999). One of the first observations of familial coexistence of primary APS and FVL and their association with thrombosis has also been reported (Schutt et al, 1998). Taken together, the coexistence of both APS and FVL in thrombocytopenic subjects, led to an increased number of thrombotic events, suggesting a critical role of combined acquired and hereditary APCR in the development of thrombosis in this family. In one report from

six family members with autoimmune thrombocytopenia, three had been diagnosed with APS, and all three were also heterozygous for FVL (Brenner et al, 1996).

Chopra et al (2002) determined if the prevalence of two prothrombotic genetic factors (F5 G1691A and F2 G20210A) was increased in patients with aPL with a history of venous/ arterial thrombosis compared to patients with aPL antibodies without history of thrombosis. One hundred and fifty-seven patients with aPL antibodies were studied. The occurrence of venous and arterial thrombotic events from the time of antibody detection was retrospectively determined, using appropriate clinical and diagnostic criteria. The prevalence of F5 G1691A in patients with thrombosis was $13\%$ compared to $4\cdot 6\%$ in patients without thrombosis [Odds Ratio (OR) 3-11, confidence interval (CI) 0-92-10-6]. In patients with aCL antibodies, $15\%$ of subjects with arterial thrombosis had F5 G1691A compared to $3\cdot 5\%$ of patients without thrombosis (OR 4-9, CI 1-2-19-3). On the other hand, F2 G20210A was identified only in five patients, none of whom had thrombosis.

Several polymorphisms within the tissue factor pathway inhibitor gene (TFPI) may determine TFPI expression and increase the risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE) in predisposed individuals. Lincez et al (2007) tested this hypothesis by comparing TFPI activity and the frequency of common TFPI polymorphisms (T-33C, C-399T and T-287C) or FVL in patients with APS who had a history of VTE and compared them with those without VTE and also with normal control individuals. They found that only APS patients with a history of venous thrombosis had TFPI activity levels significantly different from control individuals $(1\cdot 77\pm 0\cdot 60$ vs. $0\cdot 77\pm 0\cdot 19$ U/ml; $P = 0\cdot 0001)$ , and this was associated with inheritance of the TFPI -33C allele $(1\cdot 70\pm 0\cdot 72$ U/ml for TC/ CC genotypes vs. $0\cdot 97\pm 0\cdot 56$ U/ml for TT; $P = 0\cdot 01)$ . Multivariate analysis of APS and FVL patients revealed that the greatest independent contributor to VTE was TFPI activity, while inheritance of either the TFPI-33C or -399T alleles each increased the odds of VTE by nearly 13 times. These results indicate that the TFPI T-33C and C-399T polymorphisms are significantly associated with venous thrombosis in the presence of other risk factors, especially in APS, and may be clinically relevant in patients who are prone to hypercoagulability.

The single base pair substitution in F2, termed F2 G20210A, is one of the most common genetic alterations described in thrombophilia. Its prevalence is higher in patients with venous thrombosis $(6 - 18\%)$ when compared with the general population $(1 - 3\%)$ (Poort et al, 1996).

Ames et al (2001) evaluated the prevalence of F2 G20210A and methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) C677T in 40 patients with primary APS and in 27 persistent carriers of aPL antibodies without underlying diseases. Non-APS thrombotic patients and healthy subjects served as control groups. Plasma total homocysteine (tHcy) was measured in all patients with aPL antibodies and in 51 subjects from the healthy control group. Heterozygous F2 G20210A was more frequent in the thrombotic group without APS $(18\%)$ than in the

control $(4\%)$ , aPL $(11\%)$ or APS $(12\%)$ groups, whereas homozygous MTHFR C677T was equally distributed. After genotype sub-grouping, plasma tHcy was higher in APS patients with homozygous MTHFR C677T compared with non-homozygous APS patients $(22 \pm 5 \cdot 4$ vs. $11 \pm 1 \cdot 3 \mu \mathrm{mol} / \mathrm{l}$ ; $P < 0 \cdot 01)$ and with homozygous MTHFR C677T controls $(22 \pm 5 \cdot 4$ vs. $15 \pm 2 \cdot 0 \mu \mathrm{mol} / \mathrm{l})$ . In the APS group, mean age at first event was lower in homozygous MTHFR C677T patients than in non-homozygous patients $(26 \pm 7 \cdot 5$ vs. $36 \pm 13$ years; $P = 0 \cdot 008)$ . In the same group, homozygous MTHFR C677T patients suffered an increased average number of events per person than non-homozygous patients $(1 \cdot 9$ vs. $1 \cdot 3$ ; $P = 0 \cdot 04)$ . Heterozygous F2 G20210A contributed little to the thrombotic tendency of primary APS whereas plasma tHcy in aPL subjects may influence age at first event and number of events.

Hyperhomocysteinemia (abnormal elevation of homocysteine plasma concentrations) is a well-known risk factor for thrombosis and is associated with a mildly increased thrombotic events risk. Acquired causes of hyperhomocysteinemia include nutritional deficiencies of vitamin B6, vitamin B12, folate, advanced age, chronic renal failure, and the use of antifolic drugs. Defects in the genes encoding MTHFR and cystathionine beta-synthase (CBS), two enzymes involved in homocysteine intracellular metabolism, may result in enzyme deficiency and hyperhomocysteinemia. Most of these gene defects are rare and only have clinical consequences in homozygosity. Two mutations in MTHFR (C677T and A1298C) and one mutation in CBS (844ins68) are the most prevalent. Hyperhomocysteinemia is a common finding in patients with APS, and may contribute to a severity of the thrombotic events observed in this syndrome. In one study, elevated homocysteine concentrations (15 mmol/l or more) were found in 16 of 52 $(30\cdot 8\%)$ patients with APS (Avivi et al, 2002).

Thrombomodulin is an integral membrane protein of endothelial cells and monocytes. When thrombin binds to thrombomodulin, it loses its procoagulant activity, but becomes capable of activating PC. Therefore, a hereditary deficiency of thrombomodulin may very well play a role as a risk factor for thrombotic disease. A number of missense mutations are currently known in the thrombomodulin gene (THMD) of patients with venous thrombosis (Norlund et al, 1997). A particular amino-acid dimorphism, Ala455Val, was found with frequencies of $0\cdot 81 / 0\cdot 18$ in Caucasian patients who suffered from thrombophilia (Van der Velden et al, 1991). The potential role of THMD mutations in myocardial infarction recently received further support from the documentation of a frame-shift mutation in a family with arterial disease (Kunz et al, 2000).

The recently discovered endothelial protein C receptor (EPCR) on endothelial cells is another important regulator of the PC anticoagulant pathway. A 23-bp insertion at position 4031 in exon 3 (4031ins23) of the gene encoding EPCR (PROCR) has been identified that may predispose patients to deep venous thrombosis (Biguzzi et al, 1999). Furthermore,

mutations in PROCR and THMD have been associated with late fetal loss (Franchi et al, 2001).

Given that EPCR plays a role in the anticoagulation system and in placental development, a study from Spain (Hurtado et al, 2004) hypothesized that anti-EPCR autoantibodies may be involved in clinical manifestations of APS and in fetal loss. They found that both IgM and IgG anti-EPCR serum levels were higher among APS patients than in controls (57 vs. 45 Arbitrary Units (AUs) and 75 vs. 72 AUs respectively). They concluded that anti-EPCR autoantibodies could be detected in APS patients and constituted an independent risk factor for a first fetal death episode for IgM (OR 23; CI 2·0–266·3) and IgG (OR 6·8; CI 1·2–38·4).

Another Spanish study (Jiménez et al, 2008) analysed the genetic polymorphisms in platelet glycoproteins (GP) Ib-alpha (GP1BA), Ia/IIa (ITGA2) complex and IIb/IIIa (ITGA2B) complex and their correlation with the development of arterial thrombosis and preclinical arteriosclerosis in patients with APS or with SLE. Thrombotic events were assessed clinically and confirmed by objective methods. They found a significant correlation between the 807 T/T genotype of ITGA2 and arterial thrombosis (22% in APS patients vs. 7% in controls, $P = 0\cdot 04$ ; OR 3·59, CI 1·20–10·79). The variable number tamden repeat (VNTR) GP1BA and PIA1/2 ITGA2B polymorphisms were not associated with arterial thrombosis in patients with APS when they were individually analysed. The coexistence of both ITGA2 807T and ITGA2B PIA2 alleles increased the arterial thrombosis risk (28% vs. 7%, $P = 0\cdot 005$ ; OR 4·84, CI 1·67–13·96). Interestingly, the coexistence of ITGA2 807T of and ITGA2B PIA2 was associated with the presence of carotid plaque (35% vs. 4%, $P = 0\cdot 002$ ; OR 12·92, CI 2·39–69·81). They concluded that the T/T genotype of the ITGA2 807 C/T polymorphism may be an additional risk factor for the development of arterial thrombosis in APS.

Impaired fibrinolytical outcomes may be one of the pathogenic factors for thrombotic events in patients with antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL). Yasuda et al (2002) investigated the consequences of gene polymorphisms of tissue plasminogen activator (PLAT) and plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (SERPINE1) in patients positive for aPL. Seventy-seven Japanese and 82 British patients with aPL were examined for an Alu-repeat insertion (I)/deletion (D) polymorphism of PLAT and for the 4G/5G polymorphism in the SERPINE1 promoter. Correlations between these polymorphisms and clinical symptoms of APS (arterial thrombosis, venous thrombosis, miscarriage) were analysed. No significant differences in the allele frequencies of these genes between patients and controls were found. There was no significant correlation between these gene polymorphisms and clinical symptoms of APS in patients with aPL antibodies. Therefore, polymorphisms of PLAT or SERPINE1 probably do not significantly influence the risk of arterial/venous thrombosis, or pregnancy morbidity in patients with aPL antibodies.

The effect of the 4G/5G polymorphism of SERPINE1 on the risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE) remains controversial.

In a recent meta-analysis, Tsantes et al (2007) investigated the association between the SERPINE14G/5G polymorphism and the risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE) in 18 papers; it included patients without another known risk factor, and comprised 2644 cases and 3739 controls. Based on their findings, the SERPINE1 4G allele appears to increase the risk of venous thrombosis, particularly in subjects with other genetic thrombophilic defects.

Diz-Kucukkaya et al (2004) found that a polymorphism $(\mathrm{Val}^{34}\mathrm{Leu})$ in the factor XIIIA gene $(F13A1)$ decreased the risk of both arterial and venous thrombosis. Nevertheless, the results showed that the $F13A1$ Leu34 allele had no protective effect in the development of thrombosis in patients with APS. On the contrary, de la Red et al (2009) found that this polymorphism was associated with a higher risk of thrombosis in patients with the presence of both aPL antibodies and high fibrinogen levels. They found no significant differences in $F13A1$ Leu34 allele frequencies between primary APS, APS/ SLE, SLE-aPL and asymptomatic-aPL patients, or between patients with and without thrombosis. In this study, the $F13A1$ Leu34 allele seemed to have a protective effect on the development of thrombosis in patients with aPL antibodies, but only in those patients with high plasma fibrinogen values.

Another study (de Laat et al, 2006) on the ANXA5 polymorphism (ANXA5 -1C-T) and the presence of anti-annexin A5 antibodies in APS concluded that the detection of anti-annexin A5 antibodies does not seem relevant for estimating the risk for thrombosis or miscarriage in APS. The -1C-T mutation was an independent risk factor for miscarriage which is independent of APS.

Diz-Kucukkaya et al (2007) studied the gene encoding P-selectin glycoprotein ligand-1 (SELPG) and showed that a VNTR polymorphism on this gene is a significant determinant of thrombosis predisposition in patients with APS. Furthermore, this risk appears to correlate better with the combination of alleles inherited rather than with the presence of any particular allele (Table I).

# Conclusions

APS is still seen as a rather obscure disease despite extensive research; this view is mainly the result of the unreliability of the current assays for detecting the presence of aPL antibodies. The consequences are a poor correlation between serological markers and clinical manifestations, and a lack of clarity about the pathogenetic mechanism causing the syndrome. Genetic susceptibility related to aPL antibodies and APS has been extensively examined during recent years. However, it has been difficult to determine genetic risk factors for aPL antibodies and APS because of the heterogeneity in the antigen specificity and the pathogenesis of clinical manifestations related to APS. It is becoming increasingly clear that interactions between more than one genetic abnormality or between a genetic factor (coagulation proteins) and environment components determine whether

and when an individual will suffer from venous thrombosis. Given the fact that APS is characterized mainly by the presence of thromboembolic events, it seems perfectly plausible that several genetic factors may also be involved in its pathophysiology. Genome-wide linkage analysis and larger cohort case-control association studies, as well as multicentre international collaborations would be useful for a better understanding of the genetic predisposition to develop APS. As more genes responsible for these effects can be identified, a clearer understanding of the pathogenesis of this syndrome will be achieved, which undoubtedly will lead to more useful and safer therapeutic strategies.
