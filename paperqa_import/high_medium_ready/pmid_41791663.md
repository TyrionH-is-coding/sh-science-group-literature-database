# Abstract

Background: Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a thrombotic autoimmune disease, treated with long-term anticoagulation to prevent thrombotic complications. APS is defined by persistent presence of antiphospholipid antibodies (aPLs). However, certain patients lose aPL positivity over time. The risk of thrombotic recurrence after aPL negativization is unknown; hence, the necessity of continued anticoagulation in this subset of patients is uncertain.

Objectives: To assess thrombotic recurrence in patients with APS and aPL negativization after discontinuation of anticoagulation.

Methods: In this systematic review and meta-analysis, Pubmed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Emcare, and Academic Search Premier were searched for studies including patients with APS, aPL negativization and discontinued anticoagulation. Risk of bias was assessed using the Risk Of Bias In Non-Randomized Studies - of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool. The primary outcome was the pooled incidence rate (95% CI) for thrombotic recurrence after discontinuation of anticoagulation, estimated using a random-effects Poisson regression model.

Results: In total, 55 patients from 6 relevant observational cohort studies fulfilled the inclusion criteria. Heterogeneity among these studies was found in the definition of aPL negativization, timing of treatment discontinuation and follow-up. A total of 3 venous thrombotic recurrences were reported, resulting in a pooled thrombotic recurrence rate of 1.61 per 100 person-years (95% CI: 0.52-4.98).

Conclusions: Interpretation of the thrombotic recurrence rate in patients with APS and aPL negativization found in this meta-analysis is limited by the nature and quality of the included data. Future research should assess whether aPL negativiation could

# 1 | INTRODUCTION

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an autoimmune disorder characterized by thrombosis, pregnancy morbidity and/or nonthrombotic manifestations, as well as the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies (aPLs) [1,2]. Due to the high risk of thrombotic recurrence, long-term anticoagulation is the mainstay of treatment in patients with thrombotic APS [3,4].

Persistent presence of aPLs, specifically lupus anticoagulant (LAC), anti- $\beta 2$ -glycoprotein I (a2GPI) and/or anticardiolipin (aCL) antibodies, is required for APS classification. Patients who test positive for all 3 aPLs are at the highest risk for thrombosis [5] and the contributing role of aPLs in the pathophysiology and development of thrombosis in APS has been widely studied [6]. However, aPL levels have been known to fluctuate or, in certain patients, disappear altogether over time, also known as aPL negativization. Although it could be expected from the thrombogenic properties of aPLs that loss of aPL positivity may result in a lower risk of thrombosis, evidence for this hypothesis is lacking and the effect of aPL negativization on thrombotic recurrence risk is unclear; hence, the necessity of continued anticoagulant therapy in this specific subset of patients is uncertain.

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, the aim was to systematically summarize the available literature and to estimate the incidence rate of recurrent thrombosis in patients with APS and aPL negativization and discontinuation of anticoagulant therapy.

# 2 METHODS

This study was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis statement and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024513509) prior to the initiation of the review process [7].

# 2.1 Search strategy and data sources

PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Emcare, and Academic Search Premier were systematically searched up to April 16, 2024, with the help of a research librarian. Search terms focused on APS, aPLs, and aPL negativization (complete search strategies can be found in the supplementary materials).

# 2.1 Study selection and eligibility criteria

Two authors (L.J.H. and T.E.M.) independently performed study selection. Clinical trials, cohort studies and case-control studies published in the English language, including patients with APS, aPL negativization, who either continued or discontinued anticoagulant therapy were considered eligible for inclusion. Abstracts of meetings or conferences were not included. Other study designs, such as case reports and case series, were excluded. All identified studies were first screened by title and abstract. After exclusion of nonrelevant studies, full text of the remaining articles was screened for eligibility. Any disagreement on study inclusion was resolved by discussion.

After selection of eligible studies, it was thought that accurate assessment of the differences in thrombotic recurrence between patients with APS with aPL negativization who continued anticoagulant treatment and those who discontinued was precluded by the limited data—both in terms of the sample sizes in the individual studies, as well as the insufficient control for confounding. Therefore, we decided to focus the systematic review and meta-analysis only on the risk of thrombotic recurrence after discontinuation of anticoagulant treatment. The PROSPERO protocol was amended accordingly.

# 2.2 Data extraction

One author (L.J.H.) extracted data from each included study, using a predesigned, standardized data extraction form. All filled in data extraction forms were reviewed and verified independently by 2 other authors (T.E.M. and E.C.). Any disagreement was solved through discussion. Data extracted through this form included study characteristics (author, year of publication, study design, study aim, and inclusion and exclusion criteria), patient characteristics (total number of patients included and number of patients with aPL negativization), APS profile (previous manifestations and antibody profile, primary or secondary APS), antithrombotic therapy use and discontinuation, definition of aPL negativization, start and duration of follow-up, and thrombotic recurrences.

# 2.3 Risk of bias assessment

The Risk Of Bias In Non-Randomized Studies - of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool was used to assess the risk of bias in the included studies, which focuses on 7 domains of bias (confounding, classification

Each domain is scored separately as low, moderate, serious or critical risk of bias, after which an overall judgment is made for the risk of bias. Two authors (L.J.H. and E.C.) independently evaluated the included studies using this tool. Results of the risk of bias assessment were visualized through a traffic light plot using the robvis tool [9].

# 2.4 Statistical methods for meta-analysis

Incidence rates for thrombotic recurrences (with $95\%$ CIs estimated using the approximate normal method) after discontinuation of anticoagulant therapy were estimated for each included study, expressed as the number of recurrent thromboses per 100 person-years. Person-years were estimated for each study, by multiplying the number of included patients by the mean time of follow-up after discontinuation of anticoagulant therapy. If only a median time of follow-up was reported, it was converted to a mean time of follow-up using methods developed by Wan et al. [10], which estimates the mean using the median, interquartile ranges and sample size. One study did not report follow-up time after discontinuation of anticoagulant therapy [11]. Therefore, follow-up after discontinuation was estimated by subtracting the median time between aPL negativization and discontinuation of anticoagulation, from the median time of

follow-up since aPL negativization. Estimated incidence rates were pooled using a random-effects Poisson-normal regression model [12].

Heterogeneity was assessed using the between studies variance $(\tau^2)$ and the $1^2$ statistic with its $95\%$ CI, which describes the proportion of variability between studies attributed to heterogeneity rather than chance. Because of the limited number of included studies, publication bias could not be properly assessed.

Analyses were performed using R studio (version 4.4.0), particularly the meta-package (R Foundation of Statistical Computing, 2024: http://www.R-project.org/).

# 3 | RESULTS

# 3.1 Study selection

A total of 500 records were identified by our database search. After removal of duplicates ( $n = 15$ ), title and abstracts were screened for 485 records. Of these, 472 nonrelevant articles were excluded, leaving 13 reports for full-text screening. Finally, 6 studies were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis [11,13-17]. Figure 1 shows the study selection process and detailed reasons for exclusion.

# 3.2 | Characteristics of included studies

All 6 included studies were of retrospective, observational design, including a total of 98 patients with APS and aPL negativization. In 55

out of these 98 patients, anticoagulant therapy was discontinued. The Table shows the characteristics of each included study. Two studies only reported demographic and clinical characteristics for the whole study population, not specifically for the population of our interest (patients with aPL negativization and discontinuation of anticoagulation) [14,16].

In the majority of patients, venous thromboembolism (VTE) was the main previous clinical APS manifestation. One study included 4 patients with obstetric APS with no history of thrombosis [13]. In the 4 studies that reported on our specific population of interest, most patients had a single positive aPL profile before aPL negativization [11,13,15], with an exception of the study by Zen et al. [17]. Notably, this study was the only one that solely included patients with secondary APS, whereas all other studies mostly included primary patients with APS.

The definition of aPL negativization used in each study varied, or was not clearly specified. One study only reported aPL negativization at the end of follow-up, not at time of anticoagulation withdrawal [16]. Five out of 6 studies reported follow-up after discontinuation of anticoagulation [13-17], with an exception of the study by Ballul et al. [11], which reported follow-up after APS diagnosis and follow-up after aPL loss, as well as a median time of discontinuation of anticoagulation after aPL loss of 0.8 years (IQR, 0-3.0 years). Two other studies explicitly mentioned a delay between aPL negativization and discontinuation of anticoagulation; mean delay 96.1 (±38.5) months [13] and median delay 45 (1-276 months) months [17].

Although anticoagulation was discontinued, in 2 studies all patients were switched to antiplatelet therapy [15,17]. One study reports antiplatelet therapy to also be withdrawn at the time of anticoagulation discontinuation [13]. In the other 3 studies, it was not explicitly mentioned whether patients were (still) being treated with antiplatelet therapy after discontinuation of anticoagulation.

# 3.3 Risk of bias

Using the ROBINS-I tool, the risk of bias was deemed either serious or critical for all 6 studies [8]. This was mainly a result of risk of bias due to confounding and risk of bias due to (uncertainty regarding) missing data. Figure 2 summarizes the risk of bias for each individual domain for all studies.

# 3.4 Thrombotic recurrence rates

In the 55 patients with aPL negativization and discontinuation of anticoagulation, 3 venous thrombotic recurrences were reported in a total of 186.68 person-years (Figure 3). This resulted in a pooled thrombotic recurrence rate of 1.61 per 100 person-years (95% CI: 0.52-4.98).

An $1^{2}$ of $0.0\%$ was observed among these studies, with a notably wide $95\%$ CI of $0.0\%$ to $74.6\%$ . No further subgroup or sensitivity

analyses were performed, due to the low overall event rate and limited number of included studies, all with small sample sizes.

# 4 | DISCUSSION

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we found an estimated thrombotic recurrence rate of 1.61 per 100 person-years in patients with APS and aPL negativization whose anticoagulant treatment was discontinued, although this estimate is limited by the small sample sizes, heterogeneity, missing data and high risk of bias found in the included studies. In comparison with previously reported thrombotic recurrence rates in patients with APS (both with and without anti-coagulation), this rate appears to be relatively low, but should be interpreted with caution in the context of these limitations.

A previous meta-analysis found a 2-year proportion of $5.4\%$ (95% CI: $3.8\% -7.5\%$ ) for recurrent thromboembolism (TE) after an initial VTE in patients with aPL who were on chronic anticoagulant treatment, in comparison to a 2-year proportion of 0.178 (95% CI: 0.150-0.209) in patients with aPL and initial VTE who had discontinued anticoagulant therapy [18]. Moreover, a recent prospective cohort study using an international registry of patients with APS found a 5-year thrombotic recurrence rate of $12\%$ in patients with thrombotic APS who were not being treated with anticoagulation, compared with a rate of $10\%$ in matched patients with APS who were treated with anticoagulation [19]. Reasons for not being anticoagulated were missing in $59\%$ of the cases and confounding by indication should be taken into account when interpreting these results. Still, the similar recurrent thrombosis rates in both groups led the authors to conclude that a risk-stratified approach to anticoagulant treatment in patients with APS could be beneficial and should be studied in future trials.

The 2019 European League Against Rheumatism recommendations for the management of APS in adults do not state any suggestions on aPL negativization in regard to anticoagulant therapy management in patients with APS, but do mention it as a controversial issue in their future research agenda [4]. The more recent 2024 guidelines by the British Society of Haematology on the investigation and management of APS suggest not to change the management of patients with thrombotic APS whose aPL levels become negative, but simultaneously state that there is insufficient evidence to make recommendations for these patients [3].

Ideally, our study would have compared thrombotic recurrence rates between patients with aPL negativization and discontinued anticoagulation and those with aPL negativization still using anticoagulation. As mentioned, it was thought that publication bias, selection bias, and the very limited data, especially on patients who continued anticoagulation, prevented an accurate comparison between these 2 groups. For this reason, it was decided to only focus on the population who discontinued anticoagulation in this study.

This rate is much higher compared to our findings, as well as the previously mentioned thrombotic recurrence rates in the general APS population treated with anticoagulation. It should be noted that more than a third of the patients in this study had a history of arterial thrombosis. The median number of previous VTEs was 4 per patient, with 2 patients having a history of over 10 thrombotic events each. Besides, $29.0\%$ of patients tested positive for both aCL and LAC and no triple positivity was reported. However, aB2GPI was only tested in a subset of patients who tested negative for both aCL and LAC, meaning that the true rate of double or triple positivity is presumably higher than reported. Moreover, cardiovascular risk factors were already present at baseline in $45.8\%$ of patients and it is stated that after follow-up, cardiovascular risk factors increased in $75\%$ of patients. All of this taken together suggests that the patients in this cohort constituted a very high risk study population that may not be representative of the majority of patients with APS.

Our study has limitations. First, only a limited number of studies have been published that fulfilled our inclusion criteria, all of observational design, with very small sample sizes and high risk of bias. Due to the observational nature of these studies, the decision of continuing or discontinuing anticoagulation was not standardized and made by treating physicians. Moreover, in some studies, there was a delay between the detection of aPL negativization and the discontinuation of anticoagulant treatment, meaning that the patients who eventually discontinued treatment were highly selected. It is probable that anticoagulation was more likely to be discontinued in patients who were perceived to be at a lower risk of recurrent thrombosis, which may have played a role in the low recurrent rate that was found in this meta-analysis.

Second, publication bias may have also contributed to the low thrombotic recurrent rate, as studies with beneficial outcomes may have been more likely to be published. As a result of the small number of studies with small sample sizes, no additional analyses focusing on publication bias (such as a funnel plot or statistical tests) could be performed.

Third, although the point estimate of $I^2$ of was $0.0\%$ , the wide $95\%$ CI (0.0%-74.6%) indicates considerable imprecision and uncertainty. It can be assumed that there is substantial heterogeneity in the meta-analysis, as is to some extent inherent to research on APS. When systematically reviewing the literature, we found heterogeneity in the definitions of aPL negativization that were applied. Besides, variability in aPL assays, specifically differences in cut-off values for positivity, may influence both the initial diagnosis of APS, as well as the determination of aPL negativization in the included studies. The direction of this effect is difficult to predict. Moreover, certain aspects of treatment regimens varied between studies. Although anticoagulation was discontinued in all patients included in the meta-analysis, certain studies switched their patients to antiplatelet therapy [15,17], whereas other studies did not [13] or did not clearly report this [11,14,16]. Although antiplatelet monotherapy is considered by all international guidelines as not potent enough for thrombotic APS, it is in fact being investigated as an alternative for vitamin K antagonists in specific patients with APS with previous arterial thrombosis (NCT05995600). Pending the results of this trial, it cannot be ruled out that certain patients included in this meta-analysis in fact received adequate secondary prevention, although their anticoagulant treatment was discontinued. Additionally, the delay between the detection of aPL negativization and the discontinuation of anticoagulation reported in some studies [11,13,17], while not in others [14-16], means that not all studies discontinued anticoagulant treatment at the same time after aPL negativization. Lastly, a limited number of patients with only obstetric APS were included [11,13,15]. It was decided not to exclude these studies from the analysis, as this would have resulted in the loss of valuable data on other patients with thrombotic APS, which form the majority in these cohorts, in an already scarce evidence base.

Fourth, it should be noted that the incidence rate for thrombotic recurrence in this meta-analysis is an estimation based on study-level data, and we were not able to pool incidence rates for standardized time intervals.

To conclude, while the estimated thrombotic recurrence rate in patients with APS and aPL negativization in whom anticoagulation is discontinued found in this study appears to be relatively low, interpretation of this finding is limited by the nature and quality of the included research. Still, this study provides a summary of the evidence base that is currently available. Further research is needed to investigate whether aPL negativization could be a factor to consider when aiming to select patients with APS who may possibly benefit from a discontinuation of anticoagulant treatment. Ideally, continuing versus discontinuing anticoagulation in low-risk patients with APS with aPL negativization would be investigated in future clinical trials.
