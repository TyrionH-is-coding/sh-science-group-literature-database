# SUMMARY

Chorea can be an initial manifestation of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) or antiphospholipid syndrome (APS). It has been mostly described in younger female adults in association with other manifestations of SLE. When chorea appears as an initial and only manifestation in SLE/APS patients, the establishment of the correct diagnosis is difficult, and it may be initially attributed to a more common aetiology. Here we report an elderly man who presented with a new onset of right-sided chorea without other clinical manifestations of SLE/APS. He started on steroids a year later, however, there was no improvement. His chorea was symptomatically managed along with aspirin, and hydroxychloroquine as he refused to be on additional immunosuppression. Anticoagulation was relatively contraindicated, and also not favoured by this patient; therefore, aspirin was initiated. Even in elderly patients, once the common etiologies of chorea have been worked up, we suggest doing a rheumatological evaluation. Early diagnosis and prompt treatment can prevent persistent neurological abnormality.

# BACKGROUND

An imbalance of direct and indirect dopaminergic pathways because of dysfunctional or damaged basal ganglia circuitry manifests as chorea. Chorea can be present at all stages of life, from newborns to elderly individuals. According to the Committee on Classification of the World Federation of Neurology, chorea is defined as 'a state of excessive, spontaneous movements, irregularly timed, nonrepetitive, randomly distributed and abrupt in character. These movements may vary in severity from restlessness with mild intermittent exaggeration of gesture and expression, fidgeting movements of the hands, unstable dance-like gait to a continuous flow of disabling, violent movements'. Huntington's disease is the main suspicion in adults presenting with hyperkinetic movement disorders like chorea, once drug-induced or Parkinsonian dyskinesia has been ruled out. However, chorea is also known to occur from vascular, endocrine, metabolic, infectious, paraneoplastic, neoplastic, and autoimmune pathologies.[12] Chorea could manifest as one of the neurological manifestations in many autoimmune disorders such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), Bechet's disease, vasculitis (CNS and systemic), rheumatoid arthritis, Sjogren syndrome, and coeliac disease. Chorea is one of the rare CNS manifestations of

young females with SLE and/or APS. The onset of SLE or APS in elderly individuals is uncommon and usually has an underlying trigger like medications, infections, or malignancies. The prevalence and clinical significance of chorea in elderly onset SLE or APS remains to be elucidated. Here, we report a patient with elderly onset SLE who had chorea as an initial symptom of SLE accompanied by APS. We emphasise the importance of early autoimmune workup, once common etiologies are ruled out so prompt treatment can be started.

# CASE PRESENTATION

A man in his 70s with a medical history significant for recurrent occult gastrointestinal bleeds and diverticulosis, presented to the emergency room for a new onset of involuntary movements of his right arm and leg. These movements had started 4 weeks earlier and gradually became worse, involving the right side of the body; he had difficulty in holding things with his right hand and difficulty walking. There was no history of fever, dysuria, upper respiratory infection, rash, photosensitivity, hair loss, oral ulcer, Raynaud's phenomenon, dryness of mouth or eyes, weight loss, headache, loss of consciousness, seizure, weakness, numbness, speech, or visual disturbances. He had no family history of rheumatic or neurological diseases, and he denied smoking and alcohol consumption. The patient and family also denied the use of any medication in the last 2 years. Physical examination revealed exuberant choreiform movements of the right hemi body, afflicting the right upper and lower limb, with predominant involvement of the upper limb, consistent with chorea. Examination of other systems was unremarkable.

# DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

We diagnosed him with neuropsychiatric lupus (chorea) and also with APS based on prior lacunar infarcts noted on imaging and persistent anticardiolipin IgG and IgM, lupus anticoagulant, and beta 2-glycoprotein, $>12$ weeks (table 1).

# TREATMENT

Anticoagulation was relatively contraindicated and not favoured by the patient given his prior history of recurrent gastrointestinal bleeds. He was maintained on low-dose aspirin (LDA) and hydroxychloroquine to reduce the future risk of thrombosis and SLE flares. He was started on oral prednisone $(1\mathrm{mg / kg / day})$ for 8 weeks, approximately a year after his initial manifestation of chorea. However, no subjective improvement was observed. At this time, steroids were tapered in the next 3 weeks. The patient was offered additional immunosuppression, but he refused. His chorea was symptomatically managed with deutetrabenazine. Patient reported subjective improvement in his chorea, without complete resolution.

# OUTCOME AND FOLLOW-UP

He was established with rheumatology approximately a year after his initial manifestation of chorea. He was diagnosed with SLE based on positive ANA by IFA 1:640, positive anti-DNA by IFA 1:40, hypocomplementemia (low C4), positive APL antibodies.

# DISCUSSION

SLE or APS manifesting without triggering drug exposure or occult malignancy in an older man is exceedingly rare. Approximately $40\%$ of the neuropsychiatric SLE manifestations develop before the onset of SLE or at the time of diagnosis. Chorea accounts for only $1\%$ of that.[3-5]

The pathogenesis of SLE or APS-associated chorea could be related to a vascular, or immune-mediated neuronal, and glial injury resulting in dysfunctional basal ganglia circuitry. It is a combination of either vascular injury induced by circulating immune complex, occlusive vasculopathy because of endothelial cell activation induced by cytokines and complement activation, or macrovascular and microvascular thrombosis induced by antiphospholipid (aPL) antibodies.[67] The prevalence of aPL antibodies especially anti- B2GPI antibodies are higher in SLE patients with neuropsychiatric disease. Our patient had high titers of anti-B2GPI antibody, anticardiolipin antibody, and positive lupus anticoagulant by both dRVVT and aPTT-based assays. Chorea in SLE/APS is mostly described in younger female adults in association with other manifestations of SLE.[89] Our patient is an elderly man and has no other clinical features of SLE. There were no other underlying triggers to explain the cause of his chorea. Chorea can be the first and at times the only sign before the establishment of diagnosis of SLE. Under such conditions, the establishment of the correct diagnosis is difficult, and the chorea may be initially attributed to a more common aetiology.[10] It is important to rule out more common etiologies like cerebrovascular accidents, drug intoxication, hyperthyroidism, and Huntington's disease first. CSF analysis of NPSLE may or may not help, as we know from prior studies that it can be normal in up to $40\%$ of cases.[10] However, once ruled out, we suggest sending autoimmune work up for SLE/APS promptly, regardless of the age and sex of the patient. Neuroimaging findings may or may not fit in the clinical picture. MRI is more sensitive than CT for neuropsychiatric SLE-associated lesions. One of the the most common MRI findings of neuropsychiatric SLE is white matter hyperintensities which is seen in about $42\%$ of the patients.[10] Our had chronic lacunar infarcts in the right basal ganglia and right cerebellum and mild chronic small vessel ischaemic change which raised concern for increased thrombosis risk. Predominantly attributable to thromboembolic phenomena that occur in the context of SLE-related hypercoagulable states and are clearly correlated with the presence of aPL antibodies.

There is no consensus about therapeutic strategies in NPSLE, but the recommendation is a therapy based on the primary pathogenic mechanism: either immunosuppressive treatment or anticoagulation/antiplatelet treatment, depending on the involved pathophysiologic mechanisms. The combination of both treatment options is preferred when both mechanisms are considered a possibility. In our patient, we had various barriers to the recommended treatment, which included delayed diagnosis after a year. After no improvement with steroids, additional immunosuppression was refused by the patient, which contributed to suboptimal results.[11] [12]

As seen in the prior case series summarised in table 2, like our patient, $80\% -90\%$ of patients had APL antibodies positive which increased their risk of developing arterial thrombosis. However, only one study[13] used a prophylactic antithrombotic agent to reduce the further risk of thrombosis. Full-dose anticoagulation was mostly reserved for patients with evidence of arterial thrombosis. We also saw in the above studies that suboptimal response was achieved with corticosteroids and there was a need for additional immunosuppression. However, in our case, we believe that the treatment was already delayed by almost a year and at this point, we saw neurological damage. In the management of primary thromboprophylaxis in patients with SLE, several drugs have been used with favourable results and other pharmacological approaches are currently under investigation with promising preliminary data. In patients with aPL, there is agreement to use LDA. The rationale of this recommendation is that LDA was shown to have a favourable balance between the prevention of thrombotic events and the risk of bleeding episodes in patients.[14] [15] Similarly, HCQ is recommended as baseline therapy in all SLE patients, as it has been demonstrated to be protective against thrombosis in all patients, no matter of their aPL status,[16] [17] and prevents Lupus flares. Our patient had a history of recurrent occult GI bleed, and anticoagulation was relatively contraindicated and also was declined by the patient. We decided to start aspirin to reduce the risk of further disease events. We suggest making treatment decisions based on the patient's active disease profile, and careful consideration of benefits and risks in each scenario since there are no prior high-quality prospective or randomised control studies to guide this.

# Learning points

Differential diagnosis of chorea should consider systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and antiphospholipid syndrome.   
$\triangleright$ Crucial is a prompt diagnosis and treatment to improve the long-term outcomes of these patients.   
Elderly-onset SLE whose initial and solitary symptom is chorea is rare and is a difficult diagnosis.
