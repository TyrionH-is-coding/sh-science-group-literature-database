# REVIEW

# Domain I of $\beta 2$ -glycoprotein I: its role as an epitope and the potential to be developed as a specific target for the treatment of the antiphospholipid syndrome

Y Ioannou<sup>1,2</sup> and A Rahman<sup>1,2</sup>

$^{1}$ Centre for Rheumatology Research, Windeyer Building, University College London, London W1T 4JF, UK; and $^{2}$ Medical Molecular Biology Unit, Institute of Child Health, 30 Guilford Street, London W1CN 1EH, UK

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) represents one of the most common acquired causes of thrombophilia and recurrent miscarriages. The only treatment of proven benefit is anticoagulation, often required at high intensity and life-long duration. This therapy can be associated with side effects such as bleeding and is not always effective. Hence, there remains a need for safer, targeted and ideally more effective therapies. Antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL) are pathogenic and promote thrombosis. Independent groups, including our own, have show that the major epitopes that pathogenic aPL targets lie within domain I of the protein $\beta 2$ -glycoprotein I ( $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ ). This review focuses on the evidence presented thus far which characterizes the immunodominant epitopes within domain I, demonstrating that the epitope is a conformational one centred around residues R39-G43 and also involving other residues within domain I, such as residues D8 and D9. The hypothesis is proposed that a recombinant domain I molecule, and a recombinant mutant domain I with enhanced aPL binding properties, may be used as an inhibitor of aPL binding and thus inhibit aPL-induced pathogenicity. In vivo proof-of-concept studies within the murine femoral vein injury model are presented supporting this hypothesis, and the rationale as well as potential benefits and problems of employing such an approach to treat APS are discussed. Lupus (2010) 19, 400-405.

Key words: Antiphospholipid syndrome; antiphospholipid antibodies; $\beta 2$ -glycoprotein I; thrombosis

# Introduction

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an autoimmune condition characterized by vascular thrombosis and/or recurrent miscarriage in the presence of persistent antiphospholipid autoantibody (aPL) positivity. The huge advances in biotechnology over the past two decades have enabled the development of a plethora of targeted biologic therapies for a variety of conditions in the field of autoimmunity. However, this success in therapeutic development has not been observed in APS, with the accepted therapy of anticoagulation remaining unrivalled over the past two decades. Could this be due to APS not being perceived as a high-profile disease by biotech companies or could it be due to

deficiencies in our knowledge of disease processes in APS limiting our capacity to develop targeted therapies for this disease. In this review, we argue that there is a significant unmet need for a safer and targeted therapy in APS. The evidence identifying the immunodominant epitope for pathogenic aPL is presented, with the focus then shifting to how this knowledge can be harnessed to develop a novel approach to targeting specifically the pathogenic population of aPL, with discussion orientated around the potential advantages of such an approach.

# An unmet need for a safer and targeted therapy in APS

APS remains the most common cause of acquired thrombophilia and recurrent miscarriage. There have been no formal epidemiological studies estimating the prevalence of APS in the community.

<!-- page 2 -->
One editorial suggested that as APS is present in $10-15\%$ of patients with deep venous thrombosis (which has a population prevalence of $2 - 5\%$ ), the prevalence of APS may approximate to $0.3 - 1\%$ . APS is almost unique in having the potential to cause both arterial and venous thrombosis and furthermore represents a leading acquired cause of pregnancy loss and pregnancy related morbidity, with approximately $15\%$ of women with recurrent miscarriage being aPL positive. Clearly APS carries a significant burden of morbidity and mortality.

The only treatment that has proven benefit in APS is anticoagulation. Both retrospective and prospective studies show that no time point has been identified at which anticoagulation may be safely discontinued and thrombosis can recur in the first 6 months post-discontinuation.[5,6] The required intensity and duration of anticoagulation remain debated issues, although many agree that a substantial proportion of APS patients require long-term high-intensity anticoagulation, perhaps even of life-long duration. This is particularly the case in arterial thrombosis and such treatment is associated with problems. Use of long-term anticoagulation carries the risk of bleeding, affecting up to $25\%$ of patients on high-intensity warfarin in clinical trials.[7] In addition to the risk of bleeding, a significant proportion of patients with APS may still re-thrombose despite antithrombotic therapy.[8,9] Hence, the argument supporting the development of a targeted therapeutic agent with a superior safety profile and arguably greater efficacy than current treatment is compelling.

How might such an agent look? One possibility is to create a recombinant molecule that could act as a decoy, selectively binding pathogenic aPL and preventing binding to target cells via native antigen. What attributes should such a candidate harbour? Certainly it would need to be biologically inert to be safe. Also, it should lack the potential to interfere with host function, such as inhibition of host autoantigen or inhibition of key signalling pathways. It should also be relatively cheap to make with ease of administration. Could such challenges be met?

# aPL are pathogenic

The strategy of employing a peptide modelled around the immunodominant epitope of aPL as a therapeutic candidate to inhibit such antibodies from binding to native antigen is only valid if such antibodies can be shown to be pathogenic.

There is a wealth of evidence supporting the concept that aPL mediate pathogenicity in APS. Multiple independent groups have tested polyclonal IgG purified from patients with APS for the ability to cause activation of target cells in vitro. These bioassays have shown that these polyclonal human IgG can activate human endothelial cells, monocytes and platelets.[10,11,12] Similarly passive transfer of polyclonal IgG from patients with APS into animal models shows that these IgG can promote venous thrombosis,[13] arterial thrombosis[14] and pregnancy morbidity,[15] whereas IgG samples purified from healthy people or disease controls (with other autoimmune diseases) do not promote these adverse outcomes. Within these animal models, IgG derived from APS patients has also been shown to activate arterial endothelial cells and monocytes in vivo.[16]

The lupus anticoagulant (LA) effect is mediated through aPL antibodies in the blood of patients. In systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), the presence of aPL with LA represents the strongest predictor of thrombosis, even more so than smoking which was the second most common predictor.[17] In one recent study of young patients admitted with stroke, being LA positive was associated with an odds ratio (OR) of 43.1 (95% confidence interval [CI] 12.2–152.0) of having a stroke, which went up to 201.0 (95% CI 22.1–1828.0) in patients who were LA positive on the oral contraceptive pill.[18]

# What constitutes the antigenic target for pathogenic aPL?

Given the evidence that aPL are pathogenic, can we treat APS by preventing the interaction of these pathogenic aPL with their cognate antigens? In order to do this we must first identify the immunodominant epitopes that are bound by pathogenic aPL. This remains a disputed area. Autoantibodies to a number of autoantigens involved in thrombosis (including prothrombin, annexin V and protein C) have been described in patients with APS, although the relevance of these autoantibodies in promoting pathogenicity has not been fully elucidated. It is generally accepted that autoantibodies to $\beta 2$ -glycoprotein I ( $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ ) represent the most relevant aPL autoantibody population with regards to pathogenicity.[19,20,21] de Laat and colleagues[22] have convincingly shown that patients with both anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ antibodies and LA activity have a very high association with thrombogenic pathogenicity and other studies support this by showing

<!-- page 3 -->
that triple positivity (anti-cardiolipin, anti- $\beta 2$ GPI and LA) has the highest association with pathogenicity and severity of thrombosis.9

If anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ antibodies are indeed the major pathogenic population in patients with APS, which immunodominant epitope within $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ is recognized by these pathogenic autoantibodies? $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ is a member of the complement control family of proteins containing five homologous domains termed 'sushi' domains (Figure 1(a)). The function of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ remains uncertain, although it is conserved in evolution and present in the circulation at high concentrations $(4\mu \mathrm{M})$ . It is thought to be involved in haemostasis through binding serine proteases and apoptotic cell clearance. Domain V (DV) is unique in containing a positive patch adjacent to a hydrophobic loop and is responsible for anchoring $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ to anionic phospholipid (PL) and cell surface receptors such as annexin II,[23] toll-like receptors,[24] glycoprotein Iba[11] and ApoER2.[25] Studies utilizing domain deletion mutants in which one or more domains of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ are missing have shown that the balance of evidence favours domain I (DI) as harbouring the immunodominant epitope for binding of pathogenic aPL (i.e. aPL samples derived from patients with clinical features of APS).[26,27] This is underlined by DI alone being able to bind these aPL in

both solid and fluid phase assays. $^{28,29}$ The evidence supporting DI as the location of relevant aPL-binding epitopes on $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ has been reviewed extensively by our group and confirmed through experiments from multiple independent groups. $^{30}$ This does not mean that no aPL bind other domains of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ , simply that DI-binding is the important interaction for pathogenicity in the majority of patients. Some groups have argued for the importance of antibodies targeting domain IV (DIV) in patients with APS. $^{31}$ However, both domain III (DIII) and DIV are glycosylated. Such large sugar molecules (as shown in Figure 1(a)) would act as a shield and tend to inhibit aPL from targeting epitopes in these domains. Furthermore, unlike DI, no group has shown that DIV alone can bind pathogenic aPL, further enhancing the evidence that DI harbours the majority of pathogenic immunodominant aPL-binding epitopes. These epitope-mapping studies employed purified IgG antibodies. It should be noted that recent studies investigating IgA anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ antibodies show that binding to DIV/V of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ correlates with thrombotic disease. $^{32}$

Having identified the aPL-DI interaction as being important in the development of thrombosis in APS, the next step is to focus on and identify the fine immunodominant epitopes within this domain. Multiple studies, again by independent groups, utilizing single-point mutations within whole recombinant $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ have shown that binding to aPL is altered by changes in sequence of the positively charged patch from glycine 40 (G40) to arginine 43 (R43) within DI.[27,29,33] de Laat and colleagues[33] have shown that where the blood of patients contains IgG whose binding to $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ is dependent upon this G40-R43 region, this is a strong predictive factor for development of thrombosis. However, it is unlikely that aPL binding is dependent upon this single linear epitope as aPL does not bind denatured $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ for example (unpublished observation), and there is evidence that other regions of the DI sequence may also play a role. Some groups have used a peptide homologous to the DI/DII interlinker region which bound a proportion of patient aPL serum samples,[34,35] whilst another group has shown that a peptide homologous to the amino terminal of DI (and also to the CD40 ligand) was also capable of binding aPL.[36] We undertook a detailed analysis of the published crystal structure of DI and found that R39, adjacent to the region G40-R43, represents the amino acid with the greatest surface exposure within DI ( $\sim 70\%$ ) and hence is most amenable for antibody binding[29] (Figure 1(b) and (c)).

<!-- page 4 -->
In contrast to other groups, we produced isolated DI (without the rest of the molecule) by expression in bacteria[28] and tested the effects of point mutations in this domain on its ability to bind IgG from patients with APS. We mutated positively charged R39 to neutral serine (R39S) and found that binding to a series of purified IgG samples from eight patients with APS in the fluid phase was completely abrogated. When mutating G40 and R43, binding to these samples was also reduced significantly in the fluid phase, but not to the same level as with the R39S mutation.[29] Through other mutations of DI, we confirmed that the interlinker region DI-DII was also important. DI contains two negatively charged aspartic acid residues at positions 8 and 9 (D8, D9). The findings relating to sequence changes in this region were intriguing. When either D8 was mutated to serine (D8S) or D9 was mutated to glycine (D9G), the products showed reduced binding to the IgG samples from patients with APS. However, the DI variant containing both mutations (D8S, D9G) was a very strong binder to all of these samples in the solid and fluid phases.[29] Hence, we proposed that pathogenic aPL bind a conformational epitope within DI, based around residues R39-G43, D8,D9 and the interlinker,[29] all three regions being in close approximation to each other in the three-dimensional structure of DI as shown in Figure 1(b) and (c).

de Laat and colleagues have shown that $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ in the fluid phase does not bind aPL unless the large sugar molecules on DIII and DIV are removed by deglycosylation.37 DI on its own is not glycosylated, thus as expected we found that DI in the fluid phase binds aPL better than whole $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ . Hence, there is a rationale for employing DI as a therapeutic to bind pathogenic aPL in vivo and prevent it from binding to cells via whole $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ . Given that aPL binds a conformational epitope within DI as we have shown recently, the whole DI molecule rather than a linear peptide is likely to be a better inhibitor to a greater number of patient samples.

# Production of DI β2GPI: hurdles to overcome

The vast majority of studies investigating domain deletion mutants of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ employ recombinant protein expressed through the infection of Sf9 insect cells with baculovirus.[38] This technique yields sufficient quantities of protein (around $2\mathrm{mg}/$ l culture supernatant). However, given that DI does not require complex post-translational modification

such as the addition of sugar molecules, bacteria could be employed to express DI. Such an expression platform would be preferable given its ease of use and relative cost-effectiveness. However, it is interesting that such an expression system had not been published prior to our first description of this method.[28] There are a number of problems to overcome with regards to establishing an expression system in Escherichia coli. Chief among these is the fact that the cDNA sequence of human DI contains numerous clusters of codons that are rarely used by $E.$ coli, although commonly employed by eukaryotic cells. Also DI has two disulfide bonds which is a problem for prokaryotic cells given that the cytoplasm of bacteria is a reducing one. Finally, DI can be toxic to bacteria. We developed a method of expressing DI by synthesizing a gene encoding for DI using recursive polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and ensuring that all codons are those most frequently seen in $E.$ coli. A leader sequence was also added to allow for protein transportation to the periplasm which is an oxidative environment allowing for the formation of disulfide bonds. Finally, expression was tightly regulated utilizing the T7lac promoter to overcome issues relating to toxicity. This resulted in good yields of DI $(750\mu \mathrm{g} / 1$ culture), purified through the use of a C-terminal hexahistidine tag.[28] Subsequently, this method has been modified by expression of DI in the cytoplasm as inclusion bodies and re-folding and purifying the proteins, considerably enhancing yield (unpublished observation).

One final problem to overcome is the size of DI: around $8\mathrm{kDa}$ . If this is given parenterally, then the half-life is likely to be very short. DI needs to be attached to a larger molecule to allow for the half-life to be lengthened.

# Inhibitory capacity of wild-type and mutant DI in vivo

The ease of expressing DI in $E.$ coli allowed a large panel of DI mutants to be screened with mutations incorporated through hypothesis-led approaches, rather than through random inclusion as seen with peptide libraries. This effort invested in establishing an expression platform for DI using $E.$ coli was rewarded with the discovery that the double mutant D8S,D9G binds aPL better than wild-type DI. Thus, wild-type DI and the high-binding mutant D8S,D9G were employed to assess the ability of DI to act as a decoy and inhibit aPL pathogenicity in vivo.

<!-- page 5 -->
The mouse model employed to investigate this was the femoral vein trauma model, which has been developed by Professor Pierangeli and validated over many studies.[12,13,39] As expected, mice injected with IgG derived from an APS patient developed a large thrombus post-femoral vein injury as compared to mice injected with IgG purified from normal healthy volunteer serum. If mice were pre-injected with a his-tagged irrelevant control peptide or the DI mutant R39S (which has lost the ability to bind human aPL), no inhibition of APS-derived IgG thrombus enhancement was seen. However, if mice were pre-injected with wild-type DI or the high binding mutant D8S,D9G, then complete abrogation of thrombus enhancement by APS-derived IgG was observed.[16] This marked inhibitory effect on pathogenicity of passively transferred human IgG aPL in vivo was dose dependent and, furthermore, at lower levels of inhibitor, the D8S,D9G mutant was a better inhibitor than wild-type DI, mirroring the in vitro inhibition studies. Thrombus enhancement by APS-derived IgG was not the only outcome measure investigated in these studies. DI was also shown to inhibit APS-IgG induction of murine monocyte tissue factor expression and also APS-IgG mediated activation of murine aortic endothelial cells.[16]

It is most likely (although as yet unproven) that DI is inhibiting aPL-induced thrombosis in this model through a decoy mechanism, in which it binds circulating aPL leading to their removal from the circulation. It is possible that DI could be acting through an aPL-independent antithrombotic mechanism. However, this is unlikely as mice injected with IgG from healthy individuals do develop a small thrombus post-femoral vein trauma, but this thrombosis is not inhibited when DI is administered. Also, a recent study shows that DI inhibits LA activity of APS derived plasma.[40] Given that LA activity has been shown to be antibody mediated, this also supports the notion that DI can bind antibodies in plasma.

# Future development of DI and the mutant DI(D8S,D9G)

Much progress has been made over the past decade with regard to establishing the immunodominant epitope within $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ and using this information to identify a novel target for inhibition of pathogenicity caused by aPL. Certainly the idea of specific blockade of the pathogenic anti-DI population,

whilst not interfering with host biology, is appealing and warrants further exploration through in vivo studies. There is also the possibility that polymeric DI derivatives may cross-link anti-DI receptors in B cells and induce anergy in the absence of T-cell help, as has been shown by La Jolla Pharmaceuticals in preliminary studies using a tetramer of DI attached to an inert scaffold.[41] Also, given that DI is not a 'foreign' protein, it is unlikely to be immunogenic, making it more likely to be safe. However, further work needs to be done. It is necessary to show that DI can be modified to increase half-life without losing biological activity. Also, its safety and pharmacokinetic profile need to be delineated in animals carefully. These studies are currently underway, and it will be interesting to observe whether these experiments lead to new developments in the treatment of APS in the coming years.
