## Abstract

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a rare autoimmune disorder characterized by the persistent positivity of antiphospholipid antibodies (aPLs) along with thrombotic manifestations, obstetrical complications, or nonthrombotic manifestations. The kidney is a major target organ in APS and is associated with poor prognosis. In light of the 2023 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) and the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) classification criteria for antiphospholipid syndrome, this review aims to cover the expanding data on the broad spectrum of renal involvement in aPL carriers and in patients with APS, including microvascular involvement (antiphospholipid syndrome nephropathy) and macrovascular involvement in native kidneys, and in transplant recipients with primary or secondary antiphospholipid syndrome.

## Full Text

APS is an autoimmune disorder, characterized by thrombotic manifestations, obstetrical complications, and other nonthrombotic events, along with persistent aPL positivity. 1 APS can be primary or can be associated with another autoimmune disorder, most commonly systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). The incidence of APS is about 1 to 2 cases per 100,000 adults, whereas the prevalence ranges from 40 to 50 cases per 100,000 adults. The prevalence of aPL positivity in different populations is not fully defined, but is estimated to be between 1% and 5% of healthy individuals. This prevalence is much higher in specific populations such as patients with SLE. 2 , 3 , 4 About 1% of patients with APS will develop catastrophic APS (CAPS), a life-threatening rapid onset of thromboembolic disease with a mortality rate of approximatively 50%. 5

New classification criteria have been released in 2023 by the ACR and the EULAR. The ACR/EULAR classification criteria for APS are based on a scoring system for both laboratory and clinical criteria. After fulfilling entry criteria, patients can be classified as APS for research purposes if they score ≥ 3 points both from clinical and laboratory domains. aPL positivity must be confirmed at 12 weeks to validate the laboratory domains. Three aPL assays are recommended, including IgG or IgM anticardiolipin antibody (aCL), IgG or IgM anti-beta 2 glycoprotein I antibody (aβ2-GPI), or lupus anticoagulant (LA). The 2023 APS classification criteria no longer consider isolated IgM aCL and/or IgM aβ2-GPI positivity as sufficient to classify a patient as APS 1 ( Figure 1 and Table 1 ). The ACR/EULAR classification criteria advocates the use of solid phase assays, that is, “moderate” or “high” level aCL and aβ2-GPI, based on enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay to ensure homogeneity in research and a cutoff calculated by the 99th percentile has been abandoned. 1

Alternative automated solid phase platforms are now widely used in clinical practice because of their reproducibility and feasibility compared with enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. 6 Recently, the International Society of Thrombosis and Haemostasis Scientific and Standardization Committee subcommittee on Lupus Anticoagulant/Antiphospholipid Antibodies published a practice guideline, suggesting the use of enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay for research, but warning that this should not impact the routine practice of some laboratories that efficiently employ platforms other than enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. 7 The International Society of Thrombosis and Haemostasis Scientific and Standardization Committee subcommittee also recommends the interpretation of results according to the local cutoff values (e.g., 99th percentile) and warns against grading positivity of aCL and aβ2-GPI into low-medium-high titers because there is not enough harmonization between methods. 6

With respect to the clinical domains, the ACR/EULAR classification criteria allow for the stratification of risk for macrovascular events through the evaluation of traditional risk factors, with weighted assessment. 1 New domains, previously referred to as “non-criteria manifestations” such as microvascular domain items considered mechanistically distinct from larger vessel disease, have been added. Thus, aPL nephropathy (aPLN), cardiac valve disease, livedo racemosa, and thrombocytopenia have been added to better capture and quantify the broad spectrum of APS manifestations, while maintaining a high specificity. 1

Other “non-criteria” aPLs such as antiphosphatidylserine/prothrombin antibodies, anti-Annexin V, or IgA isotype of aβ2-GPI and aCL, etc., have been linked to clinical manifestations. However, these antibodies are not included in the ACR/EULAR classification criteria. 8

The kidney is a major target organ in primary and secondary APS, including microvascular involvement (aPLN either with or without thrombotic microangiopathy [TMA]), macrovascular thrombosis that can affect renal artery and vein, renal infarction, and renal artery stenosis (RAS). 9 This review aims to cover the expanding data on the broad spectrum of renal involvement in aPL carriers and in patients with APS, including microvascular and macrovascular disease both in native kidneys and in transplant recipients.

### aPLN

aPLN is a rare and probably underestimated manifestation of APS. It is mainly characterized by acute and/or chronic vascular changes in kidney biopsy, associated with the persistent positivity of aPL. 10 The estimated prevalence of aPLN ranges from 2.5% to 9% in patients with primary APS, 11 , 12 , 13 and can reach > 50% in patients with secondary APS. 14 , 15

#### History and Definition

aPLN was first reported in the early 1990s by D’Agati et al. 16 and Becquemont et al. 17 in the form of renal TMA lesions in association with the presence of aCL. Shortly after, Amigo et al. 18 first described the renal pathological lesions in patients with primary APS. In 1999, Nochy et al. 19 proposed specific small-vessel histopathologic findings of aPLN, that were used as part of the revised Sapporo criteria in 2006. 20 Recently, the APS Classification Criteria Renal Pathology Subcommittee published a report that aimed to better characterize this entity. A definition and terminology have been proposed. 10 Thus, aPLN has been included as a distinct and specific entity in the new criteria 1 and provides the most accepted terminology for both acute and chronic pathologic lesions of aPLN ( Table 2 ). Histopathological features such as immune complex, immunofluorescence deposits, inflammation, fibrinoid necrosis, or vasculitis have to be absent in aPLN, particularly in the setting of APS related to other connective tissue disease, mainly SLE. 15 ( Table 2 , Figure 2 ). Other renal manifestations have been reported to be associated with APS; examples include membranous nephropathy, minimal change disease, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, mesangial C3 nephropathy, and pauci-immune crescentic glomerulonephritis. 13 , 21 , 22 Antiphospholipase A2 receptor–positive membranous nephropathy associated with primary APS has also been reported in the literature. 22 , 23

Renal biopsy is the cornerstone of aPLN diagnosis. However, patients with APS are often treated with anticoagulants and discontinuation might increase thrombotic risk in high risk profile patients. In addition, a higher risk of bleeding has been reported in patients with APS with lupus nephritis. 24 Tailored assessment of both the bleeding and thrombotic risk is mandatory. Transjugular renal biopsy in patients with APS under low dose aspirin or anticoagulants have shown similar safety profile as percutaneous renal biopsy in untreated patients with APS. Thus, it could be considered in selected cases. 25

#### Clinical and Laboratory Presentation

Patients with aPLN may experience new-onset or worsening arterial hypertension, acute kidney disease or chronic kidney disease (CKD), mild to nephrotic range proteinuria, and inconstant hematuria. aPLN can occur as chronic nephropathy or a rapid onset of acute kidney injury. aPLN can be limited to the kidneys or it can occur concomitantly to microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and ischemic organ damage. 13 , 15 , 16 , 26 , 27 In Figure 3 , we represent a proposed diagnostic algorithm for aPLN. In addition, patients with aPLN more frequently exhibit livedo reticularis or racemosa, 15 arterial hypertension, LA positivity, aPL double (aCL and aβ2GPI) positivity or triple positivity and some noncriteria aPL (i.e., antiphosphatidylserine/prothrombin antibodies). 28 , 29 , 30 aPL (mostly LA) can be detected in up to 25% of patients with biopsy-proven TMA and suggests that the presence of aPL may confer an increased risk for the development of TMA. 29

Some clinical phenotypes have been reported to be suggestive of higher likelihood to develop aPLN. In an analysis of the AntiPhospholipid Syndrome Alliance for Clinical Trials and InternatiOnal Networking registry, 3 clusters were identified. Female patients with SLE, venous thromboembolism, thrombocytopenia, hemolytic anemia, and a positive LA test were more prone to have concomitant aPLN. 31 Another cluster analysis study evaluating 123 aPL positive patients (88.6% with SLE) identified 3 disease types. Cluster 1 represented 18.7% of the cohort and was characterized by a higher prevalence of glomerular capillary and arteriolar thrombi and fragmented red blood cells in the subendothelial space. Cluster 2 represented 26.8% of the cohort, showing a higher prevalence of fibromyointimal proliferative lesions as seen in hyperplastic vasculopathy. Cluster 3 represented 54.5% of the cohort (mainly patients with SLE), and was characterized by higher prevalence of subendothelial edema, of both glomerular capillaries and arterioles. 32 Cluster analysis could be a valuable tool to provide clinicians with insight on disease phenotypes and long-term outcomes, thereby facilitating patient management. 33

#### Pathophysiology

The pathophysiology of aPLN is not well-understood and is mainly extrapolated from pathophysiological mechanisms of thrombogenesis induced by aPL. Specific mechanisms imply the activation of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway, 34 , 35 the activation of type I interferon pathway as well as Neutrophils Extracellular Traps. 36 Tektonidou et al. 36 performed a whole transcriptomic profiling by RNA sequencing on kidney biopsies from 4 patients with APS, 5 patients with SLE, and 3 controls. Complement, interferon and NETs-related genes were highly expressed in APS kidney tissues, pointing out the role of innate immunity in aPLN pathogenesis. Type I interferon signature in kidney of APS mice model has also been described. 37

#### Prognosis

Renal prognosis and predictors of outcome are not well-known in aPLN. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis including 709 patients with biopsy-proven aPLN showed that about one-third of the patients experience CKD or end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) during the follow-up (odds ratio: 6.89 [95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.42–19.58)]. 26 Rousselin et al. 38 reported ESKD-free survival rates at 5 and 10 years of 80.0% (95% CI: 57.6%–91.4%) and 72.7% (95% CI: 46.9%–87.4%), respectively, in a retrospective multicenter study including 31 patients with primary aPLN. In this study, no histological lesions were associated with a decrease in estimated glomerular filtration rate at 12 months. 38 Hoxha et al. 39 performed a retrospective single-center cohort study including 282 patients with primary APS. Twenty-eight percent of the patients presented with damage accrual during the follow-up, defined as organ or tissue impairment present for ≥ 6 months or causing permanent loss, with a 5-fold higher risk (odds ratio: 4.9 [95% CI: 2.1–11.7], P < 0.0001). 39

### aPLN in Patients With SLE

aPLN occurs in primary APS and SLE-associated APS and occurs independently of any lupus nephritis class lesion. 10 , 40 However, these 2 entities can be challenging to distinguish. 41 The revised 2018 ISN/Renal Pathology Society classification criteria for lupus nephritis, do not include certain renal histopathological entities associated with lupus nephritis such as aPLN. 42

The prevalence of aPL positivity can reach 30% to 40% in patients with SLE 2 , 3 and has been associated with a 3-to-5-fold increased risk of microvascular lesions. 14 The prevalence of aPLN in patients with SLE with positive aPL ranges from 31% to 39.5%. 14 , 15 , 40

aPL positivity has been suggested as an independent risk factor for chronic impairment of renal function in patients with SLE. 9 Concomitant aPLN worsens the prognosis of lupus nephritis, and both activity and chronicity indexes have been described to be higher. 43 , 44 Other studies have not found such association. 45 In addition, LA and IgG aCL have been linked to mortality in patients with SLE. 46

The risk factors for aPLN in patients with SLE are LA positivity, arterial thrombosis, and obstetrical manifestations, livedo reticularis, 15 , 40 aCL positivity. 43 , 47 In the cohort of the AntiPhospholipid Syndrome Alliance for Clinical Trials and InternatiOnal Networking registry, antinuclear antibody positive patients without concomitant systemic autoimmune rheumatic diseases did not represent a risk factor of aPLN. 11 Zonal cortical scarring has been shown to have a 89% specificity, with a 62% positive predictive value for aPLN detection in SLE patients. 48

### Treatment of aPLN

Evidence-based recommendations for the management of primary or secondary aPLN are currently lacking. The 2019 EULAR recommendations for the management of thrombotic APS recommend anticoagulation with vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) in patients with a history of thrombotic APS. 49 There is no controlled study evaluating VKAs in isolated aPLN and these recommendations are mostly extrapolated from guidelines on lupus nephritis associated with TMA. Some cohort studies described a higher rate of renal response in aPL-positive patients with lupus nephritis with TMA treated with anticoagulation. 50 In contrast, some studies have described the occurrence of aPLN or unfavorable renal outcome in patients treated with anticoagulation. 15 , 38 To our knowledge, there is no study evaluating the use of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) in aPLN. The effectiveness of DOACs in APS is debated, especially in patients with arterial manifestations and aPL triple positivity, because of the significant risk of recurrence. 51 One case reported an LA-positive patient with lupus with deep venous thrombosis treated with DOACs. While being treated with DOACs, the patient developed renal biopsy-proven TMA and multiple cerebral infarcts that completely resolved after rivaroxaban was replaced by warfarin. 52

Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone inhibitors are recommended because they have shown to improve long-term renal outcome in SLE-associated aPLN, 53 , 54 as well as a decreased risk of kidney disease progression (hazard ratio: 0.11 [0.02–0.59]; P = 0.010], but not proteinuria or arterial hypertension. 54

Some data indicate that the mTOR pathway may play a significant role in the pathogenesis of aPLN, providing a rationale for the use of mTOR inhibitors for its treatment. 34 , 35 , 55 , 56 The use of mTOR inhibitors as an add-on treatment to anticoagulation will offset the possible concerns about their procoagulant effects. Prospective randomized controlled studies including a large number of patients are warranted to clarify its place in the treatment of aPLN.

B-cell depletion treatment has been used in acute aPLN with TMA lesions. Rituximab was the first to be used in a pilot open-label study (i.e., RITAPS study), evaluating 2 patients with aPLN. One patient had partial response at 24 weeks of therapy whereas the other withdrew from the treatment early because of an infusion reaction. 57 Few case reports describe favorable outcomes with the use of rituximab in patients with SLE. 58 However, Sun et al. 59 reported that rituximab in addition to conventional therapy was not associated with better renal outcome in 21 patients with SLE with TMA, but it was associated with better overall survival.

Belimumab is a monoclonal antibody blocking the B-lymphocyte stimulator factor, avoiding B-cell activation and proliferation. It is the first biological drug approved for the treatment of SLE in active phase and it has been associated with reduced antibody levels, including anti–double-stranded DNA antibody. 60 Belimumab has been proposed as a potential targeted therapy in APS because it has been associated with aPL disappearance in patients with SLE. 61 A single-center, open-label, prospective, phase 2 descriptive pilot trial, evaluating belimumab in aPL-positive patients is currently being performed. In this study, changes in clinical outcomes of aPLN will be evaluated. 62

Complement inhibition with eculizumab has been successfully used in primary and secondary aPLN, mainly in case reports, and in case series. 63 , 64 Complement inhibition should be further evaluated in clinical trials to clarify its place in the treatment of aPLN in native kidney.

Some other drugs have been suggested as therapeutic options in aPLN. In a BALB/c APS mouse model, tyrosine kinase 2 inhibition has been shown to reduce the elevation of blood urea nitrogen and microalbuminuria and to reverse the pathological vascular changes in the kidney, fibrin and C3 deposition. In addition, the type I interferon signature observed in the kidneys of APS mice was reversed by tyrosine kinase 2 inhibition. 37

Finally, some adjunctive treatments have been proposed in aPLN, with low levels of evidence. Hydroxychloroquine has been proposed as an adjunctive treatment in primary and secondary aPLN, mainly based on efficacy in preventing thrombotic manifestations, and recurrence of lupus nephritis. 65 , 66 In addition, other medications have been suggested, mainly based on preclinical studies, including vitamin D and statins. Pravastatin downregulates tissue factor and prevents glomerular injury in both mouse aPL- and human aPL-treated mice. In humans, fluvastatin has been shown to significantly reduce proinflammatory and prothrombotic markers in patients with APS. In addition, vitamin D has shown some immunomodulatory on endothelial cells, inhibition of tissue factor expression, and inhibition of the type 1 interferon synthesis. 65 , 66 , 67 , 68 Some other potential targeted treatments are currently under evaluation in APS, without evaluation on aPLN outcome. These include daratumumab, a human monoclonal antibody that targets CD38; obinutuzumab, a type II anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody; and telitacicept, another B-cell inhibitor that binds and neutralizes the activity of B-lymphocyte stimulator factor/APRIL. Anti-TNF-a therapy such as adalimumab or certolizumab have also been suggested as potential biological treatment of APS, mainly in obstetrical APS. 69 These novel therapies may represent potential treatment candidates for aPLN. Further studies are warranted to evaluate their efficacy in this indication.

In addition, some pathophysiological mechanisms of aPLN could be promising treatment targets, such type I interferon pathway inhibition with anifrolumab, or NET inhibition with treatment such defibrotide. 70

### Transplantation

The prevalence of aPL in ESKD is high. 71 Some studies found that the prevalence of aPL positivity decreases after kidney transplantation. 72

Nevertheless, aPL positivity at the time of kidney transplantation has been reported as a risk factor for early allograft failure. In a multicenter study, Vaidya et al. 73 analyzed a subgroup of 23 patients with APS awaiting renal transplantation, of which 11 received kidney transplants either with (4 patients) or without (7 patients) concomitant anticoagulation therapy. In this study, all the patients without anticoagulation lost their allografts within 1 week because of renal thrombosis. Other studies have reported that pretransplant IgA aβ2-GPI was a risk factor for allograft thrombosis and delayed graft function. 74

Canaud et al. 75 reported that LA positivity was associated with high rates of poor transplantation outcomes during the first year after transplantation. In addition, 70% of kidney biopsy at 1 year showed relapse of chronic aPLN. 75 Similarly, Gauthier et al. 76 report that patients with positive aPL, even without thrombotic events before transplantation have a higher risk of glomerular filtration rate decline within the first-year posttransplant. Some data suggest that aPL-positive patients with ESKD who were on peritoneal dialysis or preemptively transplanted were at higher risk of posttransplant renal involvement, compared with patients on hemodialysis, probably because of the exposure to heparin. 77 In contrast, some studies found that aPL positivity before kidney transplantation was not associated with inferior renal outcomes after transplantation. 78 Ornella et al. 79 performed a retrospective monocentric study evaluating 41 patients with kidney transplants because of lupus nephritis and 89 because of other causes of ESKD. Among the patients with lupus nephritis, 28 patients had aPL assay and 15 (55.5%) were classified as APS. There were no statistically significant differences in terms of causes of graft loss and graft survival between aPL-positive and aPL-negative patients with SLE. However, in this study, IgG aβ2-GPI positivity was associated with mortality. 79

Vaidya et al. 80 published a cohort study including 1625 patients with ESKD awaiting kidney transplantation. They identified 94 aPL positive patients (5.8%), of which, 46 aPL carriers and 21 with APS (according to revised Sapporo criteria) received kidney transplantation. All patients with APS were treated with either low molecular weight heparin or Coumadin. Despite anticoagulation, patients with APS had lower 10-year graft survival compared with aPL carriers and to controls (18%–20% vs. 28%; P = 0,0001). 80

Canaud et al. 35 first reported that mTOR inhibition (sirolimus) in transplant recipients with aPL positivity was associated with a better allograft prognosis in a prospective study evaluating 37 patients with aPL and 74 control patients with a follow-up of 82 ± 35 months. Indeed, 70% of patients treated with sirolimus had functioning allograft 10 years after transplantation and 11% among the untreated patients. The effect was independent of anticoagulation treatment. The treated group showed less vascular proliferation on renal biopsy and less vascular lesion recurrence. 35

Because complement activation plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of APS, eculizumab has been proposed as a promising therapeutic option in kidney transplant recipients with posttransplant aPL-induced TMA. The first report was published in 2010 by Lonze et al. 81 in a patient with CAPS undergoing kidney transplantation. Canaud et al. 82 reported 3 patients with posttransplant recurrence of acute aPLN (i.e., TMA) treated with eculizumab. Allograft biopsies were performed before and after treatment. Eculizumab resulted in the remission of TMA. In addition, they described a persistent C5b-9 deposition in allografts; however, these findings are commonly found in allografts developing posttransplant TMA. The authors also show that eculizumab did not prevent the occurrence of chronic vascular changes related to aPL positivity and conclude that chronic aPL may not be related to complement activation. 82 Similarly, Lonze et al. 83 reported a successful treatment with eculizumab on 3 patients with APS (2 with CAPS) undergoing kidney transplantation. Anticoagulation and eculizumab were started prior to and following kidney transplantation (2 patients also received plasmapheresis). All patients had functioning renal allografts after 4 years of follow-up. 83

Although the appropriate duration of eculizumab has to be determined, these data suggest that complement inhibitors such as eculizumab may be effective in preventing the recurrence of aPLN after kidney transplantation and they are currently under investigation. 84 , 85

### Kidney Involvement in CAPS

CAPS is a rare, life-threatening variant of APS, characterized by widespread micro- and macrovascular thrombosis, affecting multiple organ systems within < 1 week, 86 with a mortality of approximately 37%. 87 CAPS may occur more frequently in triple-positive high titers aPL patients. 66 Although TMA is frequent in CAPS, biological markers of TMA only occur in 15% and are therefore not specific for CAPS diagnosis. Renal involvement occurs in 73% of CAPS cases, making it the most frequently affected organ. 87 Renal biopsy studies reveal acute lesions, including TMA with fibrin thrombi in glomeruli and/or arterioles (100% of cases), as well as endothelial swelling and fibrinoid necrosis. Chronic lesions, such as fibrous intimal hyperplasia (66.7%), focal cortical atrophy (50%), arterial occlusions, and onion-skin fibrosis are also frequently observed. Clinically, CAPS nephropathy presents with hypertension (50%), proteinuria (100%), hematuria (66.7%), renal insufficiency (66.7%), and nephrotic syndrome (50%), with acute renal dysfunction being a hallmark feature. 88 In a multicenter, retrospective study including 134 patients with APS admitted in intensive care units, TMA was seen in 12% of the patients and was associated with a 2-fold higher in-hospital mortality (44% vs. 20% in patients without TMA, P = 0.05). 89

Early intervention is critical to preventing full-blown CAPS and reducing morbidity and mortality. 66 , 90 The cornerstone of CAPS treatment involves anticoagulation, high dose steroids, plasma exchange or i.v. Igs. 5 , 86 , 91 , 92 , 93 Data on renal prognosis are lacking in CAPS.

Because complement activation has been involved in the pathogenesis of aPLN and CAPS, eculizumab has been proposed as a potential add-on therapy, in addition to standard treatment with inconsistent benefit from case reports and case series. 81 , 87 , 92 , 94 A 2022 descriptive analysis of “CAPS Registry” reported 39 patients treated with eculizumab, of which 84.6% had kidney involvement. Kidney biopsy was not reported. Eculizumab was used in addition to the triple therapy in 59% and was used as a first-line therapy in 16.7% of patients, whereas it was used as a rescue therapy in 83.4%. This study reported 74% favorable response, 23% worsening and 5 deaths; however, renal outcome was not systematically evaluated. Four patients did not recover from dialysis, all of them were treated with eculizumab as a second-line therapy. 95 Other studies reported mixed responses to eculizumab. 96 Carrara et al. 97 reported a refractory CAPS with renal TMA treated with eculizumab. The authors confirmed the activation of the complement system by evaluating ex vivo C5b-9 deposition on cultured human microvascular endothelial cells type 1. Despite the overall clinical response and the resolution of ex vivo C5b-9 deposition, kidney function did not recover, suggesting that the timing of eculizumab initiation is crucial to improve kidney outcome. 97

Rituximab has been proposed in addition to standard therapy, for the management of CAPS with renal involvement. 66 , 98 , 99 , 100 Data from the CAPS registry show that some report successful treatment with rituximab. 98 , 101 , 102

Activation of mTOR was found in the vessels of autopsy specimens from patients with CAPS. 35 The potential beneficial effect of sirolimus in CAPS-associated kidney involvement, and outcomes need to be assessed. 103

### Macrovascular Involvement

#### RAS

APS is a rare cause of RAS that may lead to renal infarction and acute kidney injury. RAS may present as an acute occlusion, or it may present as a progressive disease with decline in kidney function because of chronic ischemic nephropathy. 7 , 104

Hypertension is common in APS in the setting of aPLN or RAS. In addition, some studies describe a higher prevalence of RAS in hypertensive patients with APS. Sangle et al. 105 compared 77 aPL-positive patients (60 secondary APS, 11 primary APS, and 6 aPL carriers) to 91 aPL-negative patients attending hypertension clinics for uncontrolled arterial hypertension who were receiving ≥ 2 antihypertensive drugs, and to 92 normotensive healthy controls. In this study, the prevalence of RAS was significantly higher in aPL-positive patients, compared with the 2 other groups; 26%, 8% and 3%, respectively. In addition, 80% of aPL-positive patients with RAS ( n = 16) had stenosis of the proximal third of the renal artery. 105 In patients with APS, RAS has been associated with a higher risk of end-stage renal disease and mortality. 106

The pathophysiology of RAS in APS is not well-understood. Endothelial cells play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of vascular nonthrombotic disease in patients with APS. 106 , 107 Both recanalization of preexisting thrombosis and in situ intimal hyperplasia with occasional inflammation have been suggested. 108 The pathophysiology of stenotic lesions, such as intimal hyperplasia in patients with APS have been linked to the mTOR pathway activation; however, this was not evaluated in RAS. 35 , 108 In addition, an atherogenic hypothesis has been suggested because some aPL (i.e., aCLs) have been shown to favor atherosclerosis, by cross reacting with oxidized low-density lipoprotein, which may ultimately lead to monocytes or macrophages to foam cells transition and to plaque formation. 109 , 110 , 111

The treatment of RAS in patients with APS is not well-defined. Treatment of associated vascular risk factors and autoimmune disease is of importance. Some data suggest that anticoagulation with warfarin can lead to recanalization without intervention, suggesting that thrombosis is the culprit pathology in the stenosis. 112 , 113 , 114 Indeed, anticoagulation with INR ≥ 3 has been associated with initial reduction of CKD and hypertension. 24 However, Casian et al. 107 performed a longitudinal study evaluating the long-term outcome of RAS in APS patients over 10 years. This study found that most patients developed CKD and did not benefit from renal angioplasty. In addition, anticoagulation was not associated with a better renal outcome or lower rate of death. 107 Another retrospective database study including 33 patients, performed by the same group in 2020 found that patients with APS with RAS who underwent angioplasty or stenting had a trend to a lower probability of developing CKD. Interestingly, the beneficial effect of angioplasty was lost in patients who smoke. 106

Percutaneous angioplasty with or without stenting has been however suggested for the treatment of RAS, depending on the nature of the stenosis, the length, the underlying kidney function, the progression of renal failure, the severity of hypertension, and the occurrence of pulmonary edema. 104 , 105 In addition, drug-eluted stents, such as mTOR-coated stents, have not been not evaluated in aPL associated RAS, and could be a promising approach.

#### Renal Artery and Vein Thrombosis

Renal artery and vein thrombosis are rare complications of APS. In the AntiPhospholipid Syndrome Alliance for Clinical Trials and InternatiOnal Networking registry, arterial visceral thrombosis represented 2% of patients ( n = 9), whereas other studies report prevalences up to 10%. 115 , 116 , 117 , 118 Similarly, renal vein thrombosis seems to be rare in APS, mostly reported in case reports and case series. In the Euro-Phospholipid Project cohort evaluating 1000 patients, renal intraabdominal manifestations (including glomerular thrombosis, renal infarction, renal artery thrombosis, and renal venous thrombosis) represented 2.7% ( n = 27), without detailed information. 12 In the AntiPhospholipid Syndrome Alliance for Clinical Trials and InternatiOnal Networking registry, among the 446 thrombotic APS, baseline visceral vein thrombosis represented 1% of the patients ( n = 4). In this study, no details are available regarding renal vein thrombosis. 119

Renal artery and vein thrombosis can be either unilateral or bilateral and can lead to acute kidney injury or CKD. Nephrotic range proteinuria and nephrotic syndrome have been described as a presentation of renal vein thrombosis, less frequently with flank pain and macroscopic hematuria in acute onset of thrombosis. 120 , 121 Renal vein thrombosis should be suspected in patients with APS who suddenly develop nephrotic-range proteinuria. Interestingly, some cases reporting renal vein thrombosis associated with primary or secondary APS acknowledge the association with other etiologies of nephrotic syndrome (e.g., membranous nephropathy). 122 Knowing that renal vein thrombosis is a well-known complication of nephrotic syndrome, it would be interesting to further evaluate this association. 123

The EULAR recommendation for the management of APS advocates to treat arterial thrombosis with VKAs, with INR of 2 to 3 or INR of 3 to 4, considering the individual’s risk of bleeding and recurrent thrombosis. In addition, treatment with VKAs with INR of 2 to 3 plus low-dose aspirin can be an option. 124 Recent meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials evaluating DOACs in secondary thromboprophylaxis in APS shows no significant difference in the odds of venous thromboembolism between DOACs and VKAs. However, patients with previous arterial and microvascular disease, or triple-positive patients are at higher risk of recurrence; therefore, DOACs are not recommended in patients with APS. 51

There is no clinical study evaluating treatment or renal prognosis in the setting of renal vein or renal artery thrombosis. However, renal vein thrombosis in the setting of nephrotic syndrome has been associated with higher mortality and kidney disease worsening in the general population. 125

#### Cortical Necrosis and Renal Infarction

Renal infarction is rare in APS, mainly secondary to renal artery thrombosis, either in situ renal artery thrombosis, or emboli from an upstream circulation. 7 Clinical manifestations include abdominal and flank pain, arterial hypertension, and kidney injury. The severity of clinical presentation varies depending on the extension of ischemia. 126 Unilateral and bilateral renal cortical necrosis has been rarely described in primary APS 127 and secondary APS 128 as well as in secondary APS in late pregnancy. 129 Symptoms at presentation include flank pain, hypertension, and kidney failure. 130

#### Other Macrovascular Thrombosis Related to CKD Management

Several studies have shown that aPL positivity is associated with hemodialysis vascular access complications. Ames et al. 131 performed a systematic review and meta-analysis with the aim of evaluating aPL as a risk factor for arteriovenous fistula (AVF) thrombosis. They evaluated LA and IgG aCL by including 1554 and 511 patients, respectively. They found that both IgG aCL and LA were associated with AVF thrombosis. 131 Since then, 3 studies have shown a similar association between AVF thrombosis and LA, IgG and IgM aCL, 132 IgG and IgM aCL, 133 and aPL. 134 IgM aCL as well as IgG and IgM aβ2-GPI are inconsistently associated with AVF thrombosis. Interestingly, some noncriteria aPL such as IgA aβ2-GPI, 135 , 136 IgA aCL, 132 and antiphosphatidyl serin antibody have been associated with AVF thrombosis. 137 , 138

Few cases report on arteriovenous graft thrombosis in aPL-positive patients. 139 Prakash et al. 140 found that IgG aCL was a significant risk factor for arteriovenous graft multiple thrombosis (defines as ≥ 2 episodes of arteriovenous graft thrombosis) in a retrospective study including 74 patients (odds ratio: 3.7 [95% CI: 1.2–11.8; P < 0.04]). In addition, recurrent failure of hemodialysis catheter has been reported in case reports. 141 Some data suggest that aPL-positive patients may be at risk of central venous occlusive disease in hemodialysis, although, these findings should be confirmed in dedicated studies. 142

## Conclusion

The kidney is a major target organ in primary and secondary APS, mainly characterized by thrombosis that can affect any vascular site in the kidneys. aPLN can occur as an acute disease (e.g., TMA) or a chronic vascular disease, based on pathology examination. Evidence-based recommendations for the management of primary or secondary aPLN are currently lacking. There are conflicting results on the efficacy of anticoagulation for the treatment of aPLN. Other treatments, such as mTOR inhibition, complement inhibition, and B-cell depletion are currently under evaluation and have shown some favorable outcomes. aPLN has been associated with unfavorable outcomes in transplants recipients. Both mTOR inhibition with sirolimus and complement inhibition with eculizumab have shown promising results and are under evaluation. There is a need for randomized controlled trials in aPLN as well as the development of new reliable biomarkers of risk stratification.
