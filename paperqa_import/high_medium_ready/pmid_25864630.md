# ABSTRACT

Background: Long-term anticoagulation is recommended in antiphospholipid syndrome with thrombosis in order to prevent recurrences. While the current mainstay relies on vitamin K antagonists, their long-term maintenance may remain challenging.

Objectives: To report on the safety and the efficacy of oral direct inhibitors of thrombin and factor Xa (ODIs) in antiphospholipid syndrome (APS).

Methods: We performed a descriptive analysis of patients with APS enrolled in a French multicentre observational cohort between January 2012 and March 2014 and receiving ODIs. The main outcomes were the occurrence of a thrombotic recurrence or bleeding events.

<!-- page 3 -->
Results: Twenty-six patients with APS (primary in 12) received OIDs. Twenty patients had been previously treated with VKA $(n = 19)$ , or fondaparinux $(n = 1)$ for a median duration of 3 years. OIDs were introduced as second-line therapy because of INR lability/therapeutic simplification $(n = 17)$ , recurrent thrombosis $(n = 1)$ , VKA's associated bleeding event $(n = 1)$ , and atrial fibrillation $(n = 1)$ . Six patients received OIDs as first-line therapy. After a median [IQR] follow-up of 19 [8-29] months, one relapse of arterial thrombosis, two bleeding events (hypermenorrhea and rectal bleeding under rivaroxaban) and one recurrent migraine were reported, leading to discontinuation of therapy in these 4 patients.

Conclusion: ODIs might be an alternative therapeutic option in APS. Prospective studies are warranted to evaluate their safety in this condition.

# 1. Introduction

The antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is defined by a combination of clinical manifestations (arterial and/or venous thrombosis, pregnancy morbidity) and persistently positive tests for antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL) [1,2]. Anticardiolipin (aCL) antibodies, anti-β2 glycoprotein I (anti-β2GPI) antibodies and the lupus anticoagulant (LA) are the laboratory tests included in the revised criteria for the classification of the APS, but other autoantibodies directed to other proteins of the coagulation cascade (i.e. prothrombin and/or phosphatidylserine-prothrombin complexes) might be used [3,4].

Long term anticoagulation is recommended in APS with thrombosis in order to prevent recurrences [5]. The current mainstay relies on vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) but their long term maintenance remains challenging [6,7]. The optimal intensity of VKA-based anticoagulation (i.e., target International Normalized Ratio (INR)) remains controversial, especially in arterial APS. Moreover, the monitoring of VKA's efficacy may be complicated by lupus anticoagulant (LA) interferences on the INR, resulting in variability and misinterpretation of this testing, and in more frequent monitoring affecting the quality of life of the patients. Finally, the management of relapsing APS despite VKA is debated. Because of the activation of platelets and endothelial cells, as well as immune cells (monocytes, B and T lymphocytes), immunomodulatory approaches and targeted therapies have been proposed [8]. In their review of the literature, the APS Treatment Trends Task Force suggested that low-molecular-weight heparin, hydroxychloroquine, which could have self-anticoagulant properties, and other molecules could be used [5]. However, no controlled studies evaluating the use of such treatments have been published to date.

Recently, oral direct inhibitors of thrombin and factor Xa (ODIs) have been approved for the prophylaxis and curative treatment of thrombosis and for atrial fibrillation with several potential advantages compared to VKAs [9,10]. Although some papers have raised the theoretical interest of OIDs in APS [11], only small series are available [12-14], suggesting that a few patients with antiphospholipid syndrome could experience thrombotic recurrences when switching from VKAs to OIDs. Also, the large randomised open label trial Rivaroxaban in AntiPhospholipid Syndrome (RAPS) is still ongoing. The primary aim of this international study is to show the non-inferiority of rivaroxaban vs. warfarin in patients with APS (with or without associated lupus) following at least one episode of thrombosis. However, there is no data to date to recommend the use of OIDs in APS patients with recurrence of venous thrombosis occurring on VKAs, or in arterial manifestations of APS [5]. Herein, we report on the safety and efficacy of OIDs in 26 APS patients from a French multicentre observational cohort.

# 2. Patients and methods

Twenty-six patients (14 women, 12 men) with definite APS and treated with ODIs were enrolled in a French multicentre observational

cohort between January 2012 and March 2014. APS was defined according to the Sydney criteria [1].

ODIs included oral direct thrombin inhibitor (dabigatran etexilate) or direct factor Xa inhibitor (rivaroxaban). We retrospectively collected clinical data (gender, age, history of APS and clinical manifestations, associated comorbidities), biological data (type of antibodies defining APS, associated autoimmune diseases, renal function, platelet count), and therapeutic data (history of VKA therapy prior to ODI introduction, reason for switching/introduction, reason for discontinuation - if applicable).

The main primary outcome was the occurrence of a thrombotic recurrence. Secondary outcomes included adverse effects, and biological tolerance. Kaplan-Meier survival analyses were performed to take into account censored data, using therapeutic interruptions/modifications as events.

# 3.3. Follow-up and outcome

As shown in Fig. 1, four patients withdrew the treatment during a median follow-up of 19 [8-29] months. One relapse of thrombosis was observed (cutaneous microangiopathy, patient #10). Two bleeding events were reported in patients with normal renal function, leading to discontinuation of therapy. Patient #11 presented with hypermenorrhea under rivaroxaban after one month. Patient #16 experienced early recurrence of a rectal polyp bleeding after 1 month of therapy. Rivaroxaban had been introduced in the latter case because of this rectal bleeding occurring with warfarin $(\mathrm{INR} = 2)$ . One patient also discontinued rivaroxaban because of recurrent migraine (#6). No gastro-intestinal tract disorders or dizziness, and no liver or renal impairment were reported. Overall, the event-free survival rate was $87.9\%$ at 12 months and $82.4\%$ at 24 months using Kaplan-Meier survival curve analysis (Fig. 1). D-Dimer dosage, performed in 7 patients during the follow-up, remained within normal range.

# 4. Discussion

# 4.1. Limitations of VKA's maintenance in antiphospholipid syndrome

Our results suggest that ODIs could serve as an effective alternative to VKAs in APS. Although VKAs are the mainstay of APS therapy [5],

some concerns have been raised regarding their long-term maintenance in this condition. Firstly, the optimal intensity of anticoagulation is still controversial. Some historical studies had prompted to yield a higher intensity of VKA therapy to prevent recurrences [7], but two prospective studies have suggested that an INR between 2 and 3 could have similar effects with less bleeding complications [6,15]. Secondly, VKA's efficacy varies widely according to genetic or environmental factors (diet or disease states) and to drug-drug interactions, such as high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone, frequently used in SLE [16]. Thirdly, thromboplastins are variously responsive to antiphospholipid antibodies, and such interferences might perturb INR monitoring in some APS patients [17,18], requiring more frequent monitoring. Since APS occurs mainly in young patients, such constraints in the perspective of life-long VKA's anticoagulation might alter the patient's quality-of-life and reduce the therapeutic adherence.

# 4.2. Efficacy of ODIs in APS

ODIs differ from other anticoagulants, in that they directly antagonize a specific target of the final clotting pathway, namely thrombin (dabigatran) or factor Xa (rivaroxaban), independently of cofactors such as platelet factor 4 or antithrombin. They have a rapid onset of action when compared with VKAs, and they do not require biological monitoring of the anticoagulant intensity [19].

Recurrences of thrombosis despite VKAs occur in up to $5 - 20\%$ of APS patients [5,6]. In such situations, the APS Treatment Trends Task Force [5] suggests the use of low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), hydroxychloroquine and statins as additional therapies despite poor levels of evidence. The use of ODIs has been suggested in this condition but only few data are available [12-14]. Their efficacy has been previously investigated in various conditions. Studies have assessed the non-inferiority of dabigatran (150 mg twice daily) versus warfarin for the curative treatment of acute venous thromboembolism [10].

Einstein-DVT and Einstein-PE studies have allowed labelled indications for rivaroxaban in the treatment of deep vein thrombosis (DVT), and prevention of recurrent DVT and pulmonary embolism (PE) following an acute DVT in adults [9].

In the context of APS, concerns have been raised in a recent case series of three patients from the Mayo Medical Center diagnosed with antiphospholipid syndrome [12]. In these patients previously treated with VKAs, switching to dabigatran (1 patient) or rivaroxaban (2 patients) led to thrombotic relapses. Also, in another recent study of 12 APS patients treated with rivaroxaban [13], 2 patients experienced thrombotic relapses after switching from VKA to OIDs. Win and Rodgers also reported on 3 APS patients experiencing thrombotic relapses on rivaroxaban $(n = 2)$ or dabigatran $(n = 1)$ [14]. In these two former studies [13,14], the patients had high risk of thrombotic recurrence, i.e. severe presentations of APS with triple antibody positivity, or had experienced many recurrences when being on VKAs. In this context, the adherence to the treatment (either VKA or ODI) is to be taken into account for the analysis of the thrombotic risk. However, it is suggested that the severity of APS manifestations also depends on the antibody "profile" of patients [20]. Patients with LA have a 6-fold increased risk of venous thrombosis relative to LA-negative APS patients, and similar trends are found for patients bearing aCL antibodies as compared with aCL-negative patients. Moreover, triple-positive patients (i.e., patients with LA, aCL and aβ2GPI Abs) seem to be at higher thrombotic risk than patients with either one or two APS antibodies [4,20]. In our series, 7 patients were triple positive, whereas 11 patients had a single antiphospholipid antibody leading to the diagnosis. Only one patient (#10, bearing 2 positive antibodies (LA, aCL)) out of 26 had proven failure of rivaroxaban to prevent thrombotic recurrences. However, other autoantibodies directed to other proteins of the coagulation cascade (i.e. prothrombin and/or phosphatidylserine-prothrombin complexes) or their complex with phospholipids other than cardiolipin, or to some domains of β2GPI, are potentially relevant to APS, as well as other isotypes than IgG/IgM aCL/aβ2GPI. These antibodies might be interesting in order to evaluate the potential risk of recurrences, but were not available in our series. These elements have to be considered in the interpretation of the thrombotic risk in further studies.

# 4.3. Safety concerns of ODIs in the context of APS

One major concern is the safety of ODIs. In our study, two patients experienced early bleeding events leading to rivaroxaban's discontinuation, even if the second patient had confounding factors [thrombocytopaenia, preexisting bleeding rectal polyp, high dosage of rivaroxaban (15 mg bid)]. One patient also experienced recurrent

migraine, which could not strictly be attributed to ODI rather than to APS itself. The rate of adverse events usually observed in APS patients treated with VKAs is $3\%$ /person-year [21], ranging from 0.5 to $10\%$ with a minor/major bleeding event ratio of 2.5-3 [6,15,21]. The REMEDY/RE-SONATE studies have shown that the rate of major bleeding with dabigatran was significantly lower than with warfarin [10]. Overall, we show here that $87.9\%$ and $82.4\%$ of APS patients maintain ODIs after 1 and 2 years, respectively.

However, one should notice some limitations to these therapeutic regimens. First, elimination of dabigatran as well as of rivaroxaban depends on the renal function [22], contraindicating their use in patients with severe kidney impairment. Second, no commercial antidote is yet available in case of major bleeding event or urgent surgical procedure. Also, LA detection in patients treated by ODIs is impossible by usually recommended coagulation tests [3,23]. Finally, a potential concern is raised regarding APS associated thrombocytopaenia, which may increase the risk of bleeding, as well as potential drug-drug interactions.

# 4.4. Study limitations

We acknowledge some limitations in our study. First, our analysis was performed as a retrospective review. Anyway, the patients were regularly followed-up and no recurrence of thrombosis or major bleeding should have been missed. Second, our short follow-up might thus have limited our ability to observe thrombotic relapses.

# 4.5. Conclusion

Taken together, OIDs might be considered as an alternative therapeutic option in APS, especially for patients with INR lability. Safety concerns are raised as discontinuation of OIDs related to bleeding was observed in 2 patients from our real-life study. Results from prospective long terms studies are warranted to define novel guidelines of APS management in the era of novel anticoagulant therapies.

# Take-home messages

- The current mainstay of antiphospholipid syndrome relies on vitamin K antagonists (VKAs), but their maintenance remains challenging, especially in young people.   
- ODIs might be considered as an alternative therapeutic option in APS, especially for patients with INR lability.   
- One recurrence of thrombosis was observed on rivaroxaban in 1 out of 26 patients with a follow-up of 19 [8-29] months.   
- The bleeding risk of APS patients must be strictly evaluated before initiation of OIDs.
