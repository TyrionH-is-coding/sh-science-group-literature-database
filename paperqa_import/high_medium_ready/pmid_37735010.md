# Abstract:

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a chronic autoimmune disease involving vascular thrombosis and/or obstetric morbidity and persistent antibodies to phospholipids or certain phospholipid-associated proteins. It is a rare condition in adults and even rarer in children. The diagnosis of APS can be facilitated by the use of classification criteria based on a combination of clinical and biological features.

APS may be rapidly progressive with multiple, often synchronous thromboses, resulting in life-threatening multiple organ failure. This form is known as "catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome" (CAPS).

It may be primary or associated with systemic lupus erythematosus (associated APS) and in very rare cases with other systemic autoimmune diseases.

General practitioners and paediatricians may encounter APS in patients with one or more vascular thromboses. Because APS is so rare and difficult to diagnosis (risk of overdiagnosis) any suspected case should be confirmed rapidly and sometimes urgently by an APS specialist. First-line treatment of thrombotic events in APS includes heparin followed by long-term anticoagulation with a VKA, usually warfarin. Except in the specific case of stroke, anticoagulants should be started as early as possible. Any temporary discontinuation of anticoagulants is associated with a high risk of thrombosis in APS.

A reference/competence centre specialised in autoimmune diseases must be urgently consulted for the therapeutic management of CAPS.

Keywords: anti-phospholipid syndrome, catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome, treatment, management

# 1. Summary for the general practitioner

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an autoimmune disease involving vascular thrombosis and/or obstetric morbidity and persistent antibodies to phospholipids or certain phospholipid-associated proteins. APS is rare in adults and even rarer in children. Its diagnosis is facilitated by using a classification system<sup>1</sup> based on a combination of clinical features and biological criteria:

# Clinical features:

- Vascular thrombosis: any documented arterial, venous or microcirculatory thrombosis, either with a typical clinical presentation, such as deep vein thrombosis of the lower limbs or a pulmonary embolism, or presumed thrombosis at an unusual site (e.g. aseptic osteonecrosis, haemorrhagic adrenal infarction, nasal septal perforation, alveolar haemorrhage, and Budd-Chiari syndrome).

O Obstetrical event:

One or more unexplained deaths in a morphologically normal fetus at or beyond the 10th week of gestation, with normal fetal morphology documented by ultrasound or by direct examination of the fetus,

or

One or more premature births of a morphologically normal neonate before the 34th week of gestation due to:

- eclampsia or severe pre-eclampsia,

or

- recognized features of placental insufficiency,

or

- Three or more unexplained consecutive spontaneous abortions before the 10th week of gestation, after exclusion of maternal anatomical or hormonal abnormalities and paternal and maternal chromosomal causes.

# Biological criteria:

The presence of at least one anti-phospholipid antibody (aPL) on at least two occasions 12 weeks apart, in particular:

Lupus anticoagulant $(\mathrm{LA})^2$ (or formerly anti-prothrombinase),   
$\mathrm{OgG}$ or IgM anti-beta 2 glycoprotein I (aβ2GPI) at moderate or high titres $(>99^{\mathrm{th}}$ percentile) measured by a standardised ELISA,   
○ Anti-cardiolipin IgG or IgM (aCL) at moderate or high titres (> 40 IgG phospholipid units [GPL] or IgM phospholipid units [MPL], or > $99^{\text{th}}$ percentile), measured by a standardised ELISA.

Not all aPLs have the same thrombotic risk. The thrombotic risk may be stratified according to positive aPL tests (LA, aCL or $\alpha 2\mathrm{GPI}$ ), their isotype and titre. Other clinical disorders that may be found and are associated with APL include:

- Neurological: seizures, chorea, cognitive impairment, vascular dementia, transverse myelopathy and cerebral white matter hypersignals.   
- Cardiological: diffuse or localized valve retractions and/or thickenings, and, more rarely, pseudovegetations (Libman-Sacks verrucous endocarditis), early coronary disease, diastolic or systolic dysfunction associated with hypertrophy of the left ventricular wall or, on the contrary, dilatation of the cardiac cavities, pulmonary hypertension.   
- Dermatological: livedo racemosa, necrotic purpura, skin ulcerations, dry gangrene, livedoid vasculopathy, subungual splinter haemorrhages, white atrophy often very painful, anetoderma.   
- Renal: chronic occlusive disease of the small arterial vessels of the kidneys ("anti-phospholipid associated nephropathy").   
- Haematological: peripheral thrombocytopenia, mechanical or autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, exceptionally bone marrow necrosis with central pancytopenia.   
- Osteoarticular: aseptic osteonecrosis, perforation of the nasal septum.

<!-- page 9 -->
In rare cases APS is rapidly progressive with multiple, often synchronous thromboses, resulting in life-threatening multiorgan failure, known as "catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome" (CAPS). CAPS is defined as the occurrence of at least 3 organ/tissue/system attacks within 7 days, with evidence of small vein occlusion on biopsy in patients with a diagnosis of APS based on current criteria.

APS can be primary or associated with systemic lupus (associated APS) and even less frequently with other autoimmune diseases.

# 1.1. APS with thrombotic events (thrombotic APS)

# 1.1.1. Initial assessment

General practitioners and paediatricians may encounter APS in patients with one or more vascular thromboses. Because it is so rare and difficult to diagnose (risk of overdiagnosis) a physician specialised in APS (at reference centres, centres of excellence, expert centres and their networks) should be rapidly consulted in any suspected cases.

The diagnosis of APS is based on a system that combines at least one clinical and one biological criterion (see above).

Following the diagnosis of APS the following steps must be taken:

Assess the phenotype:

Clinical: isolated obstetrical forms (early spontaneous miscarriage? pathology in the $2^{\mathrm{nd}}$ trimester?), venous, arterial forms or even CAPS at the outset.   
Biological: type of antibody? Triple positivity?

- Establish treatment.

- Educate the patient about vitamin K antagonist (VKA) management (bleeding risk, drug combinations), possible risk of severe forms (warning signs). Anticipate risk situations (interruption of anticoagulants, surgery, pregnancy, infection).   
- Identify and manage cardiovascular risk factors.   
- Test for associated systemic lupus or other autoimmune diseases.   
- Draft a treatment plan with the patient or his/her family (long term condition off list, transport, etc.).   
- Inform the patient or family about any patient associations.

# 1.1.2. Therapeutic education management

# 1.1.2.1. Objectives

Short term:

$\mathrm{O}$ Resolve the thrombotic event.   
○ Provide a solution to functional or life-threatening events.

Intermediate term:

Prevent additional thrombotic events (secondary prevention).   
○ Anticipate and plan possible pregnancies (contraception, pre-conception consultation).   
o Preserve the patient's quality of life and socio-professional and educational integration.

- Long term:

$\circ$ Limit the effects of the disease.   
$\circ$ Limit the cumulative harmful effects of treatment.   
Prevent secondary cardiovascular complications, mainly early atherosclerosis.

# 1.1.2.2. Treatment of thrombotic APS

Management is based on drug treatment, monitoring and therapeutic education.

# 1.1.2.2.1. Anticoagulant and antiplatelet drugs

First-line treatment of APS-related thrombotic events includes heparin followed by long-term anticoagulants with a VKA, usually warfarin. Anticoagulants should be started as soon as possible, except in the specific case of stroke. The switch to VKAs should then begin as soon as possible, except if the patient has recently undergone a biopsy.

Long-term secondary prophylaxis with VKAs prevents recurrent thrombosis. Temporary discontinuation of anticoagulants is associated with a high-risk recurrent thrombosis in APS. Although the target INR for VKAs is 2 to 3, some teams prefer a prophylactic target INR of 3 to 4 in cases of arterial APS and others combine AVK with a target INR of 2 to 3 and an antiplatelet. At present treatment with direct oral anticoagulants ([DOACs] notably dabigatran, apixaban, rivaroxaban) is usually not recommended because of a high risk of recurrent thrombosis.

In patients with arterial ischaemic stroke, anticoagulants should be initiated after analysing the thrombo-embolic and haemorrhagic risks in consultation with a neurovascular team.

A reference/competence centre specialised in autoimmune diseases should always and urgently be consulted for the therapeutic management of CAPS.

# 1.1.2.2.2. Therapeutic patient education

Therapeutic patient education (TPE) ensures that the patient understands the disease, its treatment and its complications. A referent specialist should provide the patient with this information. Therapeutic education is especially important in APS to obtain good adherence to treatment as well regular monitoring. Management of anticoagulants is particularly rigorous and specialised in patients with APS, especially in case of CAPS.

# 1.1.3. Follow-up

<!-- page 11 -->
The frequency of consultations varies according to the initial disease severity, associated disorders and/or associated events. If necessary, systematic follow-up can be organised every 6 to 12 months to assess disease progression, evaluate the anticoagulant treatment (efficacy, side effects, adherence to VKAs, history of INRs and percentage of INRs within the therapeutic target), to prescribe additional tests if necessary (especially in the presence of associated events), and to review other treatments (contraception, hormonal treatment).

Surgery and other invasive procedures should be carefully monitored, keeping the anticoagulant discontinuation window as short as possible, even if the patient must be hospitalised throughout the VKA-heparin regimen.

# 1.2. Obstetric APS (Contraception / Pregnancy / Breastfeeding)

# 1.2.1. APS and pregnancy

APS is a disease that mainly affects young women and there is a high risk of complications during pregnancy. Pregnancy in patients with APS is still associated with higher maternal and foetal morbidity and mortality than that in the general population. Complications, mainly related to placental insufficiency, include obstetric foetal complications (foetal deaths in utero, in utero growth retardation [IUGR], prematurity), and maternal complications (pre-eclampsia, Haemolysis, Elevated Liver enzyme, Low Platelets [HELLP] syndrome, caesarean section, CAPS). Repeated spontaneous miscarriages may also occur. Patients with associated Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) may have flares, and neonatal lupus may develop in case of associated anti-SSA and/or anti-SSB antibodies.

The management of these pregnancies is optimised by planning, ideally during a preconception consultation, and with multidisciplinary expert teams (reference/competence centre specialised in autoimmune diseases including APS), with consultations at least once a month in these "high-risk pregnancies".

# 1.2.2. APS and hormone treatments

# 1.2.2.1. Contraception

Contraception involving oestrogens (whatever the method of administration) is contraindicated in patients with APS because of the increased risk of thrombosis. Oral or implantable progestin, intrauterine devices (IUDs), including those delivering small doses of levonorgestrel, can be used in APS. Emergency contraception with levonorgestrel ("morning-after pill") can be prescribed.

# 1.2.2.2. Other

Alternatives to chlormadinone acetate and nomegestrol acetate (progestins used in the management of menopause, certain menstrual disorders and endometriosis as well as for contraceptive purposes) should be offered because of the increased risk of intracranial meningioma after 6 months of exposure.

Certain endometriosis treatments (such as medroxyprogesterone acetate) increase the risk of thrombosis and are not recommended.

# 1.2.3. APS and breastfeeding

<!-- page 12 -->
Breastfeeding is usually possible, but treatment compatibility should be confirmed. The Teratogen Reference Centre (CRAT - www.lecrat.fr) website provides up-to-date information and can be consulted.

# 2. Introduction [1-2]

# 2.1. Objectives of the French National Diagnostic and Care Protocol

# 2.1.1. Objectives

The objective of this national diagnosis and care protocol (NDCP) is to provide healthcare professionals with a description of the current optimal diagnostic and therapeutic management and the care pathway for patients with APS. The goal of this NDCP is to optimise and harmonise the management and follow-up protocols for this rare disease throughout the country. It also identifies drugs used for an indication that has not received Marketing Approval as well as specialties, products, or services necessary for patient care but not usually covered or reimbursed

This NDCP can be used as a reference by the general practitioner in consultation with other specialists.

However, the NDCP cannot take into consideration all specific cases, comorbidities or complications, therapeutic specificities or all hospital care protocols. It does not claim to cover all possible management approaches or to replace the physician's individual responsibility to his patient. Nevertheless, this protocol describes the reference standard of care for a patient with APS. It should be updated according to new validated data.

# 2.1.2. Working method

This French NDCP has been drafted according to the "Method for drafting a national protocol for diagnosis and care of rare diseases" published by the French National Authority for health (HAS) in 2012 (methodological guide available on the HAS website: www.hassante.fr).

# 3. Diagnosis and evaluation [3-18]

# 3.1. Main objectives

To make the diagnosis of APS.   
- To announce the diagnosis and present the different aspects of management.   
To assess disease severity.   
- To test for associated conditions, including autoimmune, neoplastic or infectious diseases to differentiate primary APS from APS associated with another autoimmune condition, usually systemic lupus.   
To determine treatment.

# 3.2. Professionals involved (and coordination arrangements)

A patient with APS should initially be managed by a physician with experience in this disease, often under emergency conditions. Management should be multidisciplinary and coordinated by specialists and reference hospital physicians and/or physicians from the competence centre (FAIPR network) in association with the attending physician.

The clinical diagnosis of APS is usually made by adult physicians or paediatric internist/rheumatologists and vascular physicians, obstetrician-gynaecologists and specialists in haemostasis, because it usually presents as an isolated thrombosis in a young patient or associated with another autoimmune disease, usually systemic lupus. Depending on the organs involved other specialists may play a role including: neurologists, neuropaediatricians, nephrologists, nephron-paediatricians, pneumologists, cardiologists, intensivists, etc.).

Obstetricians provide specific care to patients during pregnancy and maternity, while paediatricians manage new-borns.

Anaesthetists must adjust anticoagulant treatment pre-, peri- and post- operatively or per partum.

Haemostasis and immunology biologists confirm the diagnosis by identifying the biological markers described below.

# 3.3. Presentation

# 3.3.1. Classic signs in adults

APS usually presents as two clinical events: thrombotic and obstetric, which may coexist.

The disease may be isolated (primary APS) or associated with a systemic autoimmune disease (associated APS), usually systemic lupus (20% of patients with systemic lupus have APS).

# 3.3.1.1. Thrombotic events

Thrombotic events can affect all vessels in all organs (Table 1).

Although usually only one vascular component, (venous or arterial), is affected, about $20\%$ of patients present with both arterial and venous signs, often at different times.

Venous thromboembolisms are the presenting feature at least $40\%$ of patients. Although superficial thrombophlebitis is not used to classify APS, some patients with this condition may develop deep vein thrombosis or other conditions on the APS spectrum.

<!-- page 15 -->
Arterial thrombosis may occur, mainly affecting the cerebral arteries (at least $25\%$ of patients). Coronary arteries and other peripheral arteries are involved in fewer than $10\%$ of cases.

Microvascular ischaemia may occur in the skin, kidneys, heart, etc.

Catastrophic anti-phospholipid syndrome (CAPS) is a specific form of thrombotic APS. CAPS presents as multiple thromboses that occur within a limited time period, and mainly affect the microcirculation leading to single or multiple organ failures. CAPS is related to thrombotic microangiopathies and is discussed in Chapter 4.

# 3.3.1.2. Obstetrical events

Obstetrical events may be isolated, with no thrombotic events, associated with past or future thrombotic events, or with non-APS specific events.

The obstetrical signs of APS can be classified into two groups that may occur in the same patient:

- One or more unexplained deaths of a morphologically normal fetus at or beyond the 10th week of gestation, with normal fetal morphology documented by ultrasound or by direct examination of the fetus,   
One or more premature births of a morphologically normal neonate before the 34th week of gestation because of:

eclampsia or severe pre-eclampsia, or   
recognized features of placental insufficiency, or   
○ three or more unexplained consecutive spontaneous abortions before the 10th week of gestation, after excluding maternal anatomical or hormonal abnormalities and paternal and maternal chromosomal causes.

These phenotypes (spontaneous miscarriage vs placental vascular insufficiency) are different and are related to different pathophysiological mechanisms.

# 3.3.2. Other clinical signs not included in Sidney's classification criteria for APS

For certain features of APS there is no clinical presence of thrombosis. These events may be related to thrombotic mechanisms that cannot be identified or easily identified. These APS-related signs have no obvious relationship with thrombosis and may be due to:

- Direct damage to the arterial microvessel wall (due to activation/proliferation of endothelial cells or parietal smooth muscle fibres).   
- Complement activation.   
- A directly deleterious aPLs effect on cell targets (for example, on neurons in epilepsy).

Specific therapeutic management of these signs must be managed by the expert centre.

# 3.3.2.1. Neurological signs

Many "specific" neurological signs can occur during the course of APS: seizures, chorea, cognitive impairment, vascular dementia, transverse myelopathy and white matter hypersignals.

<!-- page 16 -->
The direct relationship between APS and headache and/or migraines and acute or chronic psychosis has not been established.

If a clinical or radiological picture suggests inflammatory/demyelinating central nervous system disease, systemic lupus erythematosus, multiple sclerosis and neuromyelitis optica should be excluded before considering APS. Ischaemic myelopathies are possible during APS.

# 3.3.2.2. Cardiac manifestations

Non-thrombotic cardiac manifestations are mainly valvulopathies associated with varying degrees of diffuse or localised valve retraction and/or thickening, and less frequently with vegetations (Libman-Sacks endocarditis). All four valves may be involved but mitral and aortic valves are the most common locations. These patients have a risk of complications such as arterial embolism (in particular cerebral), valve failure (more rarely valve stenosis) and infective endocarditis (rare). The main differential diagnoses of valvular disease in APS are rheumatic fever and infective endocarditis.

Other APS-related cardiac signs include:

- Early coronary atherosclerosis, considered in the case of coronary disease in young people, and to be distinguished from coronary thrombosis in healthy vessels (very rare).   
- Diastolic or systolic dysfunction associated with hypertrophy of the left ventricular wall or, on the contrary, dilation of the cardiac chambers. Myocardial microcirculation disorders with fibrosis may sometimes be associated with severe damage.   
Pulmonary hypertension.

# 3.3.2.3. Dermatological manifestations

This paragraph only presents complications that are not the result of direct thrombotic phenomena as described in paragraph 4.1.

- Livedo racemosa (large, irregular, unclosed, relatively fine mesh), frequently associated with arterial phenotype and valvulopathy, in contrast to livedo reticularis (small closed mesh).   
- Livedoid vasculopathy.   
- Subungual flare haemorrhages.   
- White atrophy, often very painful.   
- Anetoderma (localized loss of elastic tissue) (rare).

# 3.3.2.4. Renal manifestations

Chronic progressive occlusive disease of the small arterial vessels of the kidney, also called anti-phospholipid nephropathy, is the most common renal condition associated with aPl.

This complication is secondary to progressive proliferative and obstructive intimal lesions of the interlobular arteries and arterioles. It can lead to progressive destruction of the kidney.

The clinical signs are those of vascular nephropathy including arterial hypertension, impaired renal function (initially moderate but very slowly resulting in end-stage renal failure) and proteinuria that may exceed $1\mathrm{g} / 24\mathrm{h}$ .

A renal biopsy shows arteriolar lesions (arteriosclerosis, fibrous intimal hyperplasia of the interlobular arteries and preglomerular arterioles, as well as fibrous or fibrocellular arterial

<!-- page 17 -->
and arteriolar occlusions that may be recanalised). The downstream consequences of ischemia may also be seen on the glomerulus and renal parenchyma (focal cortical atrophy with triangular interstitial fibrosis with a base applied to the renal capsule, tubular atrophy with a pseudo-thyroidal appearance and small sclerotic glomeruli or, on the contrary, dilated glomeruli with atrophy of the flocculus). All of these strongly suggest APS.

APS-related nephropathies may also present as an acute episode resembling a thrombotic arterial microangiopathy that is sometimes associated with haematological signs (thrombocytopenia and mechanical haemolytic anaemia). Thrombi are visible in small arterial vessels on biopsy. This is very common in CAPS.

In rare cases renal artery stenosis may cause severe hypertension or associated membranous glomerulonephritis.

# 3.3.2.5. Haematological manifestations

Peripheral thrombocytopenia is present in $10\%$ to $30\%$ of patients with APS and in $60\%$ to $90\%$ of those with CAPS.

Mechanical haemolytic anaemia (direct antiglobulin test, formerly the direct Coombs test, negative) may occur, especially in CAPS.

Immunological thrombocytopenic purpura and autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (positive direct antiglobulin test) is fairly frequent in APS.

# 3.4. Tests for aPL

# 3.4.1. There are three main types of aPLs

# 3.4.1.1. Lupus anticoagulant (LA)

The presence of the LA is usually associated with a prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), although a normal APTT may also rarely occur.

The LA is detected on citrated plasma following double centrifugation in the following steps.

- LA is first screened for by showing prolonged phospholipid-dependent coagulation test-times on two tests based on different assays. The diluted Russell viper venom time (dRVVT) and the APTT with an LA-sensitive reagent are recommended.   
- The inhibitory effect of LA is then demonstrated by a mixture test showing no or incomplete correction of the screening test prolongation (assessed by the circulating anticoagulant index, formerly Rosner).   
- Finally, phospholipid dependence is confirmed (shortening of screening tests after phospholipid enrichment).

False positives and false negatives may occur in the following cases:

- A false positive may occur if C-reactive protein (CRP) and fibrinogen are elevated. Persistent LA should be confirmed a certain time after the thrombotic event and any inflammatory syndrome.   
- A false negative test may occur due to an increase in factor VIII levels during pregnancy. In the presence of a negative LA during pregnancy, the test should be repeated after the $3^{\mathrm{rd}}$ postpartum month.   
- Anticoagulants may be associated with false positive or negative LA results. Thus, ideally, LA should be tested for in the absence of any anticoagulant treatment.

<!-- page 18 -->
However, in practice, if necessary, it may be performed in patients treated with anticoagulants, but the following precautions must be taken:

The nature, dose and time of administration of the patient's anticoagulant treatment must be recorded by the prescriber of the test so that it can be taken into account by the biologist, both to perform and interpret the test.

Patients on VKAs:

- INR $< {1.50}$ : test performed according to the usual procedure.   
- $1.50 \leq \mathrm{INR} \leq 3.00$ : test performed directly on $\mathrm{P} + \mathrm{C}$ mixture (patient + control), with adapted positivity threshold and mentioning in the results the risk of false negative if LA is low).

- INR $>3.00$ : LA cannot be performed

Patients on heparin:

- Low molecular weight heparin (LMWH): the sample must be taken just before the next LMWH injection (at least 11 hours or 23 hours after the last injection depending on whether the treatment is in two injections per day or a single injection). Then anti-Xa activity is measured by the biologist to assess the feasibility of the LA test.   
- Unfractionated heparin (UFH): most dRVVT reagents (not APTT) contain a heparin inhibitor and the biologist must measure heparin levels by anti-Xa activity to ensure that the patient's anti-Xa does not exceed the neutralising capabilities of the reagent used.   
- DOAC: the presence of even very low concentrations of DOAC is associated with a significant risk of LA false positives, particularly with dRVVT. Thus, LA test should not be performed in the very small group of patients treated with DOACs, because discontinuing this treatment is associated with high risk of APS recurrence.

# 3.4.1.2. aCL and $a\beta 2GP1$ antibodies

aCL and aβ2GP1 are detected in serum or plasma by conventional ELISA tests or by more recent immunological tests (chemiluminescence, multiplex beads...). The results of immunological tests are not influenced by anticoagulants and can be performed on a small sample. In contrast to coagulation tests for LA, samples can be thawed and refrozen several times without significantly altering the immunoassay result [4]. The presence of rheumatoid factor, hypergammaglobulinemia or monoclonal gammopathy may interfere with the IgM aCL test.

The significant levels of aCL and $\mathsf{a}\beta 2\mathsf{GP1}$ are:

- aCL isotype IgG and/or IgM in serum or plasma present with levels $>40$ GPL, or $>40$ MPL, or $>99^{\text{th}}$ percentile, measured by a standardised ELISA.   
- $\beta 2\mathrm{GP1}$ antibodies of IgG and/or IgM isotype in serum or plasma with levels $>99^{\mathrm{th}}$ percentile, measured by a standardised ELISA.

Although there are other tests, their value has not been confirmed in current practice.

# 3.4.2. Hypoprothrombinemia-LA syndrome

In rare cases, LA may be associated with an acquired factor II deficiency (hypoprothrombinemia-LA syndrome), which is associated with very frequent bleeding and venous or arterial thrombosis. Factor II levels are often very low (median value: $10\%$ ).

# 3.5.1. Diagnosis of APS

APS may be diagnosed in the presence of at least one biological criterion of APS that persists for at least twelve weeks after the first test result and a clinical sign of APS, if there is no more than 5 years between the clinical event and the positive aPL biology (see paragraph 4).

However, no formal diagnostic criteria exist for APS, and it may be diagnosed in certain rare situations that do not strictly fulfil the classification criteria. - Patients with unexplained venous thrombosis or with minor triggers, especially if the patient is under 50 years old.   
- Phlebitis at atypical sites.

- Extracerebral arterial thrombosis in a patient under 50 years old.   
- Microvascular thrombosis.

# 4. Catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (CAPS) [50-77]

# 4.1. Definition: classification criteria

APS can present in a rapidly progressive form, comparable to a "thrombotic storm", responsible for multiple thromboses, often synchronous, which may cause life-threatening multi-organ failure. This syndrome was described in 1992 and named "Catastrophic Antiphospholipid Syndrome" (CAPS). It is defined as the occurrence of at least three organ/tissue/system attacks within seven days, with biopsy evidence of small vessel occlusion, in patients with APS confirmed by the current classification criteria. Classification criteria were established in an international consensus meeting in 2003 and revised in 2010 (Box 1). CAPS is a rare complication and the most severe form of APS. It is thought to affect approximately $1 - 5\%$ of patients with APS.

Any new biological or clinical signs (increase in livedo, skin necrosis, hypertension, etc.) in particular thrombocytopenia during an anticoagulant switch or window period, should suggest early CAPS.

# 4.2. Limitations of the classification criteria

CAPS classification criteria were developed in the early 2000s to raise awareness of this little-known complication of APS as well as to create homogeneous groups of patients for collaborative studies. Although they should be used to help confirm the diagnosis, all criteria need not be met before making the diagnosis and beginning treatment. Indeed, CAPS criteria are not intended to be used as diagnostic criteria, but in practice they often are. In particular, obtaining the histological results of CAPS criteria is difficult in emergency situations and often impossible due to the need for curative anticoagulants and frequent thrombocytopenia. A skin biopsy should be performed in these cases as soon as possible and is usually informative. Thus, while the criteria to confirm CAPS are rarely met, patients should fulfil the criteria for probable CAPS. Indeed, certain patients, especially if they are identified very early, do not fit into either category and are called pre-CAPS or near-CAPS. A recent study has shown that CAPS criteria do not predict mortality in patients with APS admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) with a new thrombotic event [6].

# 4.3. Mode of discovery and triggers

CAPS may complicate an existing case of APS or be the initial presentation. In the latter cases the diagnosis is extremely difficult because the presence of aPLs antibodies is unknown and a diagnosis is urgent. A triggering factor is found in $65 - 80\%$ of patients. The most common triggering factors are: infection, surgery or any situation in which anticoagulant treatment is interrupted or modified (bleeding complication from anticoagulants, VKA-heparin switch, and introduction of a DOAC to replace heparin 'or VKA, especially in triple positive APS). Other triggers include cancer, the introduction of oestrogen-based contraception, pregnancy, and especially the peri- and post-partum period (especially if the pregnancy is complicated by a HELLP syndrome), and SLE flare. In practice, any situation associated with a decrease in, or interruption of anticoagulants in a patient with APS (especially those with a high thrombotic risk such as triple-positive patients) can trigger CAPS. Surgery and other invasive procedures should be carefully monitored and the anticoagulation window should be kept as short as possible, even if the patient must be hospitalised for the duration of the VKA-heparin switch. Similarly, a bleeding complication exposes patients to the interruption of anticoagulants and therefore to CAPS.

<!-- page 25 -->
# 4.4. Different organ damage

CAPS was initially defined as the occurrence of microvascular thrombosis in patients with APS. However, it has recently been shown that macrovascular venous or arterial thrombosis is found in over $25 - 75\%$ of probable/certain CAPS.

Microvascular thrombosis can involve all organs and manifest as organ failure. Diagnosis is difficult and although a histological confirmation is the only way to be certain, it is often impossible due to anticoagulants, thrombocytopenia or to the patient's clinical status. Imaging, such as CT or MRI, can help in the diagnosis. Thrombocytopenia and mechanical haemolytic anaemia are also suggestive.

The most frequently affected organ is the kidney, defined in the CAPS consensus [52] as: an increase $>50\%$ in plasma creatinine level, severe hypertension $(>180/100\mathrm{mHg})$ and/or proteinuria $>500\mathrm{mg}/24\mathrm{h}$ .

Myocardial damage results in microvascular infarctions in healthy coronary arteries (MINOCA for myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries) or with a few atheromatous lesions. It is not found in a single coronary territory on imaging (late gadolinium retention on cardiac MRI) and may lead to cardiogenic shock due to myocardial failure. Emergency cardiac imaging (trans-thoracic echocardiogram [TTE], MRI, coronary angiography, coroscanner) should be performed in the presence of elevated cardiac markers (troponin and NT-ProBNP) to look for signs of ischaemia.

Diffuse cerebral ischaemic damage may occur. Cerebral MRI is the examination of choice in these cases.

Adrenal gland involvement can lead to haemorrhagic adrenal necrosis, often bilateral, with acute adrenal insufficiency (10-36% of CAPS patients).

Pulmonary microvascular damage presents as alveolar haemorrhage which is suggested on lung CT and confirmed by bronchoalveolar lavage.

Skin involvement is seen as more or less diffuse patches of necrosis, the development of, or an increase in, livedo, sub-nail haemorrhages and/or, more rarely, vascular purpura.

Macrovascular thrombosis can affect all vessels and organs and is easily diagnosed using conventional radiological imaging (ultrasound, CT, MRI). Besides the typical sites (myocardial infarction, stroke, pulmonary embolism, deep vein thrombosis, etc.), APS may occur in unusual sites. For example, venous thrombosis of the digestive system (portal thrombosis, thrombosis of the splenic and mesenteric system, Budd-Chiari syndrome), retinal occlusions and cerebral venous thrombosis have been reported.

A systemic inflammatory syndrome is frequent, without microbiological documentation and should be distinguished from sepsis.

# 4.5. Biological signs

The risk of CAPS depends on the patient's aPL antibody profile. Thus, LA positivity, double positivity and especially triple positivity $(\mathrm{LA} + \mathrm{aCL} + \mathrm{a}\beta 2\mathrm{GPI})$ are considered to be serological profiles with a high thrombotic risk and most frequently associated with CAPS. The main biological signs are:

- Thrombocytopenia, which is common (at least $60\%$ ) in CAPS, is peripheral, almost constant and may be profound (up to $20\%$ of patients with a platelet count $< 20~\mathrm{G / L}$ ). Because it is related to platelet-consuming micro-thrombi, anticoagulation should not be systematically interrupted, except in case of major bleeding (particularly cerebral or digestive), which is the main treatment. The progression of thrombocytopenia parallels

<!-- page 26 -->
the clinical course thus, the resolution of thrombocytopenia suggests that CAPS is evolving well. A rapid decrease in platelet count suggests CAPS in patients with APS.

Anaemia is common. The presence of schistocytes or a collapsed haptoglobin is less frequent and suggests a complete thrombotic microangiopathy.   
D-dimer levels are almost always increased. If they are negative, the diagnosis should be questioned.   
- A marked biological inflammatory syndrome with fever associated with elevated fibrinogen, CRP, procalcitonin, and, when measured, interleukin 6, especially in cases of infectious triggers or extensive thrombosis.

# 4.6. Role of biopsy in the diagnosis of CAPS

According to international criteria there must be evidence of small vessel thrombosis to classify a patient as having CAPS. Except for specific skin involvement, which requires a systematic biopsy, histological evidence is often difficult to obtain. Indeed, performing a deep biopsy (renal, hepatic or even myocardial) is dangerous in these anticoagulated patients who are often receiving antiplatelet drugs and are thrombopenic. Moreover, any bleeding complications could compromise treatment and thus the prognosis. Transjugular renal biopsy can be performed under anticoagulant treatment, and is the technique of choice in this indication. In practice, the benefit/risk ratio must be carefully weighed and, in most cases, therapeutic decisions can be made without histological evidence alone. Renal biopsy may be proposed sometime after the acute phase.

# 4.7. Differential diagnoses of CAPS

There are several differential diagnoses for suspected CAPS.

- In a patient with SLE, a lupus flare should be suspected in the presence of renal, cardiac, neurological, digestive, haematological or pulmonary involvement. CAPS and SLE relapse may coexist and it is difficult to determine the mechanism of damage of the different organs.   
- Profound thrombocytopenia $< 30$ G/L (especially associated with a systemic autoimmune disease):

○ Immunological thrombocytopenia especially in the absence of new organ dysfunction or macrovascular thrombosis, associated with bleeding and negative, or low-positive d-dimer supports this diagnosis.   
- Thrombotic microangiopathy and organ damage(s), alternative causes of thrombotic microangiopathy should be ruled out. In thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, the thrombotic microangiopathy syndrome is significantly more marked than in APS (thrombocytopenia is usually $< 30\mathrm{G / L}$ , and schistocytes are present in large numbers, a rare feature in CAPS), macrovascular thrombosis is rare, and ADAMTS-13 activity is very low $< 10\%$ . ADAMTS-13 level is often decreased in CAPS (approximately $40\%$ ) but the diagnosis should be considered in case of very low level ( $< 10\%$ ). ADAMTS-13 level should be systematically assessed. Other aetiologies of thrombotic microangiopathies are atypical haemolytic uremic syndrome, HELLP syndrome, purpura fulminans and thrombotic microangiopathy associated with severe proliferative lupus glomerulonephritis.   
Patients with CAPS are often mistakenly diagnosed with heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) or vaccine-induced thrombocytopenia (VITT or VIPIT). Indeed, the clinical and biological signs of HIT and CAPS may be

<!-- page 27 -->
similar and there is often a delay between the initiation of heparin and the platelet count rebound. This may lead to relay anticoagulant therapy, which can delay curative anticoagulation, and worsen the patient prognosis. The interpretation of biological tests for the diagnosis of HIT is complex in these cases and requires a team of haemostasis specialists.

- Multiple macrovascular thrombosis (arterial and/or venous).   
- Left heart infectious endocarditis, marantic endocarditis, left intraventricular thrombus (often during advanced heart disease).   
- Patent foramen ovale.   
Neoplasia.   
- There is a clinical picture of a thrombotic storm that is similar to CAPS in all ways, but with no identified conventional aPL (other aPL specificities? viral stimulus?). Management of this entity is not codified and some practitioners recommend therapeutic management similar to CAPS to be discussed with the centre of expertise.

# 4.8. Prognosis

Mortality from CAPS has decreased significantly: it was $\geq 50\%$ before 2000 and is currently $< 30\%$ .

Patient prognosis has improved thanks to better understanding of the syndrome, earlier diagnosis, better-defined treatment protocols, and progress in intensive care medicine, in particular, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). CAPS is both life-threatening and associated with organ damage. The frequency of organ damage 6 months after an episode of CAPS is high:

renal failure $(\mathrm{GFR} < 60~\mathrm{mL / min})$ $48.9\%$ end-stage renal failure $6.4\%$   
- heart failure (LVEF < 50%) 21.3%;   
adrenal insufficiency $14.9\%$ ;   
- sequelae from cerebrovascular accidents $14.9\%$ ;   
permanent vision impairment $12.8\%$ .

Although CAPS may recur, the risk is low ( $< 5\%$ ) but it is higher if treatment is interrupted (preventive measures).

# 5. Specificities of APS in children [78-97]

# 5.1. Clinical presentation of APS in children

Paediatric APS is very rare, and usually occurs between the ages of 9 and 14, although it can occur at any age. There is no gender preponderance in children.

The data on paediatric APS are limited and mainly based on a cohort of 121 patients from the International Paediatric APS Registry (Ped-APS). Patients usually present with venous thrombosis of the lower limbs. Stroke is the most frequent manifestation of arterial thrombosis. Simultaneous small vessel thrombosis and venous and arterial thrombosis is rare in children. The most frequent non-thrombotic manifestations are haematological (38% of patients) including peripheral thrombocytopenia, haemolytic anaemia and Evans syndrome. Other related disorders may be neurological (headache, chorea, convulsions) or dermatological (livedo racemosa, Raynaud's phenomenon, skin ulcerations, pseudovasculitis lesions). Obstetrical manifestations are rarely observed in adolescents.

Triggering factors are present in about a $25\%$ of cases for example, infection, immobilisation, surgery or trauma.

CAPS is reported in less than $1\%$ of paediatric cases of APS. In the largest paediatric series of 45 children, CAPS developed in children with primary APS (68% of cases) and was revelatory. The classification criteria are identical to those for adults (see section 4.1).

# 5.2. Diagnosis of APS in children

There are no specifically validated criteria for childhood APS, thus the classification criteria for adult APS (see section 4.4) are applied. Although they are specific, these criteria may lack sensitivity in this population and result in a delayed diagnosis. Indeed, the current classification for adults includes two types of clinical criteria: vascular thrombosis and obstetric morbidity and the latter cannot be applied to children. Moreover, in children, nonthrombotic manifestations are frequent such as haematological, neurological (chorea, convulsions) and dermatological (livedo) disorders and are not taken into account with the existing classification. Finally, classification criteria exclude superficial venous thrombosis, which should be considered in children, because it is so rare in this population.

Thus, testing for aPLs should be performed in children in the presence of:

- Any arterial or venous thrombosis, including superficial venous thrombosis, with no obvious aetiology.   
Non-thrombotic manifestations that often precede thrombosis:

Haematological: thrombocytopenia, haemolytic anaemia.   
Neurological: chorea, convulsion.   
Dermatological: livedo racemosa, Raynaud's phenomenon, skin ulcerations, pseudo vasculitis lesions.   
○ More rarely cardiac (valvulopathy) and pulmonary (pulmonary arterial hypertension).

In addition, aPL testing should be repeated once or twice a year in any child or adolescent with systemic lupus.

# 5.3. Testing for APS in children

The diagnostic protocol is the same as in adults. Genetic causes of APS of APS are rare and should only be searched for after consultation with an expert centre. A few familial cases of APS have been reported (13% in the international register). An $IKZF1$ gene mutation was

<!-- page 29 -->
recently identified as a cause of autoimmunity and familial APS. IKZF1 encodes the IKAROS protein, a transcription factor with a key role in lymphocyte differentiation.

Clinical and biological testing is the same as that for adults (see chapter 3). In addition, a height-weight growth curve should be established.

# 5.4. Special case of the detection of isolated aPLs with no clinical signs in children

Isolated LA are often identified in children, particularly during a preoperative work-up. This is usually transient due to viral or bacterial infection or vaccination and is non-pathogenic, especially in young children. LA should be retested for at 3 months, then again at 6 months if it persists. No other biological testing or special management is necessary if the child is asymptomatic.

# 5.5. New-borns of mothers with aPL and/or APS

Thrombosis in the new-born of a mother with aPL is rare.

Its potential impact on these new-borns includes:

- Risks due to APS-related pregnancy complications, such as foetal death in utero, prematurity, low birth weight, placental insufficiency.   
- Risk due to maternal-foetal transmission of aPLs, including a theoretical risk of arterial and/or venous thrombosis. The data on these events are limited. No cases of thrombosis were identified in the European registry of 133 children born to mothers with APS, while aPL was noted at birth in $18\%$ of these children. Reports of neonatal thrombosis are extremely rare in the literature, often associated with several other risk factors of thrombosis.

Although the neurotoxicity of aPLs has been demonstrated in animal models, an increased risk of autism or developmental disorders in children born to mothers with APS remains controversial.

To date, the benefit of systemic screening for aPLs in new-borns of mothers with APS and/or aPLs carriers has not been demonstrated. Routine testing cannot be recommended due to the rarity of thrombotic complications.

# 6. Fertility / Contraception / Pregnancy / Breastfeeding [24,38,41-42,79,82,84,87-88]

# 6.1. Contraception

APS is a disease that mainly affects young women and is associated with a risk of complications during pregnancy. Questions on the management of oral contraception, pregnancy and the post-partum period are frequent. These issues should be systematically discussed early on with APS patients of childbearing age, so that pregnancies can be planned, the appropriate method of contraception chosen and patients can be reassured. Contraception may also be required for treatments that are contraindicated during pregnancy.

The main contraceptive methods are hormonal, intrauterine devices (IUDs) and mechanical barriers (condoms, diaphragm).

In general, any contraception containing oestrogens is contraindicated in patients with APS due to the increased risk of venous thrombosis (multiplied by a factor of 2 to 4 compared to the general population) (HAS 2019 recommendations: https://www.hassante.fr/jcms/c_1752542/fr/contraception-chez-la-femme-adulte-et-de-l-adolescente-en-age-de-procreer-hors-post-partum-et-post-ivg).

IUDs, including those delivering small doses of levonorgestrel, can be used in patients with APS. If the patient is receiving VKAs, IUDs with small doses of levonorgestrel limit bleeding and therefore the risk of uterine haemorrhage. Copper IUDs are poorly tolerated and cause menorrhagia in most patients who are receiving anticoagulants.

Oral or implantable progestins can be used in patients with APS. Chlormadinone acetate or cyproterone acetate (prescribed off-label as a contraceptive) are prescribed less frequently because they are associated with an increased risk of meningioma. Norpregnane derivatives, including nomegestrol acetate, are not recommended in patients with a history of thromboembolism.

Emergency contraceptives can be prescribed in the same way as in the general population: levonorgestrel, ulipristal acetate or IUD.

The same recommendations can be applied to patients with an anti-phospholipid profile without confirmed APS, especially when they are considered to be at high risk (Table 2).

# 6.2. Management of APS in pregnancy

APS is still associated with a higher rate of maternal and foetal morbidity and mortality than in the general population. Complications mainly occur due to placental insufficiency and include foetal complications (foetal loss, IUGR, induced prematurity), and maternal complications (pre-eclampsia, eclampsia, HELLP syndrome, caesarean section).

There may also be lupus flares in patients with associated SLE, and neonatal lupus if there are anti-SSA and/or anti-SSB antibodies (see French NDCP SLE https://www.hassante.fr/upload/docs/application/pdf/2017-03/dir1/pnds-__lupus_systemique.pdf).

These pregnancies should be managed by trained multidisciplinary teams, and may be optimised by planning, ideally during a preconception consultation. Consultations should be scheduled at least once a month in case of high-risk pregnancies.

# 6.2.1. Pregnancy planning: pre-conception consultation

Pregnancies should be planned during a pre-conception consultation with the referral APS specialist to evaluate and inform the patients on the different issues that will affect the pregnancy.

<!-- page 31 -->
The goals of this consultation in patients with APS or the presence of positive aPL test results are to:

- Identify APS-related situations where pregnancy is contra-indicated.   
- Avoid maternal thrombotic complications.   
- Avoid early spontaneous miscarriage in APS (before 10 weeks of gestation) or later maternal and foetal complications (at or after 10 weeks of gestation) due to placental insufficiency.   
- Define the appropriate treatment for pregnancy by identifying potentially teratogenic drugs, scheduling their withdrawal and defining the appropriate antithrombotic treatment for pregnancy. The patient should be informed of the need to stop VKAs as soon as the pregnancy is confirmed, and to begin treatment with LMWH. Pregnancy tests are recommended as soon as menstruation is delayed (or even systematically in the case of irregular cycles).   
- Check toxoplasmosis serology, rubella immunisation, vaccinations and folic acid supplementation.

Pregnancy is not recommended and may even be contraindicated in the following situations: recent extensive thrombosis (temporary contraindication), recent lupus flare in case of associated SLE, GFR $< 40 \mathrm{ml} / \mathrm{min} / 1.73\mathrm{m}^2$ , severe uncontrolled hypertension, severe valvulopathy and pulmonary hypertension, which is associated with a high risk of sudden cardiopulmonary decompensation. Not all pulmonary hypertensions are a contraindication to pregnancy and pre-conception advice should be obtained from a pulmonary hypertension reference centre if possible. Advice should be obtained from an expert centre in all of these situations, and in case of a history of severe thrombosis or especially, CAPS

In patients with associated SLE, the consultation will also assess disease activity, the specific risk associated with the presence of an anti-SSA antibody, and the compatibility of treatment with pregnancy.

It should be noted that no link between infertility and aPL has been demonstrated and that infertility is not a recognised clinical sign of APS.

# 6.2.2. Obstetrical risk stratification

Obstetrical risks are correlated with the presence of LA and are higher in case of triple positivity. Patients with thrombotic APS also have a higher risk of obstetric complications than those with APS and obstetric complications alone. Despite antithrombotic treatment, there is still $30\%$ a risk of obstetric complications

# 6.2.3. Management of APS in pregnancy

Obstetric complications may be partially prevented through management and appropriate monitoring. For example, with appropriate antithrombotic treatment, the average percentage of live births increases from $< 50\%$ to $90\%$ .

Treatment is based on low dose acetylsalicylic acid (aspirin) and LMWH. Although these treatments are not approved for this indication in pregnant women, an international consensus has validated their use during pregnancy in this population. Boxes of aspirin have a pictogram indicating "medicine contraindicated during pregnancy". Patients must be warned that low-dose aspirin $(< 300\mathrm{mg/day})$ in pregnant women has a benefit/risk ratio that is not associated with any risk of malformation:

- Low dose, antiplatelet, aspirin, usually $100\mathrm{mg/day}$ , should be prescribed during pregnancy. A dose of $160\mathrm{mg/day}$ is sometimes used. Evening dosing is preferred (better bioavailability in pregnant women).   
- LMWHs are well tolerated and routine platelet monitoring is no longer recommended in these patients.

# 6.2.3.1. Patients with APS and a history of arterial or venous thrombosis

These patients are on long-term VKA which is contraindicated after 6 weeks of gestation (this should be explained during the preconception consultation). The patient should be given a prescription for curative LMWH combined with a low-dose of aspirin as soon as the pregnancy is confirmed. Ideally a prescription should be provided during the preconception consultation to avoid undue exposure to VKA. Aspirin should be continued as long as possible during pregnancy. In practice it is often discontinued at 35-36 weeks of gestation to limit the risk of peripartum haemorrhage and also in the absence of any benefit to the risk of vascular IUGR at this stage. In rare cases, aspirin is continued for the mother (arterial thrombotic APS in particular).

LMWH should be continued throughout pregnancy and the shortest possible window of anticoagulation should be used for the delivery. Ideally the last injection should be administered the day before and treatment should be resumed a few hours after delivery. A relay with UFH may be used in higher risk situations.

# 6.2.3.2. Patients with obstetrical APS without a history of thrombosis

Long-term aspirin is usually prescribed. This is combined with a preventive dose of LMWH during pregnancy and continued for at least 6 weeks postpartum. Aspirin should be continued for as long as possible during pregnancy. It is often discontinued at 35-36 weeks of gestation to limit the risk of peripartum bleeding. LMWH should be continued throughout pregnancy and a window of anticoagulation should be established at delivery. LMWH is generally continued for 6 weeks postpartum, then aspirin is resumed if it was the long-term treatment before pregnancy.

# 6.2.3.3. Patients with isolated anti-phospholipid biology without a history of thrombotic or obstetrical events

Aspirin is recommended, especially in patients with associated SLE. A preventive dose of LMWH may be discussed depending on the specific anti-phospholipid profile, particularly in the presence of LA (and especially triple positivity).

If these treatments fail, after checking for good adherence and excluding the presence of hyperemesis gravidarum or resistance to aspirin, treatment may be intensified (increase in the dose of aspirin dose or LMWH or the addition of hydroxychloroquine) after discussion with a reference/competence centre.

# 6.2.3.4. Related measures

- Wearing support stockings (or socks) throughout the pregnancy and postpartum period can be proposed.   
- Any associated SLE must be managed (see French NDCP SLE https://www.hassante.fr/upload/docs/application/pdf/2017-03/dir1/pnds___lupus_systemique.pdf) and regularly monitored. The disease must be kept as stable as possible. Treatment is

<!-- page 33 -->
based on hydroxychloroquine, non-fluorinated corticosteroids and/or an immunosuppressant (azathioprine, ciclosporin or even tacrolimus).

- The patient should be screened for congenital atrioventricular block related to the presence of anti-SSA or anti-SSB antibodies (1% risk, usually between 16 and 26 weeks of gestation) (see French NDCP SLE https://www.hassante.fr/upload/docs/application/pdf/2017-03/dir1/pnds-_lupus_systemique.pdf)   
- All treatments must be adapted. Besides VKA, any drugs that are contraindicated during pregnancy should be discontinued before pregnancy if possible, or even well before if they have a long plasma elimination half-life. The CRAT website provides up-to-date information and can be consulted to make prescription decisions: http://www.lecrat.fr. It should be noted that 4 to $7\%$ of women exposed to VKA at between 6 and 9 weeks of gestation may develop a malformation syndrome known as warfarin embryopathy or VKA embryopathy (mainly hypotrophy, hypoplasia of the bones of the nose, hypertelorism and punctuations in the epiphyses of the long bones and axial skeleton). These women should undergo ultrasound monitoring of foetal face, skeleton and growth.

# 6.2.4. Pregnancy monitoring

Multidisciplinary monitoring during pregnancy provides early identification of complications, in particular intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) and/or pre-eclampsia (and its complications such as HELLP syndrome, retroplacental haematoma or eclampsia). Disease progression is monitored in patients with associated lupus (or lupus biology). It should be noted that maternal complications can also occur in the postpartum period, so that monitoring should be maintained in the days and weeks following delivery.

Monthly monitoring includes:

- Clinical monitoring: in addition to weight, a search for oedema, blood pressure and a urine dipstick, which are essential to identify pre-eclampsia, signs of other obstetrical complications (abdominal pain, especially epigastric, leg oedema, etc.) and of a lupus flare in case of associated lupus, should be investigated. Correct administration of heparin injections should also be confirmed (monitoring of injection points) and patients should be educated to know when to seek emergency treatment.   
- Biological monitoring: creatinine, proteinuria, transaminases, platelets, haemoglobin (or lactate dehydrogenase, haptoglobin). Angiogenic markers (e.g. SfT1/PIGF, VEGF) can be measured. Anti-Xa activity can be determined to assist in adjusting treatment to prevent any overdoses and activity during the interruption window. In patients with associated lupus, monitoring also includes measurement of complement components (which increase physiologically during pregnancy) and anti-native DNA antibodies.   
- Ultrasound screening: all pregnant women in France have three ultrasounds (at around 12 weeks, 20-22 weeks, and 32-34 weeks of gestation). If the patient has APS, or even an anti-phospholipid profile, an ultrasound may be proposed at 6-7 weeks of gestation (to confirm normal progress and intra-uterine growth). An ultrasound scan is performed to monitor the quality of foetal growth, usually monthly from the second trimester onwards. A uterine and umbilical/cerebral Doppler flow study is also performed at 20-22 weeks of gestation and after. The presence of a protodiastolic notch on one or two uterine arteries on the Doppler ultrasound during the $2^{\text{nd}}$ trimester should be looked for on ultrasound scans. The absence of this sign is reassuring.

Any abnormality should be discussed by a multidisciplinary team for appropriate management, generally in a specialised centre.

# 6.2.5. Childbirth and postpartum

- Depending on the situation, and in particular in patients with curative anticoagulants, the delivery may be scheduled at around 39 weeks of gestation (although this is not systematic).   
- Aspirin is usually discontinued at between 35 and 36 weeks of gestation but may be continued longer in special situations. Epidural analgesia is not contraindicated.   
- During pregnancy LMWH should be withheld for the shortest possible period to allow for epidural anaesthesia and resume after delivery. In patients who are not treated with anticoagulants before pregnancy, preventive LMWH should be continued for at least 6 weeks after delivery because of the increased risk of thrombosis in the postpartum period. In the absence of postpartum haemorrhagic complications, VKA can be resumed approximately one week after delivery with warfarin if the mother is breastfeeding.   
- Monitoring should be continued during the postpartum period because the complications of APS or lupus flares may occur. The HELLP syndrome is a particularly severe complication when associated with APS during pregnancy, with a high risk of progression to CAPS in the postpartum period. Thus, these patients should be closely monitored in the week following delivery.

# 6.3. Breastfeeding

Breastfeeding is usually possible, but care should be taken to confirm treatment compatibility. The CRAT website provides up-to-date information and can be consulted to make prescription decisions: http://www.lecrat.fr.

- Aspirin: High concentrations have been found in breastfeeding infants, with a peak in milk 2 hours after the maternal serum peak. When used at anti-platelet doses, breastfeeding with aspirin is possible.   
- LMWH: It is not excreted in the milk due to its high molecular weight. Breastfeeding is possible.   
VKA: Coumarin derivatives such as warfarin or acenocoumarol can be used during breastfeeding because there is very little excretion into milk. Fluindione is excreted into breast milk and is strictly contraindicated during breastfeeding. Vitamin K intake is the same as in the general population.   
- Hydroxychloroquine: Found in low concentrations in breast milk. Several studies of children born to mothers treated with hydroxychloroquine have shown no adverse effects.   
- Corticosteroids: Found in low concentrations in breast milk at maternal doses below an equivalent of $50\mathrm{mg/day}$ of prednisone. No adverse effects have been reported. The American Academy of Paediatrics has declared prednisone and prednisolone safe and compatible with breastfeeding.   
- Immunosuppressants: see French NDCP SLE https://www.hassante.fr/upload/docs/application/pdf/2017-03/dir1/pnds-_lupus_systemique.pdf.

# 7. Therapeutic management [7,10,14,16-18,20-21,27,31-32]

# 7.1. Objectives

The goals of therapeutic management are:

Short term:

To resolve the thrombotic event.   
To provide functional recovery and resolve a life-threatening event.

Intermediate term:

To prevent the occurrence of thrombotic events (secondary prevention).   
○ To discuss, anticipate and plan possible pregnancies (contraception, pre-conception consultation).   
To preserve the quality of life and socio-professional integration in adults.   
To preserve the quality of life, schooling and career orientation of children.   
To adapt sports activities.

- Long term:

To limit the after-effects of the disease.   
To limit the deleterious effects of treatment.   
○ To prevent secondary cardiovascular complications and accelerated atherosclerosis.

# 7.2. Professionals involved (and coordination arrangements)

Management is multidisciplinary and coordinated by the hospital doctors at the reference and/or centre of excellence with the attending physician and expert centres.

Because of the many manifestations of the disease any physician may encounter a patient with APS. Thus, a patient with APS first be managed by a general practitioner or paediatrician. The diagnosis should then be rapidly confirmed by a doctor with experience in APS.

The adult or paediatric specialists most often involved in the management of APS are: internists, vascular physicians, obstetricians, dermatologists, rheumatologists, nephrologists, neurologists, haematologists, haematology biologists, cardiologists, intensive care specialist, and, depending on the clinical picture, any other specialist whose opinion may be required.

Other professionals may be involved including:

- Paramedical professionals: psychologists (service only reimbursable in hospitals or networks); advanced practice nurses (therapeutic patient education, treatment follow-up), physiotherapists, midwives and dieticians.   
- Social worker, school assistant (home or school-based tutoring), life assistant.

APS is responsible for about $8\%$ of cases of venous thromboembolic disease or ischaemic stroke in children. In the absence of treatment and depending on the clinico-biological profile, the risk of recurrence is high. Thrombotic recurrence remains possible under anticoagulant treatment, especially in the most severe patients.

The treatment of thrombotic events in APS is based on long-term anticoagulant therapy with a VKA as a relay to initial heparin therapy.

The prevention of recurrent thrombosis is based on VKA treatment and therapeutic education, to prevent situations that create a risk of relapse.

# 7.3. Contraindications to anticoagulation

<!-- page 36 -->
They are listed in the Summary of Product Characteristics of each antithrombotic drug.

However, in case of thrombosis, because of the absence of an effective alternative to anticoagulants, some of these contraindications are relative and must be discussed with the treating physicians.

Because of the risk of intracranial bleeding, the following situations should be discussed in terms of the benefit-risk ratio of anticoagulant therapy:

Cerebral amyloid angiopathy.   
- Recent intracranial haemorrhage.   
- Activities at risk of trauma.

# 7.4. Antithrombotic treatment

# 7.4.1. Heparins

The treatment of thrombotic events in APS is based on the initial use of heparin (preferably LMWH) before switching to VKA. Heparins can sometimes be used for longer during periods of bleeding risk or in unstable patients.

It should be kept in mind that anti-Xa activity should be tested to determine treatment efficacy because a prolonged APTT due to LA makes it inappropriate to assess the efficacy of UFH.

# 7.4.2. Anti-vitamin K antagonists (VKAs)

The initiation of anticoagulants in patients with a venous thromboembolism follows the general recommendations for venous thrombosis. Anticoagulants should be started as soon as possible, except in case of stroke as explained below.

Warfarin (COUMADINE®), available worldwide, is the VKA of choice. Fluindione (PREVISCAN®) is now contraindicated as the initial anticoagulant due to rare but often severe immune-allergic side effects, in particular the risk of renal toxicity. This measure took effect on December 1, 2018 (https://ansm.sante.fr/informations-de-securite/traitement-par-antivitamines-k-avk-nouvelles-informations). The signs of fluindione toxicity are renal (tubulointerstitial nephropathy) and cutaneous (drug hypersensitivity syndrome with eosinophilia, drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms [DRESS] syndrome).

# 7.4.3. Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs)

An increased risk of recurrent thrombotic events has been observed in patients with APS treated with rivaroxaban and apixaban. Other DOACs (edoxaban and dabigatran etexilate) may also increase this risk compared to VKAs such as warfarin.

DOACs are therefore not recommended in patients with APS, especially those at high risk of thrombotic recurrence on DOACs (e.g. triple positive patients, double positive patients with LA, possibly isolated LA, history of arterial or microcirculatory thrombosis).

The continuation of DOAC therapy to prevent thromboembolic recurrence in patients with APS should be re-evaluated, especially when there is a high risk of thrombosis, and follow-up therapy with a VKA should be considered.

DOACs could be an alternative in single or double-positive patients who are not considered to be at high risk (absence of the above-mentioned risk factors) with a first episode of venous thrombosis, and who are allergic or intolerant to VKA or with marked variability in the INR, despite good adherence to VKA therapy.

The inclusion of these patients in a registry is essential to improve the current state of knowledge:

https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT04262492?term=OBSTINATE&draw=2&rank=2

Whenever APS is strongly suspected (prolonged APS) in the absence of other causes, the use of a DOAC should be postponed.

# 7.4.4. Antiplatelet agents

They are indicated in certain situations: see below, paragraphs "Stroke" and "Coronary syndrome".

# 7.5. Treatment of a first thrombosis

# 7.5.1. "Venous" APS

Secondary prophylaxis of a thrombotic event is based on VKA treatment with an INR target of 2 to 3.

# 7.5.2. "Arterial" APS

Patients with arterial thrombotic APS have a higher risk of recurrence than those with venous APS with a tendency to recurrence in the same type of vessel. Secondary prophylaxis is mainly based on VKA therapy with an INR target of 2 to 3. Some teams prefer prophylaxis with an INR target of 3 to 4 or VKA therapy with an INR target of 2 to 3 combined with low-dose aspirin.

# 7.5.3. Special cases

# 7.5.3.1. Stroke

In general, the early phase of cerebral ischaemia is a relative contraindication to the prescription of anticoagulants. In patients with cardioembolic stroke in the general population, early anticoagulation is associated with a non-significant reduction in recurrent ischaemic stroke with no marked reduction in mortality or disability but with an increase in intracranial bleeding. The initiation of anticoagulants after ischaemic stroke should be determined on an individual basis based on thromboembolic and bleeding risks in consultation with a neurovascular team. The severity of the stroke and the size of the infarct should be taken into account when choosing the optimal time for anticoagulants. Thus, urgent anticoagulation to prevent early stroke recurrence, to prevent neurological worsening or to improve the outcome after ischaemic stroke is not recommended. Aspirin can reasonably be given within 48 hours (100-300 mg/day) after ischemic stroke or TIA as short-term treatment, until anticoagulation is begun.

Data are scarce on APS. The thrombotic risk is high and the introduction of anticoagulants should be discussed on a case-by-case basis.

# 7.5.3.2. Coronary syndrome

This is difficult because it is necessary to distinguish between atherosclerosis-related and APS-related thrombotic manifestations. Cases must be discussed with a cardiologist.

<!-- page 38 -->
In the specific case of APS, a distinction must be made between:

- Known APS: 1 week to 1 month of triple therapy (anticoagulants + double antiplatelet treatment with aspirin and clopidogrel) followed by 12 months of dual therapy including a VKA.   
- APS discovered several months after the cardiovascular event treated with an antiplatelet drug: Switching from an antiplatelet drug to a VKA-type anticoagulant, should be discussed with a competence/reference centre specialised in systemic autoimmune diseases.

# 7.5.3.3. Recurrent superficial venous thrombosis

Although it is not recognised as a "classifying" thrombotic manifestation of APS, in some cases the presence of recurrent superficial venous thrombosis in a healthy vein associated with persistent aPL biology may be treated with anticoagulants in the same way as deep venous thrombosis.

# 7.6. Duration of treatment

Updated data from the Europhospholipid cohort confirm the risk of recurrence even after several years of treatment. Long-term unlimited treatment is the rule in APS because of the high risk of recurrence when treatment is stopped, mainly during the first six months.

# 7.6.1. Discontinuing treatment

In certain special cases (e.g. haemorrhagic complications of treatment, mild clinicobiological pictures such as induced distal venous thrombosis with non-persistent aPL), treatment can be interrupted after multidisciplinary discussion.

If treatment is stopped, regular follow-up is essential.

# 7.7. Treatment of recurrent forms

The management of patients with a recurrent thrombotic event despite optimal anticoagulant treatment is difficult and no guidelines are available. Recurrent thrombosis (arterial or venous) occurred in one third of patients during the 10-year follow-up period in the Europhospholipid cohort, which included 1000 patients with APS. A systematic review of the literature showed that most warfarin-treated patients with recurrent thrombosis had an $\mathrm{INR} < 3.0$ at the time of the event. In contrast, the percentage of recurrent events was low in patients receiving anticoagulant therapy with a target INR of between 3 and 4.

Thus, the first step is to keep the INR within this target by improving therapeutic patient education and optimising the frequency of monitoring. If recurrence occurs with an INR within the target, increasing anticoagulation seems to be the best choice (target INR: 3 - 4). In patients who cannot achieve an adequate and stable INR or with recurrence despite high intensity anticoagulation (INR at the time of recurrence $>3.0$ ), a switch from oral anticoagulation to the long-term use of LMWH or the addition of aspirin or hydroxychloroquine could be considered, although the evidence is not solid.

# 7.8. Therapeutic patient education (TPE) and lifestyle modification (case by case)

Anticoagulation is central to the management of APS.

<!-- page 39 -->
Anticoagulant therapy must be rigorously monitored and adjusted in patients with APS in particular:

- In CAPS, for whom the maintenance of therapeutic anticoagulation must be particularly rigorous.   
- In APS that has recurred on anticoagulant therapy within the therapeutic range.   
- In women with APS during pregnancy.

Thus, TPE clearly plays a central role in the long-term management of APS. The patient should be registered in a specialised anticoagulant clinic (Brest, Dôle, Lille, Limoges, Paris, Saint Ettienne, Strasbourg, Toulouse...).

# 7.8.1. The content of therapeutic patient education

The educational objectives of TPE for patients with APS and aPL are summarised in Table 4. Only the specificities of managing APS will be discussed.

In conclusion, TPE is essential to help the patient adhere to the monitoring schedule and adaptation of anticoagulant treatment. This must be especially rigorous and specific in patients with APS, especially CAPS. To reduce the burden on the patient and help him/her accept the constraints, this educational protocol can be associated with remote monitoring of anticoagulant treatment. Thus, treatment can be managed and adapted, the patient's questions can be answered, and s/he can receive reminders of upcoming deadlines using modern means of communication. Remote monitoring can be managed by the attending physician or the referring specialist if they accept to respond to the patient's needs within a reasonable time.

At present certain self-measuring devices use reagents that are too sensitive to aPL and may generate discrepancies with laboratory venous INR measurements. Thus, capillary INR self-measurement is not recommended in APS. One approach is to restrict the use of capillary INR self-measurement to patients who first confirm agreement between self-measurement and laboratory INR (difference $< 0.5$ units) in a therapeutic target situation, and then reconfirm every 6-12 months.

# 7.10. Special situations

# 7.10.1. CAPS

Because of the rarity of CAPS, no randomised or even prospective therapeutic studies have evaluated treatment. Treatment recommendations are based on the results of retrospective studies and on expert opinions. Any triggering factor, particularly infectious,

<!-- page 40 -->
should be treated and organ failure should be managed symptomatically, usually in intensive care. It is important to remember that all of the proposals listed below are based on a low level of evidence.

# 7.10.1.1. The main available treatments

The standard treatment is triple therapy: anticoagulation + corticosteroid therapy + plasma exchange or intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIG).

The first-line treatment is curative anticoagulation. This is the only treatment that has been associated with a reduction in mortality. Heparins are used during acute CAPS: UFH in unstable patients, with renal insufficiency or with a high risk of bleeding, or LMWH, especially because curative anticoagulation can be obtained more rapidly. Anti-Xa activity and not the APTT ratio should be measured to monitor treatment (for UFH) as the latter may be spontaneously prolonged in the presence of LA. Adapting heparin therapy to the APTT in a patient with LA is a common error resulting in unintentional underdosing of anticoagulants which may trigger or worsen CAPS. Curative dose anticoagulant treatment is continued in the rare cases of profound intermediate thrombocytopenia in CAPS (e.g. 50-100 G/L), because the benefit-risk ratio of anticoagulation remains favourable.

Although thrombocytopenia may be severe (<30 G/L), this is not a contraindication to curative anticoagulation in life-threatening situations. Anticoagulation should be discussed on a case-by-case basis in bleeding complications or in a patient at high risk of bleeding (e.g. massive ischaemic stroke). There is generally no indication for platelet transfusion except for life-threatening haemorrhage.

Corticosteroid therapy is also part of the first-line treatment of CAPS, even in its early stages. There is no consensus on a regimen, but it is generally administered in high doses, initially in the form of two to three IV pulses of $500\mathrm{mg}$ to $1\mathrm{g}$ of methylprednisolone in the presence of signs of severity or rapid deterioration, followed by a further $1\mathrm{mg/kg/day}$ of prednisone. The dose is gradually tapered at a distance from the acute episode. This is assessed on a case-by-case basis according to the course of disease and comorbidities. The presence of myocardial dysfunction does not contraindicate methylprednisolone pulses, but does expose the patient to an increased risk of arrhythmias. These patients should therefore be continuously monitored in intensive care.

Plasma exchange is also extensively used, and should be urgently started especially in patients with severe CAPS. There is a consensus on the use of plasma as the preferred replacement fluid to be administered daily initially, then spaced out according to clinical improvement (the number of plasma exchanges depends on the patient's progress). The choice of plasma exchange should be carefully discussed in cases of haemodynamic instability (they are not recommended in patients with cardiogenic shock). Plasma exchange can be performed on a peripheral venous line in adults, otherwise on a central venous line. The exchange volume should be between 40 and $60\mathrm{ml / kg}$ of body weight.

IVIG may be used instead of plasma exchange depending on the clinical picture and advice from the expert centre.

IVIG can be given at an immunomodulatory dose (total dose of $2\mathrm{g / kg}$ ), with a preference for a slow infusion over 4 days $(0.5\mathrm{g / kg / day}$ for 4 days) to limit the risk of thrombotic side effects and facilitate renal tolerance.

It is important to note that there is no consensus on the choice of IVIG or plasma exchange, the dose of IVIG or the frequency of plasma exchange administration, which varies among teams and according to the patient's clinical presentation. The CAPS Registry data for IVIG includes both immunomodulatory and replacement doses.

# 7.10.1.2. Assessment of the therapeutic response

There are no formal criteria for the response to therapy in CAPS. In addition to clinical improvement, the correction of thrombocytopenia is the major criterion suggesting a favourable outcome. If these criteria are not met, treatment failure should be discussed. Before considering second line treatment in these cases, it is important to confirm that anticoagulation is well-balanced, by making sure that anti-Xa activity is within the therapeutic range over several days. If there is no response after 72 hours of well-managed therapy, the patient has refractory CAPS or a differential diagnosis.

# 7.10.1.3. Refractory CAPS

If there is no response to triple therapy, second-line treatments can be proposed. This includes the addition of an anti-platelet drug (aspirin), cyclophosphamide treatment in case of associated lupus relapse, rituximab or eculizumab. It should be remembered that the level of evidence for the effectiveness of these therapies in this indication is low.

There is increasing evidence to suggest that the complement system is involved in the pathophysiology of CAPS, leading to the use of targeted therapies such as eculizumab, a terminal complement pathway inhibitor. Knowledge about the efficacy of eculizumab in CAPS is limited and the level of evidence remains low. A favourable response, when it occurs, develops rapidly after one to two injections. Eculizumab appears to be especially effective in haematological manifestations and should be preferably administered in cases with a marked thrombotic microangiopathy syndrome. The treatment regimen is similar to that in atypical haemolytic uremic syndrome, however the decision to continue treatment must be assessed on day 7 of the first dose. Efficacy is determined by clinical course, platelet count and haemolysis parameters.

The advice of a reference/competence centre specialised in autoimmune diseases including APS must be obtained for the management of all cases of CAPS.

# 7.10.1.4. Management of bleeding complications

Some haemorrhagic manifestations are a reflection of a thrombotic event (intra-alveolar haemorrhage, adrenal necrosis, etc.) thus, anticoagulant treatment is not contraindicated.

Bleeding is a serious event in CAPS. The prognosis of patients with APS admitted to the ICU is worse when the triggering factor is an anticoagulant event. Similarly, failure to introduce anticoagulation was associated with excess mortality in the two main CAPS cohort studies. Thus, bleeding complications during CAPS treatment are highly sensitive situations. First the origin of the bleeding must be controlled (endoscopy, surgery, embolisation, etc.) then preventive and curative anticoagulation must be rapidly reintroduced, preferably with UFH because of its short half-life and the possibility of changing to protamine. The use of second line treatments may be of interest when it is impossible to reintroduce a full regimen of anticoagulants (rituximab, eculizumab...).

# 7.10.2. Patients with aPL with no previous thrombotic history

# 7.10.2.1. Place of thromboprophylaxis

A meta-analysis of the published data as well as individual data have suggested that aspirin is in the primary prevention in patients with aPL. Thus, current EULAR guidelines recommend prescribing aspirin at an anti-platelet dose (75-100 mg/d in adults, 2-3 mg/kg/day

<!-- page 42 -->
not to exceed $100\mathrm{mg/day}$ in children), particularly in patients with a high-risk aPL profile as well as in patients with asymptomatic aPL, with systemic lupus without prior APS, and in non-pregnant women with a history of obstetric APS only (Table 2).

# 7.10.2.2. The role of hydroxychloroquine

The antithrombotic effect of hydroxychloroquine was not confirmed in a meta-analysis and no prospective studies have been published in on this topic. Therefore, this agent should not be considered for primary prevention in patients with aPL.

# 7.10.2.3. Control of cardiovascular risk factors

The presence of cardiovascular risk factors has a major impact on the prognosis of patients with aPL. The interaction between aPL and risk factors is exponential and the clinical phenotype of patients is different depending on the type of risk factor (arterial or venous). In the presence of an arterial risk factor (e.g. smoking, hypertension), patients with aPL tend to present with arterial thrombosis, whereas in the presence of a venous risk factor (e.g. obesity), they tend to have venous thrombosis. Thus, in patients with aPL (high risk: see Table 2) without defined APS, conventional cardiovascular risk factors should be screened for, and these factors must be controlled. In particular, contraceptives containing oestrogens should be switched to another type of contraceptive, smoking should be stopped, hypertension or dyslipidaemia controlled, thromboembolic prevention should be obtained in high-risk situations, and pregnancy planning must be organised.

# 7.10.3. Special features of treatment in children

The general guidelines for the management of thrombosis in children should be followed in children presenting with venous thrombosis and APS. Paediatric dosing regimens for LMWH and VKA therapy have been published and have been shown to be effective and safe.

TPE includes most of the items discussed in adults and is adapted to the child's maturity and level of understanding. TPE is a key element in the management of patients on anticoagulant therapy. It ensures that the child or adolescent and their parents have a good understanding of the disease, its treatment and the complications of treatment. A transition period must be anticipated during adolescence. This is the time to answer and solicit questions on smoking, drugs, sexuality, fertility, professional orientation and schooling. Child to adult transition consultations should be organised so the patient can begin to autonomously manage his/her disease to address specific points such as smoking prevention, contraception and therapeutic adherence.

An attempt should be made to adapt school and sports activities to the patient's individual manifestations.

Paediatric patients with APS should be referred to a specific TPE programme for children and their parents.

# 7.10.3.1. Special case: new-borns of mothers with aPL

Data on thrombotic events in children born to mothers with APS are limited, so clear recommendations cannot be made. The risk of perinatal thrombosis is rare, and preventive treatment should not be begun.

Initiation of treatment in children with aPL-related perinatal stroke should only be undertaken after discussion on a case-by-case basis in an expert centre.

# 8. Follow-up [19-49]

# 8.1. Objectives

- To monitor the clinical and biological progression of APS (taking into account any associated systemic disease, in particular lupus - see specific French NDCP).   
- To assess the acceptability and effectiveness of, adherence and tolerance to treatment.   
- To confirm a well-balanced VKA anticoagulant therapy according to the target INR.   
- To search for possible comorbidities.   
- To assess traditional cardiovascular risk factors and optimise their management.   
- To assess organ damage and the possible after-effects.   
- To assess the quality of life (empirical or standardised collection by validated questionnaire).   
- In children: to adapt school and sports activities and prepare career choices according to the treatment protocol. To prepare the transition to adult management.

# 8.2. Professionals involved (and coordination arrangements)

APS requires specialised care coordinated in a network, in collaboration with the attending physician.

Depending on the initial assessment and disease progression, monitoring requires:

- Involvement of reference or competence centres, with difficult decisions made at local or national multidisciplinary council meetings.   
- Specialist consultations (internists, vascular specialists, gynaecologists, paediatricians).   
- Consultations with the attending physician.   
- Possible support from physiotherapists, nurses, dieticians, psychologists and social workers.   
- Other specialists (rheumatologists, nephrologists, dermatologists, paediatricians, cardiologists, neurologists, etc.) may be involved, generally at the request of the other participating physicians.

# 8.3. Frequency and content of consultations

Outpatient follow-up should be given priority. Follow-up is proposed according to the type of clinical situation, adapted to the patient's clinical condition and degree of autonomy, with a frequency that generally ranges from 1 to 4 consultations per year.

# 8.3.1. Initial consultation

The aim of the initial consultation in the reference/competence centre is to confirm the diagnosis of APS and to propose appropriate treatment according to the clinical and biological signs and complications. The biological profile should be assessed by standard methods and the patient should fully understand its significance. Patient management should take into account his/her personal and professional plans and be defined together with physician. The consultation must take into consideration the patient's type of contraception, pregnancy plans (programming, follow-up) and define further follow-up.

# 8.3.2. Clinical examinations and follow-up procedures

<!-- page 44 -->
The follow-up medical interview and clinical examination are identical to those of the initial assessment. Particular attention should be paid to typical risk factors (arterial hypertension, diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, obesity, and smoking) and to lifestyle advice (diet, physical activity and sports, use of adapted physical activity).

The patient's understanding of his/her disease and treatment should be confirmed (using a specific TPE approach when these programmes are available). Contraceptive methods should be discussed and possible pregnancies planned during the pre-conception consultation.

The frequency and organisation of follow-up consultations vary according to the patient's clinical picture.

The development of an autoimmune disease such as systemic lupus should be checked for in patients with primary APS.

# 8.3.2.1. Patients with thrombosis-defined APS alone

Regular systematic follow-up meetings should be organised between the expert centre and the attending physician to assess the clinical progression, evaluate antithrombotic treatments (efficacy, side effects, adherence to VKAs, history of INRs and percentage of INRs within the therapeutic range), and any complementary tests needed according to the clinical picture (in particular in the case of associated events).

The adverse effects of anticoagulant treatment (bleeding) should be recorded, as well as any corrective measures taken if treatment is unstable (see the specific TPE on anticoagulant treatment).

The management of anticoagulant therapy should be assessed as well as the understanding of drug interactions, the role of diet, potential difficulties, biological quality control, drug interference.

8.3.2.2. Patients with clinical signs not included in the classification criteria

A systematic follow-up should be organised every 6 to 12 months, to be adapted to the clinical picture and the results of any complementary examinations (see specific chapters).

8.3.2.3. Patients with persistent aPL without a thrombotic event

Regular (at least annual) follow-up visits should be organised for a clinical assessment, the monitoring of risk factors and biological monitoring of aPL.

8.3.2.4. Obstetrical follow-up: see dedicated chapter

8.3.2.5. Use of teleconsultations

The use of teleconsultations can be proposed to improve follow-up in case of geographical distance or in situations requiring rapid contact.

8.3.2.6. Use of hospitalisation

Hospitalisation is needed for perioperative management, in particular for the management of switching antithrombotics which may require close clinical and biological monitoring. To facilitate these procedures and to communicate rapidly in the event of an emergency

<!-- page 45 -->
hospitalisation, it is important for patients to have an emergency card with the contact details of their referring doctors.

The frequency and type of complementary examinations are adapted to the patient's:

- clinical condition.   
age.   
aPL biological profile.   
duration and severity of the disease.   
comorbidities.   
treatments.

The goal of these examinations is to monitor APS and detect specific, sometimes asymptomatic disorders (cardiac, renal, haematological, etc.).

# 8.4.1. Vascular investigations

All cardiovascular investigations must be adapted to the patient's clinical and biological profile and any new clinical symptoms.

- Arterial or venous Doppler ultrasound should be performed in case of peripheral thrombosis. Non-invasive reference examinations must be performed to diagnose possible recurrence of venous thrombosis or peripheral arterial thrombosis. Because of the consequences of a diagnosis of a first event or of recurrent thrombosis, investigations must follow a standardised, validated and documented approach with available imaging data or examination reports.   
- Cardiac ultrasound should be performed as part of the initial work-up, to be re-evaluated according to the initial data, risk factors and clinical progression.   
- Brain MRI may be discussed to detect thrombosis or hypersignals, and for a baseline imaging report.

# 8.4.2. Biological investigations

# 8.4.2.1. Screening for organ and/or haematological damage

- Creatinine, transaminases, blood count, reticulocytes   
Proteinuria testing by urine dipstick or a urine sample with accurate quantification: sample protein/creatine ratio or 24-hour proteinuria. This can be done during consultations or by the patient in between visits.

# 8.4.2.2. Immunological monitoring

- Annual testing for anti-phospholipid antibodies (aCL, aβ2GP1, LA are one of the criteria), to confirm persistent disease during the first years of follow-up then to be adapted according to disease progression.   
- Testing for anti-dsDNA and anti-ENA antibodies even in the absence of clinical signs of associated systemic disease.

# 8.4.2.3. Other biological tests

<!-- page 46 -->
Annual testing of fasting blood glucose, triglycerides, total cholesterol, HDL, LDL.

# 8.5. Comprehensive care

Follow-up should be adapted to the initial clinical picture, to the management of acute events but also to possible sequelae. Psychological support or other medico-social professionals are called upon as needed, including for objective assessment of the quality of life as seen by the patients themselves.

# 8.6. Specificities of the paediatric follow-up

The rate of recurrent thrombosis is $20\%$ in the paediatric registry [80] with a median follow-up of 6.1 years. This is higher than in adults. The development of systemic lupus after APS is also more frequent in children than in adults. Follow-up of children or adolescents with APS includes:

Monitoring of INR similar to that in adults.   
- Regular testing for systemic lupus with a clinical examination, proteinuria and antinuclear antibodies.   
- Taking into account certain paediatric specificities.   
- Checking adherence to VKA treatment, which may be difficult, especially in adolescents.   
- Confirming that the patient is coping well with his/her chronic illness.   
Adapting sports activities to anticoagulant treatment. Contact sports with a risk of trauma and competition should be avoided.   
- Preparing the transition to adolescence, empowering the patient and preparing for the transfer to an expert adult medical centre. Transitional consultations should be organised to empower the adolescent and gain autonomy from his/her family as well as to address specific issues such as fertility, pregnancy and adherence to therapy. A group of European experts has created a checklist of issues to be addressed in the transition process to assist professionals. This transition is prepared several years in advance by the referring paediatrician, and possibly by a therapeutic education team, with transition workshops. It is important to make sure that the patient is familiar with the disease and its treatment. It is important to know the patient's life plan and support them. The adult physician must be identified in advance. A transition liaison form should be sent by the paediatrician to the adult physician before the consultation. Various transitional consultation arrangements can be considered, including a joint paediatrician/adult physician consultation. The timing of the transition should be based on the maturity and autonomy of the patient. The adult physician who was present at the transition consultation should not change. The paediatrician should also remain available to the patient and family. The frequency of these consultations should by slightly more frequent during the initial transition period and the patient should be told that the paediatrician will be regularly informed. The goal is to prevent recurrent thrombosis, the patient's exit from the care pathway and early mortality. Useful tools for this transition phase can be found and downloaded on the FAI2R website: www.fai2r.org/transition.

More specifically, the issues to be addressed include:

- The choice of occupation must be discussed. Although most jobs are possible with AVK treatment, there may be legal contraindications, depending on the responsibility of the position (e.g. airplane pilot), the isolation and distance from a medical structure

<!-- page 47 -->
(e.g. sailors) or the risk of trauma (e.g. high-level sportsman). The role of the occupational physician is important, especially to educate the patient in the management of potentially risky professional situations.

Listing possible drugs and toxic interactions with VKAs. Many drugs can increase the risk of bleeding of VKAs without altering the INR (aspirin/NSAIDs) or by increasing or decreasing the INR causing a risk of bleeding or thrombosis, respectively. The INR must be closely monitored if any of these drugs are needed and prescribed by the doctor. Thus, patients must be clearly warned not to take medicine on their own initiative, even if they are available in pharmacies without a prescription. (https://ansm.sante.fr/dossiers-thematiques/les-anti-vitamine-k-avk)   
- Contraception and pregnancy management (see above).
