# Current treatment of antiphospholipid syndrome: lights and shadows

Gerard Espinosa and Ricard Cervera

Abstract | For patients with antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), the consensus is to treat those who develop thrombosis with long-term oral anticoagulation therapy and to prevent obstetric manifestations by use of aspirin and heparin. These recommendations are based on data from randomized controlled trials and observational studies. Despite this body of knowledge, areas of uncertainty regarding the management of APS exist where evidence is scarce or nonexistent. In other words, for a subset of patients the course of management is unclear. Some examples are patients with 'seronegative' APS, those who do not fulfil the formal (clinical or serological) classification criteria for definite APS, and those with recurrent thrombotic events despite optimal anticoagulation. Other challenges include the treatment of clinical manifestations not included in the classification criteria, such as haematologic manifestations (thrombocytopenia and haemolytic anaemia), neurologic manifestations (chorea, myelitis and multiple sclerosis-like lesions), and nephropathy and heart valve disease associated with antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL), as well as the possible withdrawal of anticoagulation treatment in selected cases of thrombotic APS in which assays for aPL become persistently negative. This Review focuses on the current recommendations for thrombotic and obstetric manifestations of APS, as well as the management of difficult cases. Some aspects of treatment, such as secondary prophylaxis of venous thrombosis, are based on strong evidence—the 'lights' of APS treatment. Conversely, other areas, such as the treatment of non-criteria manifestations of APS, are based only on expert consensus or common sense and remain the 'shadows' of APS therapy.

Espinosa, G. & Cervera, R. Nat. Rev. Rheumatol. 11, 586-596 (2015); published online 30 June 2015; doi:10.1038/nrrheum.2015.88

# Introduction

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is defined by the coexistence of thromboses (venous or arterial) or recurrent fetal losses with the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL), namely lupus anticoagulant (LAC), moderate to high titres of anticardiolipin (aCL) antibodies or anti- $\beta_{2}$ -glycoprotein I $(\mathrm{a}\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI})$ antibodies, in isolation or in any combination. APS is considered primary if not associated with other underlying disease, or secondary if it appears in association with other autoimmune disorders, mainly systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). Regarding thrombotic and obstetric manifestations of APS, current consensus is to treat patients with APS with long-term oral anticoagulation therapy and with the combination of aspirin and heparin, respectively. These recommendations are based on data from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies, as reflected by the 2011 publication of evidence-based recommendations for thromboprophylaxis in patients with APS.

Despite this body of knowledge, areas of uncertainty exist in the management of APS in which evidence is scarce or nonexistent. One such grey area is the treatment of nonthrombotic manifestations associated with aPL but not included in the clinical classification criteria

for APS, such as haematologic, neurologic and cardiac manifestations (for example, chorea, demyelinating disease, myelitis and heart valve lesions).3,4 Moreover, some patients do not fulfil the formal serological criteria for the classification of APS,1 as can happen if they have low levels of aCL or $\mathsf{a}\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies without LAC. Finally, a small number of patients have the classical features of APS but persistently test negative for the aPL included in the serological criteria for classification of the disorder—the concept of 'seronegative' APS5,6—or initially have definite APS but eventually test negative for aPL.7 Thus, the management of APS is sometimes unclear and, in the absence of scientific evidence, clinical judgement and experience are relevant.

The purpose of this Review is to focus on treatment of thrombotic and obstetric manifestations of APS, representing the 'lights' of this syndrome, but we also summarize the evidence-based information about management of difficult cases, representing the 'shadows' of APS.

# Key points

- For patients with definite antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) who experience a first venous thrombotic event, the recommended treatment is prolonged use of oral anticoagulation therapy to maintain a target international normalized ratio (INR) of 2.0–3.0   
- For patients with definite APS and arterial thrombosis, several therapies have been proposed: anticoagulation therapy with a target INR 3.0–4.0; antiplatelet therapy alone; anticoagulation therapy with a target INR 2.0–3.0; or the combination of antiplatelet and anticoagulation therapy (target INR 2.0–3.0)   
- Although combined therapy with low-dose aspirin and low-molecular-weight heparin is the mainstay of treatment for women with obstetric APS, the strength of the evidence for the efficacy of this approach is still a matter of debate   
- The treatment of seronegative APS (as well as cases where antiphospholipid antibodies 'disappear' over time), thrombotic and obstetric manifestations refractory to conventional treatment, and manifestations not included in APS classification criteria is still uncertain   
- For areas of APS treatment for which published evidence is scarce or nonexistent, recommendations are based on clinical judgement and physician experience

In detail, patients with definite APS and a first venous thrombotic event should receive long-term oral anticoagulation therapy to a target international normalized ratio (INR) of 2.0-3.0—that is, conventional-intensity anticoagulation (grade of recommendation 1B; strong recommendation based on moderate-quality evidence). For patients with definite APS and an arterial event, the target INR for anticoagulation therapy was given as 3.0-4.0, reflecting high-intensity anticoagulation therapy; however, this recommendation lacked consensus, as some members of the task force contended that antiplatelet therapy alone or anticoagulation therapy with a target INR 2.0-3.0, or combined antiplatelet-anticoagulation therapy with a target INR of 2.0-3.0, would also be valid options (Table 1).

One argument against high-intensity anticoagulation therapy is that this approach increases the risk of secondary bleeding. However, in a systematic review of therapy for thrombotic APS, thrombotic recurrences were more frequent and were associated with greater mortality than haemorrhagic complications in patients treated with warfarin.[8]

Regarding the duration of anticoagulation therapy, the task force reached a consensus in recommending indefinite antithrombotic treatment for patients with APS and thrombosis (grade of recommendation 1C; strong recommendation based on low or very low quality evidence).

An important point to take into account is that individuals with only one positive aPL test seem to have a thrombotic recurrence rate similar to that of the general population. Therefore, secondary thromboprophylaxis for these patients should be the same as that for aPL-negative patients with thrombosis (grade of recommendation 1C).2

# The shadows

Apart from the type of blood vessel involved, other factors could influence the risk of thrombosis in

patients with aPL. The most important of these factors is aPL profile.

Substantial evidence (reviewed in 2013 by Pengo et al.) indicates that a 'triple-positive' aPL profile (presence of LAC plus high titres of aCL and aβ2GPI antibodies) is strongly associated with thrombosis, in contrast to positivity for a single aPL. Conversely, evidence about the effectiveness of treating triple-positive patients more intensively than patients with lower-risk serological profiles is lacking. However, clinical judgement dictates the need for close clinical monitoring, (with intensive control of cardiovascular risk factors) and therapeutic monitoring (to ensure a correct INR) in the subset of patients with a high-risk aPL profile.

Questions also remain regarding the best method of thromboprophylaxis for patients negative for LAC and with repeatedly low titres of IgG and/or IgM aCL antibodies (<40 IgG phospholipid units [GPL] or <40 IgM phospholipid units [MPL]) or $\mathsf{a}\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies ( $<99^{\mathrm{th}}$ percentile), or negative for LAC and with IgA aCL or IgA $\mathsf{a}\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies. No data are available to establish the threshold between moderate-high and low aPL levels, and evidence of the actual risk of thrombosis in this patient population is lacking. Although testing for IgA aCL and $\mathsf{a}\beta_{2}$ GPI antibodies is recommended when APS is suspected but the standard IgG and/or IgM aPL assays are negative, more studies comparing the various commercially available assays in large and well-characterized populations are needed.[10] Until more evidence on this topic is available, long-term anticoagulation therapy, in a similar manner as for patients with definite APS, is probably advisable for patients with suspected APS who do not fulfil the serological criteria (Table 2).

Finally, the role of new direct oral anticoagulants (DOAs), such as direct thrombin inhibitors (for example dabigatran) and direct inhibitors of activated factor X (such as rivaroxaban, apixaban and edoxaban) for thromboprophylaxis in patients with APS is under investigation in at least three RCTs. Unlike vitamin K antagonists, DOAs are administered as a fixed dose and have few reported drug interactions that affect anticoagulation intensity. Therefore, DOAs do not routinely require monitoring of anticoagulation intensity, improving the quality of life of patients.

# Obstetric manifestations of APS

# The lights and shadows

# Recommended treatments

The treatment most often recommended for women with aPL and recurrent pregnancy loss is the combination of heparin (either unfractionated or low-molecular-weight) and low-dose aspirin (LDA), with therapy commencing when pregnancy is confirmed. Oral anticoagulants should be discontinued before pregnancy or at conception because of teratogenicity between 6 and 14 weeks of gestation. Regarding dosing of heparin, prophylactic doses are recommended in women with definite APS without previous thrombosis. Conversely, therapeutic (full) doses of heparin are recommended in those women with previous thrombotic events (Table 1).

Overall, studies focused on the treatment of obstetric manifestations in women with APS all emphasize a very important aspect: the close monitoring of fetal and maternal health by a well-coordinated multidisciplinary team that includes obstetricians, internists (rheumatologists), haematologists and neonatologists.19 Evidence-based clinical practice guidelines for the management of antithrombotic therapy in pregnancy, published by the American College of Chest Physicians in 2012, recommend that women who fulfil the serological criteria for APS and meet the clinical criteria of obstetric APS on the basis of $\geq 3$ pregnancy losses should receive unfractionated heparin (prophylactic or intermediate-dose) or prophylactic low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) in combination with LDA $(75 - 100\mathrm{mg}$ per day) (grade 1B).20

# Weighing the evidence

Regarding the published evidence, a 2010 meta-analysis of trials involving 334 patients positive for aPL with recurrent pregnancy loss found overall live birth rates of $74.3\%$ for those who were treated with heparin (unfractionated heparin or LMWH) plus LDA versus $55.9\%$ for women who received LDA alone.[21] However, no definitive conclusions about efficacy can be drawn owing to the small number of RCTs included and their low quality. Two subsequent meta-analyses concluded that the combination of unfractionated heparin and LDA significantly improves live birth rate whereas the efficacy of LMWH plus LDA remains unproven.[22,23] In the

first of these two meta-analyses, which pooled data from five RCTs that enrolled 398 aPL-positive women with $\geq 2$ miscarriages, combining unfractionated heparin with LDA therapy had a significant effect in the prevention of first-trimester pregnancy loss compared with LDA alone (OR 0.26, $95\%$ CI 0.14-0.48), whereas the effect of LMWH plus LDA was not significant (OR 0.70, $95\%$ CI 0.34-1.45). In addition, combination therapy with LDA plus either unfractionated heparin or LMWH did not have a significant effect in the prevention of late-pregnancy (second and third trimester) losses.[22] In the second meta-analysis, including 16 RCTs involving 803 women with APS, therapy with the combination of LDA and unfractionated heparin increased live birth rates compared with LDA alone (RR 1.54, $95\%$ CI 1.25-1.89), whereas the combination of LDA and LMWH resulted in a similar live birth rate as LDA alone (RR 1.07, $95\%$ CI 0.88-1.29).[23] This conclusion was confirmed by a 2011 RCT, which included 60 aPL-positive women with a history of $\geq 3$ consecutive early pregnancy losses ( $\leq 10$ weeks gestation).[24]

Despite this published evidence, the limitations of the observational studies and RCTs of obstetric APS are very important to consider. # Practical approach to treatment

Given the heterogeneity between studies and the lack of consistent findings from well-designed RCTs, treatment of obstetric APS should be discussed with each patient. In women with aPL and a first pregnancy or previous normal pregnancies, close monitoring of the fetus and mother without specific treatment could be an option. However, LDA might be proposed in consideration of the protective effect of LDA against pre-eclampsia. In women with a history of miscarriages (pregnancy loss at or before 10 weeks gestation) and no previous thrombosis, LDA monotherapy can be used as the first-line therapy, especially in younger patients with several fertile years ahead. In women with a history of fetal losses ( $>10$ weeks gestation) and no previous thrombosis, combination treatment with LDA and prophylactic doses of LMWH could be the first option. In any case in which LDA is indicated, treatment with aspirin should begin before conception. If the combination of LDA and prophylactic-dose LMWH fails, the next step is to combine aspirin with full-dose LMWH. In patients with APS and previous thrombosis, LMWH at full therapeutic dose should be prescribed. As the use of vitamin K antagonists has been linked to fetal

malformations, especially during organogenesis (weeks 6-14 of pregnancy), switching from oral anticoagulation to full-dose LMWH is advised before this period. Oral anticoagulation therapy could be an option for pregnant patients at high risk of thrombosis, such as those with previous arterial events or recurrent thrombotic events, but only after the $14^{\mathrm{th}}$ week of pregnancy. For patients with previous thrombosis, oral anticoagulation could be reinstituted as soon as possible after delivery.

Given that women with obstetric APS are at increased risk of thromboembolism,30 the general recommendation is to treat these patients with LMWH at prophylactic doses for 6 weeks postpartum. However, in patients who did not receive prenatal thromboprophylaxis (that is, those treated with only LDA) and no other risk factors for thrombosis, LMWH should be considered for only 7 days postpartum; if additional risk factors for thrombosis are present, extending prophylactic anticoagulation for 6 weeks should be considered.

# aPL-positive women without definite APS

Another important grey area is the treatment of obstetric APS manifestations in women who do not fulfil either the clinical or serological criteria for APS; for example, women with $\leq 2$ early pregnancy losses and no previous thrombosis, or those with low levels of aCL or a $\beta 2$ GPI antibodies without LAC.[31-33] Some evidence indicates the efficacy of standard treatment of obstetric APS in this subset of patients,[35] but other evidence does not.[36,37] Factors other than past obstetric history or treatment, such as concomitant SLE or a high-risk aPL profile, could be the main determinants of obstetric outcomes in aPL-positive women who do not fulfil the criteria for APS (Table 2). In addition, aPL-positive women are at high risk of pre-eclampsia, an important consideration when deciding the course of treatment (that is, whether to treat with LDA).[38] Further evidence is required to determine the best treatment for these subgroups of aPL-positive women.[25]

# Difficult cases

In this section, we review the evidence for the management of patients with seronegative APS, patients with APS whose aPL titres become persistently negative over time, those with thrombotic and obstetric manifestations refractory to conventional treatment, and those with manifestations not included in the classification criteria. In general, the published evidence regarding the treatment of these cases is scarce and is limited to case series or case reports. Therefore, the recommendations are, in most cases, based on clinical judgement.

# Seronegative APS

In some patients, APS is highly suspected but assays for the aPL included in the serological criteria for APS (LAC and IgG and IgM isotypes of aCL and aβ2GPI antibodies) are persistently negative. In the past 5 years, investigations into so-called seronegative APS have been devoted to determining the presence in these patients of aPL not included in the serological criteria for APS.

In general, clinical manifestations of APS, both thrombotic and obstetric, seem to be associated with antibodies specific to phospholipid-binding plasma (cofactor) proteins (such as phosphatidylethanolamine, prothrombin, protein C, protein S, annexin V and certain domains of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ ), phospholipid-protein complexes (particularly vimentin-cardiolipin complexes), and anionic phospholipids other than cardiolipin (including phosphatidylserine, phosphatidylinositol and phosphatidic acid).39 In this sense, antibodies specific to the vimentin-cardiolipin complex, antibodies that recognize domain I of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ , and IgA aCL and IgA a2GPI antibodies could act as specific tests in cases of seronegative APS.40

From the clinical point of view, a retrospective assessment of 87 patients with definite APS and 67 with seronegative APS found no differences between the groups in terms of thrombotic events or pregnancy morbidity. Interestingly, SLE and Sjögren syndrome were present more often in patients with classic APS than in those with seronegative APS.[41] From the laboratory perspective, several studies have demonstrated the presence of 'non-criteria' aPL in patients with seronegative APS. Thin-layer chromatography immunostaining of sera from 36 patients with seronegative APS detected aCL, anti-lyso(bis)phosphatidic acid and anti-phosphatidylethanolamine antibodies in $47.2\%$ , $41.7\%$ and $30.5\%$ of the patients, respectively.[42] Moreover, another study detected IgG and IgM anti-vimentin-cardiolipin antibodies in 16 $(55.2\%)$ and 11 $(37.9\%)$ of 29 patients with seronegative APS, respectively. Interestingly, the presence of these antibodies was confirmed in all patients in a second sample obtained $\geq 12$ weeks after the first.[43] In a 2014 case series of 24 patients with seronegative APS, 19 $(79.2\%)$ had antibodies to $\geq 1$ aPL cofactor protein.[44] Serological analysis of 40 patients with seronegative APS patients found that three $(7.5\%)$ had IgG antibodies to domain I $\beta 2$ GPI and one patient had IgA a $\beta 2$ GPI antibodies.[45] Moreover, isolated IgA anti- $\beta 2$ GPI antibody positivity was significantly

prevalent in seven $(10.6\%)$ of 66 patients with seronegative APS compared with healthy controls.[46]

With regard to clinical practice, careful differential diagnosis with consideration of other causes of thrombophilia and repeated testing for LAC, IgG and IgM aCL and a $\beta$ 2GPI antibodies are mandatory before the diagnosis of seronegative APS can be made. If the standard aPL assays are persistently negative and given the described evidence,[39-46] the use of more-specific laboratory techniques, such as thin-layer chromatography immunostaining, or the determination of other aPL (antivimentin/cardiolipin, anti-prothrombin, anti-annexinV, anti-phosphatidylethanolamine, anti-domain I of $\beta$ 2GPI, and IgA isotypes of aCL and a $\beta$ 2GPI antibodies) can be very useful. Considering treatment, and regardless of non-criteria-aPL status, common sense dictates that patients with seronegative APS with recurrent thrombosis need long-term anticoagulation therapy, and women with seronegative APS and recurrent pregnancy losses should receive the standard treatment (Table 3).

# Loss of aPL positivity

In general, results of aPL assays remain stable for $\geq 75\%$ of subsequent tests, regardless of the laboratory performing the test.47 In some cases, however, patients who initially tested positive for aPL subsequently, and persistently, test negative for those same antibodies; the factors related to the loss of aPL positivity are unknown. As long-term anticoagulation therapy is associated with haemorrhagic complications, the question arises of whether it is possible to withdraw this therapy in selected patients who no longer test positive for aPL.

To date, only two studies have investigated the safety of withdrawing anticoagulation therapy in patients with APS in whom aPL are no longer detected.48,49 In the first study, of six patients with primary APS who became persistently negative for aPL, no new thrombotic events occurred in the follow-up period $(21\pm 4.9$ months) after

anticoagulation withdrawal. Of note, only one patient had presented with LAC (in only one determination) and most of the patients presented with aCL titres $< 40$ GPL or MPL.[48] In the second study, a series of 11 patients with APS, no new thrombotic episodes were observed after a median of 20 months without thromboprophylaxis.[49] The aPL that had been most frequently detected in these patients was LAC, present in nine $(82\%)$ of the 11 patients. Taken together, these results seem to suggest that, in some patients with primary APS whose aPL become persistently negative, thromboprophylaxis can be withdrawn. However, the question of whether treatment should be stopped after spontaneous disappearance of aPL needs further study. Notably, none of the patients in these two studies had presented with arterial thrombosis. Before withdrawal of anticoagulation therapy can be recommended in clinical practice, the clinical relevance of the disappearance of aPL needs to be further clarified (Table 3).

# Refractory cases

The management of patients with recurrent thrombotic events despite optimal anticoagulation therapy is difficult, and no evidence is available on which to base recommendations. During the 10-year follow-up period of the Europhospholipid cohort, which included 1,000 patients with APS, recurrent thrombosis occurred in one-third of the patients.[50]

A systematic review of the literature showed that most patients treated with warfarin who had a recurrence of thrombosis had an actual INR $< 3.0$ at the time of event. Conversely, the rate of recurrent events was low among patients receiving oral anticoagulation therapy at an INR of $3.0 - 4.0$ . Given this evidence, in patients with APS and recurrent thrombotic events, the first therapy is to guarantee a correct INR (that is, frequent monitoring to ensure the INR is within the target range, $2.0 - 3.0$ ). In those cases with correct INR but with recurrence of thrombosis, the second step should be to increase the intensity of anticoagulation therapy (target INR $>3.0$ ). In patients who cannot achieve an adequate INR or those who experience recurrent thrombosis despite high-intensity anticoagulation therapy, switching from oral anticoagulation to long-term use of LMWH or the addition to oral anticoagulation of aspirin or hydroxychloroquine could be considered, but this recommendation is not evidence-based.[51] Finally, rituximab has been used in a few patients with primary APS in order to decrease the titre of aPL (and, therefore, thrombotic risk) with variable efficacy (Table 3).[52-54]

Regarding obstetric morbidity, live births cannot be achieved in approximately $20\%$ of patients with APS, despite combination therapy with LDA plus LMWH.[25] Various therapeutic strategies, including starting LDA at least 4 weeks before conception, starting LMWH as early in the pregnancy as possible or increasing the dose of heparin (from prophylactic to therapeutic dose), can be considered.[55] The addition of hydroxychloroquine during pregnancy[55] or low-dose prednisone in the first trimester[57,58] to standard therapy can also be regarded as

valid therapeutic options. Finally, the use of intravenous immunoglobulin therapy in association with LDA and LMWH could be another option in patients with persistent adverse pregnancy outcomes despite different lines of treatment, although reports of the efficacy of this approach are contradictory (Table 3).[59-62]

# 'Non-criteria' clinical manifestations

Some clinical features are related to aPL but not included in the APS classification criteria, such as haematologic manifestations (thrombocytopenia and haemolytic anaemia), neurologic manifestations (chorea, myelitis or multiple sclerosis-like lesions), aPL-associated nephropathy and heart valve disease. In general, the evidence for the treatment of these manifestations is limited to case series or case reports and is insufficient to form general recommendations. Nevertheless, given the demonstrated high risk of thrombosis in aPL-positive patients, primary thromboprophylaxis should be considered to decrease the risk of thrombosis developing in any vascular territory (Table 4).

# Haematologic manifestations

The reported prevalence of thrombocytopenia in patients with APS ranges from $20\%$ to $44\%$ . [63,64] Thrombocytopenia in APS is usually of moderate severity (platelet count $>50 \times 10^{9} / \mathrm{l}$ ) and, therefore, rarely requires treatment. [65] However, in those patients with symptomatic thrombocytopenia that results in bleeding, the same treatments that are used for immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP), such as steroids, intravenous immunoglobulins, immunosuppressant agents and splenectomy, should be considered. In a pilot study of rituximab for non-criteria manifestations of APS, thrombocytopenia with haemorrhagic diathesis was the most common indication, in 10 (41.7%) of 24 patients. Interestingly, three patients had a complete response (platelet count $>150 \times 10^{9} / \mu \mathrm{l}$ ), four had a partial response (platelet count $50 - 150 \times 10^{9} / \mu \mathrm{l}$ ), and the remaining three had persistent thrombocytopenia (platelet count $< 50 \times 10^{9} / \mu \mathrm{l}$ ) following treatment with rituximab. [52] In 2014, we described two patients with SLE and refractory thrombocytopenia who were treated with thrombopoietin-receptor agonists; one of the patients was aPL-positive and achieved a complete response (platelet count $>100 \times 10^{9} / \mu \mathrm{l}$ ) to eltrombopag. [66]

A high risk of thrombosis has been demonstrated in aPL-positive patients without thrombosis and in patients with APS and thrombocytopenia.[67-69] Therefore, primary thromboprophylaxis with aspirin or hydroxychloroquine could be necessary in selected aPL-positive patients with thrombocytopenia, mainly in the presence of vascular risk factors or LAC.[68] In case of the coexistence of thrombosis and a haemorrhagic event associated with low platelet count, the combination of steroids and anticoagulation therapy, starting when platelet count has reached safe levels $(>50\times 10^{9} / \mu l)$ , is probably the more advisable option.

# Neurologic manifestations

Chorea, transverse myelitis and multiple sclerosis-like lesions are examples of clinical manifestations involving the central nervous system that are associated with aPL but not included in the APS classification criteria.

Chorea is the movement disorder most frequently described in patients with primary APS, being present in $1.3\%$ of patients in the Europhospholipid cohort.[64] Transverse myelitis is less frequent than chorea in patients with primary APS, with an estimated prevalence

of $< 1\%$ ,[73] and mainly affects the thoracic segment of the spinal cord. This disorder can cause a high degree of morbidity in the form of sensory loss below the level of the lesion, and motor and sphincter disturbances. In a study of 70 patients with SLE and transverse myelitis, aPL were detected in $54\%$ .[74] In a review that identified 22 patients with SLE and longitudinally extensive myelitis (affecting $>4$ spinal segments), half of the patients had aPL but only one had definite APS.[75]

APS can mimic multiple sclerosis but no definitive tests to differentiate these entities are available. The reported prevalence of aPL in patients with multiple sclerosis ranges between $2\%$ and $88\%$ ,[76] and, in general, aPL frequency is higher in exacerbations of the disease.[77]

With regard to the pathophysiology of neurologic manifestations of APS, direct binding of aPL to antigens located in central nervous system, rather than a thrombotic mechanism, has been postulated. In fact, studies in several animal models of APS have demonstrated that aPL can bind to neuronal structures.[78,79] Taking into account this potential autoimmune/inflammatory mechanism, antiplatelet or anticoagulant drugs would not be specifically indicated for these nonvascular neurologic manifestations of APS. On the one hand, good response to anticoagulation therapy has been described in patients with SLE, aPL and myelopathy.[80]

<!-- page 8 -->
Conversely, anticoagulation therapy did not contribute any additional therapeutic effect when added to immunosuppressive regimens in patients with SLE, aPL and transverse myelitis.[81] Symptomatic therapy with antidopaminergic drugs or dopamine-depleting agents are effective for chorea.[82] However, no standard treatment for aPL-associated neurologic manifestations not included in the APS classification criteria is available, and the role of anti-inflammatory drugs such as steroids, immunosuppressive agents or rituximab is unknown. Following the latest EULAR recommendations for the management of SLE with neuropsychiatric manifestations, antiplatelet agents or anticoagulation therapy can be considered in severe cases of patients with SLE and aPL.[83]

Clinical judgement dictates that, in an aPL-positive patient with non-criteria neurologic manifestations, antiplatelet or anticoagulation therapy for 6 months, in addition to steroids and immunosuppressive agents, could be reasonable for the treatment of selected patients, such as those with atypical features of multiple sclerosis or transverse myelitis.

# Heart valve disease

Heart valve abnormalities are the most common cardiac manifestations of APS,[84] with valve thickening and/or dysfunction prevalent in $11.6\%$ and vegetations in $2.7\%$ of patients.[64] The international consensus statement on the classification of APS provides definitions of aPL-associated heart valve lesions but does not include these features as criteria for APS.[1]

Regarding its overall prevalence, echocardiographic studies found heart valve abnormalities of any type in one-third of patients with primary APS.[85-89] Follow-up studies demonstrated that initial valvular lesions can worsen, improve, disappear or remain unchanged over time; moreover, new valvular lesions might develop.[90-93] Interestingly, anticoagulation, antiplatelet and immunosuppressive therapies are unable to reverse established valvular lesions or prevent their appearance.[94] However, scarce evidence from a few case reports indicates the possible beneficial effects of corticosteroids and immunosuppressive therapy for valvular heart disease.[95,96] These data have been corroborated by a 10-year follow-up study performed in 82 patients (17 with primary APS, 23 with SLE-associated APS, 19 with SLE and aPL but not APS, and 23 with SLE and negative for aPL).[97] In the aforementioned open-label phase II trial of rituximab for non-criteria manifestations of APS, none of the three patients in the study with valvular disease achieved a response to this therapy (defined as no change or worsening of echocardiographic lesions).[52]

Regarding treatment, an expert committee on cardiac disease in APS at a consensus conference held during the $10^{\mathrm{th}}$ International Congress on Antiphospholipid Antibodies recommended anticoagulation therapy for patients with aPL at high risk of thromboembolic events secondary to valvular heart disease, but not for the treatment of the valve dysfunction itself. In asymptomatic patients without previous thrombosis and only heart

valve lesions (without valvular dysfunction or vegetations), prophylactic LDA should be considered. The administration of corticosteroids is not recommended for valvular disease.[98] It is important to keep in mind that antibiotic prophylaxis against infective endocarditis in patients with primary or SLE-associated APS and valve lesions is not needed.[99]

Only $4 - 6\%$ of patients with valvular heart disease develop valvulopathy severe enough to require surgical treatment.[94] However, morbidity and mortality are high in patients with APS undergoing valve-replacement surgery, with most complications related to thrombosis and bleeding. In a 2010 case review of nine patients with APS who underwent heart valve surgery (mitral valve replacement, aortic valve replacement, combined mitral-aortic valve replacement or aortic valvuloplasty),[100] two patients died within days of surgery due to acute cerebrovascular accidents. During the follow-up period (median 66 months), one patient had an ischaemic stroke 5 years after mitral valve replacement, another required heart transplantation 3 years after surgery because of congestive heart failure, and a third patient presented with multiple cerebral haemorrhagic and ischaemic events (one subdural haematoma and two ischaemic strokes) leading to permanent sequelae. A meta-analysis that included 57 patients with APS who underwent heart valve surgery described a high mortality rate, with 'early' death in $7\%$ and 'late' death in $12\%$ after a mean follow-up period of $< 3$ years.[101] These results are very poor compared with operative mortality for aortic valve replacement $(2\%)$ and mitral valve replacement $(3\%)$ for patients with an average age of 66-70 years.[102] Finally, a 2012 study reviewed the outcomes of 33 valve replacements (23 mechanical and 10 tissue valves) in 32 patients with APS.[103] In post-surgery follow-up (median 33.5 months), the mortality rate was $12.5\%$ ; one patient died within 1 month of surgery and the remaining three afterward. Regarding post-surgery complications, eight patients presented with major bleeding and five with thrombotic events. Considering the type of valve implanted, tissue valves have a theoretical advantage over mechanical prostheses with respect to risk of thromboembolic phenomena; however, thromboses of homograft valves have been described.[104] In any case, anticoagulation therapy must be carefully managed to avoid haemorrhagic and thrombotic complications after surgery.

# Nephropathy associated with aPL

Chronic or acute renal lesions can occur in association with aPL, a phenomenon termed aPL-associated nephropathy. Thrombotic microangiopathy is considered the best-known renal lesion related to aPL, and represents an acute event. By contrast, chronic renal damage can present as fibrous intimal hyperplasia, fibrocellular arterial occlusion, focal cortical atrophy and tubular thyroidization.1 Overall, the reported prevalence of aPL-associated nephropathy in patients with SLE varies from $10.4\%$ to $34\%$ .105,106 In the setting of APS not associated with SLE, histological evidence of acute or chronic aPL-associated nephropathy was found in only $2.5\%$ of

<!-- page 9 -->
a cohort of 160 patients.[107] Despite this low prevalence in (non-SLE) APS, aPL-associated nephropathy has been related to poor renal outcome.[108] However, the real prevalence of this manifestation is probably underestimated. The risk of haemorrhagic events in a patient with APS and previous thrombosis on anticoagulation therapy precludes renal biopsy, which would otherwise be indicated in the presence of haematuria, variable degrees of proteinuria or renal insufficiency.

Considering treatment, in the setting of definite APS, anticoagulation therapy is mandatory to prevent thrombotic recurrence in the kidney or in any other vascular territory.[1,109] However, the role of anticoagulation therapy in the evolution of renal function is unknown, owing to the limited number of patients and limited follow-up period in the majority of case series.[106]

Consensus is lacking on the treatment of aPL-associated nephropathy in patients without previous clinical features of APS (in most cases, patients with SLE and aPL but without definite APS). Joint guidelines from EULAR and the European Renal Association-European Dialysis and Transplant Association recommended that hydroxychloroquine, antiplatelet or anticoagulant treatment should be considered in patients with lupus nephritis and aPL-associated nephropathy (strength of recommendation C; recommendation based on prospective or retrospective non-randomized controlled study). In any case of aPL-associated nephropathy, strict control of arterial hypertension and proteinuria with angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors and angiotensin-receptor blockers is highly recommended. Two patients with aPL-associated nephropathy were included in the open-label phase II trial of rituximab for non-criteria manifestations of APS; one patient withdrew early because of an infusion reaction, and the other had a partial response to treatment with rituximab.

In most studies of patients undergoing renal transplantation, aPL have been related to thrombosis (at any site of renal vasculature) leading to high risk of graft failure, both early renal allograft failure and accelerated progression of chronic vascular changes.[111] Unfortunately, no recommendations on the anticoagulation regimen during renal transplantation are available. On the one hand, the risk of systemic or renal vasculature thrombosis is high in aPL-positive patients; on the other hand, the risk of graft failure due to major bleeding should be considered. In addition, the efficacy of anticoagulation therapy in preventing thrombotic microangiopathy is uncertain.[112]

# Conclusions

Currently, treatment of patients with APS is based on long-term oral anticoagulation therapy for thrombotic manifestations and the combination of LDA and LMWH to prevent obstetric manifestations. However, the lack of well-designed studies means that the strength of some recommendations is debatable. Examples of these recommendations under discussion include the best range of anticoagulation for arterial thrombosis, or the best treatment for recurrent early miscarriages for women with APS or for the clinical manifestations of aPL-positive patients who do not fulfil formal (clinical or serological) classification criteria for APS. In other scenarios, such as patients with seronegative APS, those with recurrent thrombotic events despite optimal anticoagulation, or those with clinical manifestations associated with aPL but not included in the APS classification criteria, evidence regarding treatment does not exist. In these cases, recommendations are based on clinical judgement. In general, common sense would recommend the use of long-term anticoagulation in these cases, but without scientific evidence.
