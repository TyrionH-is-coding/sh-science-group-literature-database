# Antibodies Against Complement Components: Relevance for the Antiphospholipid Syndrome—Biomarkers of the Disease and Biopharmaceuticals

Mirjana Bećarevic<sup>1</sup>

Published online: 19 June 2017

$\langle \widehat{\mathbb{C}}\rangle$ Springer Science+Business Media New York 2017

# Abstract

Purpose of Review Laboratory criterion for the diagnosis of antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL Abs). Complement system has a role in mediating aPL Abs-induced thrombosis in animal models. The importance of antibodies against complement components (potential biomarkers of APS) and the importance of antibodies with beneficial anti-complement effects in APS (as biopharmaceuticals) are reviewed.

Recent Findings Antibodies against complement components described in APS patients, so far, are anti-C1q and anti-factor H Abs, although anti-factor B Abs and anti-C5a Abs were described in animal models of APS. Clinical studies in APS patients are limited to a small number of case reports.

Summary Studies that would confirm potential role of Abs against complement components (as potential biomarkers of APS) are lacking. Lack of randomized clinical trials (that would provide complete data for confirmation of beneficial effects of biopharmaceuticals in complement inhibition) in APS is alarming.

Keywords Antiphospholipid antibodies $\cdot$ Antibodies against complement components $\cdot$ Biomarkers $\cdot$ Biopharmaceuticals $\cdot$ Complement

# Introduction

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an autoimmune disease, and it can be primary (PAPS), secondary (SAPS), and catastrophic (CAPS). Primary APS is not associated with another diseases, while SAPS is associated with other autoimmune disease (most often systemic lupus erythematosus, SLE). Life-threatening CAPS is characterized by multiple thromboses of various vital organs [1-5], and although less than $1\%$ of APS patients will develop CAPS, high mortality in this subgroup is alarming [6, 7]. The reasons for the development of recurrent thrombotic episodes predominantly affecting large vessels (classic or simple APS) versus rapid development of small vessels thrombosis (CAPS) are not elucidated, yet [4, 5, 7].

Clinical manifestations of APS are thrombosis (arterial and/or venous) and/or recurrent pregnancy losses (RPL), and terms obstetric APS (OAPS) and vascular or thrombotic APS (VAPS) were introduced [8-10]. In addition, some of the features of CAPS are similar to the manifestations of the systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) which is characterized by excessive cytokine release from necrotic tissues [4, 5, 7].

Laboratory criteria for the diagnosis of APS are the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL Abs anticardiolipin (aCL), anti-beta2 glycoprotein I (aβ2gpI), and lupus anticoagulant (LA)). The group of Abs against PLs is increasing, and it was reported that complement system has a role in mediating aPL Abs-induced thrombosis in animal models [11]. Therefore, complement inhibition can be beneficial in autoimmune diseases where complement system is chronically activated and attacks or damages healthy tissues [12\bullet]. Currently, the majority of approaches to inhibit complement has focused either on blocking it at the level of C3 implying a

general and broad inhibition of the system or on selective blocking of C5 activation with subsequent inhibition of C5a and C5b-9 (formation of membrane attack complex (MAC)) [13].

Therefore, the focus of this article is, on the one hand, on the importance of various antibodies against complement components as potential biomarkers of the APS, and on the other hand, on antibodies that have beneficial therapeutic effects in APS patients, i.e., application as biopharmaceuticals (biopharmaceuticals are protein-based therapeutics (or peptide-based therapeutics) that express various mechanisms of action) [14].

# Complement Activation in Antiphospholipid Syndrome

The complement system is comprised of approximately 50 proteins (including activators, regulators, and receptors for complement components) that contribute to elimination of various "threatening structures" (i.e., microorganisms, apoptotic cells). The activation of the complement system can be initiated by classical, alternative, or lectin pathways. The "early steps" of the complement activation are characterized

by the production of C3b, which initiates the "late steps" of complement activation (includes production of pro-inflammatory, pro-thrombotic anaphylatoxins (i.e., C5a, C3a, C4a), polymerization of C9, and formation of MAC) [15, 16]. Table 1 summarizes the basic characteristics of proteins involved in "early" and "late" steps of complement activation.

The dysregulation in the complement system (deficiencies of particular complement components, mutations, etc.) can trigger tissue damage and inflammation [16]. It was reported that deficiencies of the "early step components" of the classical pathway (C1q, C1r, C1s, C2, C4) or genetic deficiency of mannose-binding lectin (MBL) is connected with autoimmune diseases (especially with SLE-like diseases) due to impaired clearance of apoptotic cells and/or immune complexes or due to aberrant tolerance induction [17, 18].

The precise role of complement components in APS is not completely elucidated due to the fact that causes of tissue injury in APS are multifactorial [19, 20]. Animal models of APS (Table 2) [21-29] have revealed that complement activation has a fundamental role for the induction of thrombosis and pregnancy losses associated with the presence of aPL Abs, but the exact origin of complement activation in APS is not elucidated, yet. Interestingly, it was reported that in the APS setting, complement pathway can be activated in the situation when C1q binds to phospholipid-binding proteins and/or phospholipids (i.e., in the absence of immune complexes) [35].

Usually, the classical pathway is initiated by activation of C1 (it is a multimeric protein complex composed of C1q, C1r, and C1s subunits). This activation occurs when C1q binds to Fc regions of IgG or IgM Abs (i.e., CH2 domain of $\gamma$ heavy chains of IgG molecules or CH3 domain of $\mu$ heavy chains of the IgM molecules) in immune complexes. In order to initiate classical pathway of complement activation, at least two Fc regions of Abs have to be accessible to C1q. Considering the fact that IgG has only one Fc region, it is necessary that multimerization occurs, and this happens when multiple IgG molecules are brought close together (this is possible only when they bind to a multivalent antigen). Among IgG Abs, the most efficient complement activators are IgG3 and IgG1 subclasses. It was demonstrated that C1q binding to IgG happens through immunoglobulin hexamers (they are formed after IgG binds antigens expressed on cell surfaces). The specific non-covalent interactions between Fc segments of

neighboring IgGs are responsible for formation of these IgG hexamers [12\*, 15].

The Fc regions of pentameric IgM molecules are in configuration that is not accessible to C1q, and therefore circulating (free) IgM do not activate complement. However, complexes between antigens and IgM induce conformational changes that lead to exposure of C1q binding sites in the Fc regions of IgM. A single pentameric IgM can bind two C1q molecules, and this is the explanation for the fact that IgM Abs are more efficient complement-binding (fixing) Abs than IgG are. In addition, IgM Abs also exist in hexameric form, and this form has even higher complement-activating potential than pentameric IgM [12].

The additional factors that have influence on classical complement activation pathway are flexibility of the hinge regions of immunoglobulins, heterogeneity of glycans in CH2 domains, and factors related to antigen (size and geometry, antigen density, and epitope positioning) [12]. The major antigen target for anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{gpI}$ Abs in APS patients is $\beta 2\mathrm{gpI}$ and in addition, Gropp et al. [36] have reported that $\beta 2\mathrm{gpI}$ also functions as a complement regulator in humans. Skerka et al. [37] have published that upon binding to apoptotic cells, $\beta 2\mathrm{gpI}$ changes conformation and acquires C3/C3b binding activities and it also induces C3 conformation so that factor H binds to this modified form of C3. Factor I degrades C3/C3b, and this inhibits further complement activation. In addition, $\beta 2\mathrm{gpI}$ mediated increased C3/C3b degradation, and smaller C3 cleavage products are generated (in comparison to factor H/I).

# Complement and Features (Clinical and/or Laboratory) of the Antiphospholipid Syndrome

Antiphospholipid Abs exert pro-coagulant effects, and complex interactions between endothelial cells (EC), platelets, leukocytes, and these Abs in combination with an additional predisposing factor(s) (so-called "second hit") have a role in initiating the coagulation process in APS setting [11]. Johnson and Hetland [38] have reported that EC can synthesize and secrete the components of the alternative complement pathway and late components of complement activation. Van den Berg et al. [39] reported that in an inflammatory response C1q activated the classical complement pathway and induced recruitment of inflammatory cells and production of factors involved in acute phase response.

Oku et al. [40] have investigated the prevalence of hypocomplementemia in 36 PAPS patients, 42 patients with non-SLE connective tissue disease, and 36 healthy subjects and have concluded that PAPS patients had lower C3 and C4 levels than other two analyzed groups of subjects. The abovementioned study [40] is in agreement with previously published study [41] in which it was reported that PAPS patients had significantly lower C3 levels than control subjects.

In addition, the same authors [41] have reported that despite the fact that decreased C4 concentrations were the most frequently observed in PAPS patients (in comparison to SAPS and control subjects), this finding did not reach statistical significance. In addition, the authors [41] have reported that serum C3 and C4 levels were not significantly different between PAPS and SAPS patients. Interestingly, the same authors [41] have reported that hsCRP and C3 concentrations were in positive correlation in PAPS patients, while an inverse correlation between C4 and the IgG isotype of aCL Abs was observed in SAPS patients.

Due to an inverse correlation between C3 or C4 and increased levels of C3a and C4a, the authors [40] have concluded that hypocomplementemia in PAPS is a consequence of complement components consumption and activation of complement pathway. However, C5 levels were in normal range and C5a was not detected in analyzed PAPS patients (which is in discrepancy with findings obtained from animal models of thrombosis and pregnancy losses, where anaphylatoxins (C4a, C3a and C5a) had a fundamental role in the pathogenesis of the abovementioned features of APS due to facts that they increase vascular permeability, activate platelets, stimulate release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and therefore promote coagulation and systemic inflammation [42]). In addition, complement regulatory factors (factors H and I) were not decreased in PAPS patients, and therefore, the authors [40] suggested that complement activation was not caused by deficiencies of these factors. Interestingly, the authors did not observe correlation between hypocomplementemia and clinical features of the APS [40], which is in concordance with a previously mentioned study [41].

Peerschke et al. [20] have analyzed 169 serum samples of SLE and 153 PAPS patients and reported that no significant difference in serum C4 and C3 levels between two subgroups of SLE patients (aPL Abs positive vs. aPL Abs negative SLE subgroups) was observed. However, the authors did not present serum C4 and C3 levels for patients with PAPS. In addition, the same authors [20] have reported that sera from aPL Abs positive patients had increased ability for activation of the classical complement pathway (this was defined with C1q and C4d deposits on heterologous platelets). In addition, in aPL Abs positive patients with SLE, deposition of C1q and C4d was associated with higher incidence of arterial thrombotic events. However, this association was not observed in group of PAPS patients [20].

Navratil et al. [43] have reported that levels of platelet C4d were specific for SLE patients and that C4d levels were significantly associated with positivity for the IgG and IgM isotypes of aCL Abs and with LA positivity in a study that included 105 female SLE patients, 115 patients with other rheumatic or hematologic diseases, and 100 healthy control subjects. In addition, the authors have reported that platelet C4d levels were significantly associated with low serum C4

<!-- page 5 -->
levels and with abnormal erythrocyte C4d levels in female SLE patients [43].

Becarevic and Ignjatovic [44] have reported that decreased C3c and C4 were more frequent in female than in male PAPS patients. In addition, decreased C3c levels were observed in female PAPS patients with increased titers of the IgG isotype of anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{gpI}$ Abs and in male PAPS patients with increased titers of the IgM isotype of aCL Abs. Interestingly, female PAPS patients with increased apolipoprotein (a) levels and deep venous thrombosis had significantly decreased C4 levels, while in male PAPS patients with cerebrovascular insults, the increased levels of the IgG isotype of aCL Abs were significantly associated with the decreased C3c levels.

Barratt-Due et al. [45] have reported that low C3 and C4 levels in female CAPS patients might indicate complement activation with consumption. They suggested that the ratio between an activation product and its parent protein is an indicator of complement activation. Therefore, high C4bc/C4 ratio was an indicator of increased classical and/or lectin pathway, while increased C3bBbP/C3 ratio was an indicator that the alternative pathway is activated in CAPS. High levels of sC5b-9 indicated that the terminal pathway was activated in CAPS [45].

# Antibodies Against Complement Components in Antiphospholipid Syndrome

Traditionally, complement system is involved in host's defense against microorganisms and in various physiological processes. However, complement activation is also associated with numerous diseases (due to either excessive activation or insufficient activation or both). In addition, complement components might become targets themselves of host's immune response resulting in generation of autoAbs against complement components [46**, 47].

Numerous Abs against complement components have been described in various diseases. In patients with SLE, some of these Abs are anti-C1q, anti-C1s, anti-CR1, and anti-CR3 Abs, anti-factor H, anti-ficolin 3, anti-MBL, anti-C4b2a, anti-C1 inhibitor Abs, etc. [46\*, 47, 48]. However, clinical relevance for majority of these Abs is not elucidated, yet.

The only Abs against complement components described in APS patients, so far, are anti-C1q and anti-factor H Abs, although anti-factor B Abs and anti-C5a Abs were described in animal models of APS [21].

# Anti-C1q Antibodies

The prevalence of anti-C1q Abs in SLE patients is approximately up to $50\%$ [47], and these Abs correlated with lupus nephritis [46, 47, 49].

Oku et al. [42] have analyzed the prevalence of anti-C1q Abs in 40 PAPS, 42 patients with non-SLE connective tissue diseases, 20 SLE patients negative for aPL Abs, and 20 age- and gender-matched healthy control subjects. The authors have reported that anti-C1q Abs were present in $36\%$ of PAPS patients, in $2.5\%$ of patients with non-SLE connective tissue diseases, in $55\%$ of SLE patients, and in $0\%$ of healthy control subjects. In addition, the authors reported that anti-C1q Abs correlated with serum C4a concentrations in PAPS patients, but no association between titers of aPL Abs and anti-C1q Abs was observed. Also, anti-C1q Abs were not associated with either clinical manifestation of the APS. However, due to the fact that refractory PAPS patients had higher prevalence of anti-C1q Abs than PAPS patients without recurrence, the authors concluded that anti-C1q Abs are associated with complement activation in PAPS and contribute to the pathogenesis of refractory PAPS.

Oku et al. [42] have suggested that anti-C1q Abs might be regarded as one of aPL Abs due to the fact that they bind to C1q on anionic phospholipids (their expression is provoked on cell surfaces in response to stressors like tissue injuries or infections). This hypothesis is in agreement with the "second hit theory" (the sole presence of aPL Abs is not enough to initiate thrombus formation and a second hit, such as abovementioned stressors, is necessary).

# Anti-Factor H Abs

Factor H is considered as the main regulatory protein of the complement alternative pathway [50], and it hinders the formation of C3bBb complex by competing with the factor B (FB) to bind to C3b, and it acts as a cofactor for the factor I (FI)-mediated proteolytic inactivation of C3b to iC3b. Generally, the dysfunctions of FH lead to renal diseases, such as C3 glomerulopathy and atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome [50].

Foltyn Zadura et al. [51] have determined the levels of Abs against complement factor H (FH) (anti-FH Abs) in two cohorts of patients with PAPS and SAPS (from Serbia and Italy) and in patients with venous thromboembolism (from Sweden). The authors have reported that anti-FH Abs were significantly higher in both cohorts of APS patients in comparison to healthy subjects. The anti-FH Abs were observed in 13.7 and in $33.3\%$ of Serbian $(n = 73)$ and Italian $(n = 15)$ SAPS patients, respectively. The frequency of anti-FH Abs in Serbian $(n = 33)$ and Italian $(n = 25)$ SAPS patients was 30.3 and $36\%$ , respectively. In addition, the authors have reported that PAPS patients with a history of venous thrombosis had significantly increased anti-FH Abs levels, and this finding was associated with worse clinical outcome.

# Biopharmaceuticals in Antiphospholipid Syndrome

Standard treatments of primary thromboprophylaxis in APS patients include low-dose aspirin and/or hydroxychloroquine, while vitamin K antagonists are recommended for prevention of recurrent thrombosis [52]. The use of low-molecular weight heparin, statins, rituximab, immunosuppressive drugs, and intravenous immunoglobulins is recommended for treatment of refractory and difficult cases [52-54]. The treatments of CAPS patients (in addition to treatment of precipitating factors) include combinations of anticoagulants (against thrombosis), glucocorticoids (against SIRS manifestations), intravenous immunoglobulins, and plasma exchange [4, 5, 7].

Due to the fact that more data is obtained in regard to the pathophysiology of the APS, new targeted treatments of the disease are possible. Antiphospholipid syndrome as an autoimmune disease is characterized by complement activation, and therefore, it was hypothesized that complement inhibition can be beneficial in APS patients and that such inhibition can be achieved by targeting components of the complement cascade involved in the "early" steps, amplification, and/or "late" steps of the complement system.

Studies with complement knock-out animals or with specific complement inhibitors have revealed the importance of complement in the pathogenesis of inflammatory tissue destruction [13]. However, there are only two biopharmaceuticals in current clinical use: monoclonal Abs against C5 (eculizumab) and preparations of C1-esterase inhibitor (C1-INH) [16, 55]. Focus of this article is on Abs against complement components and therefore following section will analyze only the relevance of Abs as biopharmaceuticals in the treatment of APS.

# Eculizumab

Eculizumab is a recombinant humanized monoclonal IgG kappa Ab with a designed Fc portion that is a hybrid of IgG2 and IgG4 (i.e., constant regions (CRs) of human IgG2 and IgG4 and murine complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) are "grafted" on human heavy- and light-chain variable regions (VRs)) [56-58]. Eculizumab has a molecular weight of $148\mathrm{kDa}$ and is composed of two heavy chains (each comprised of 448 amino acids) and two light chains (each comprised of 214 amino acids) [56-58].

It functions as a long-acting blocking Ab (i.e., eculizumab is attached to its target until that complex is removed from the circulation). Target for eculizumab is C5 and by blocking C5, eculizumab inhibits the cleavage of C5 into pro-inflammatory, pro-thrombotic C5a and C5b and in this way, eculizumab prevents formation of MAC. Due to the fact that eculizumab inhibits only the terminal complement system, the proximal complement system remains functional (responsible for

opsonization of microorganisms and for the clearance of immune complexes) [16].

Eculizumab is currently being assessed in more than 20 clinical trials for various conditions [16]. Previously, eculizumab is approved for the treatment of paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH) and atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS), and it has also been investigated in human transplantation for prevention and/or treatment of antibody-mediated rejection [55, 59, 60] and lupus nephritis [61, 62]. Chandran et al. [56] have reported beneficial use of eculizumab for the treatment of de novo thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) in a 34-year-old female recipient of a simultaneous pancreas-kidney transplant, while Stewart et al. [63] have reported that eculizumab may be considered as salvage therapy for isolated cases of ABO incompatible kidney allografts with severe Ab-mediated rejection (refractory to standard treatment with plasmapheresis, splenectomy, and rituximab).

Considering the fact that most common precipitating factor in CAPS is infection, it was reasonable to include biopharmaceuticals that would target C5 component and in this way complement cascade would be inhibited at C5, but immune-mediated (C3b) mechanisms to combat infections will be preserved.

Experiments based on passive immunization of mice with human aPL IgG Abs provide evidence that multiple complement pathways were implicated in the pathogenesis of APS and administration of anti-murine C5 Abs reduced both femoral thrombosis [26] and fetal loss [28] in these models. However, clinical studies in APS patients are limited to a small number of case reports.

Shapira et al. [6] have reported that treatment with eculizumab of 28-year-old male CAPS patient (previously resistant to anticoagulation, immunosuppression and plasma exchange therapy) was associated with no additional recurrent thrombotic episodes during more than 3 years of therapy.

Although impairment of renal function and renal failure is a common finding in CAPS, kidney transplantation is usually contraindicated in these patients due to lack of specific therapies that would prevent recurrence of CAPS. Lonze et al. [64] have reported successful renal transplantation (live donor) in a 51-year-old male CAPS patient with prophylactic administration of eculizumab (in addition to systemic anticoagulation and immunosuppression).

Gustavsen et al. [65] have reported successful administration of eculizumab (600 mg weekly for 2 weeks, in addition to anticoagulation therapy) to a 22-year-old pregnant APS patient with recurrent arterial thrombosis, digital amputations, and with high risk of developing CAPS. The authors did not observe APS-related complications, and healthy infant was delivered.

Barratt-Due et al. [45] reported successful use of eculizumab in the case of 34-year-old female CAPS patients,

<!-- page 7 -->
while Wig et al. [66] have reported that eculizumab had beneficial effects in 43-year-old female with relapsing and refractory case of CAPS.

Sheridan et al. [67] have reported that high-affinity binding of eculizumab for C5 resulted in complete inhibition of the terminal complement and in rapid target-mediated clearance. However, in disagreement, Harder et al. [68] provided mechanistic evidence for incomplete terminal pathway inhibition under eculizumab.

Sheridan et al. [67] have reported that half-life $(\mathrm{t}_{1/2})$ for eculizumab was $11.3 \pm 3.4$ days and bi-weekly dosing of eculizumab was enough to secure sustained and complete inhibition of terminal complement activity in patients with PNH and aHUS.

It was reported that weekly doses of $900 - 1200\mathrm{mg}$ of eculizumab in acute phase were used in CAPS patients, with "decrease in dosage frequency to $900\mathrm{mg}$ (administered every 2 weeks) after effervescence" [4, 5]. However, adequate duration of the treatment with eculizumab in CAPS patients is not elucidated yet due to rarity of the syndrome. It was reported that eculizumab is well-tolerated and relatively safe biopharmaceutical and that the main adverse reactions were nausea, nasopharyngitis, headache, and back pain [56]. However, meningococcal vaccination is recommended prior to administration of eculizumab (in addition to prophylactic antibiotic therapy) due to the fact that administration of eculizumab is linked with severe susceptibility to meningococcal infection [56]. In addition, there are reports of patients that were poor responders to eculizumab due to genetic variants in C5 [70].

# Next Generations of Abs Against C5 (ALXN1210, ALXN5500, and LFG316)

Sheridan et al. [67] described a new anti-C5 mAb (ALXN1210) which was engineered to minimize target-mediated clearance and to increase recycling efficiency of mAb via neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn). These modifications resulted in longer $t_{1/2}$ (in comparison to eculizumab and dosing intervals of at least once per month) in the presence of C5 and human FcRn by more than three-fold, while preserving inhibition of terminal complement activity.

Another antibody, designated as ALXN5500, has improved half-life and is intended for subcutaneous application [16, 71].

An antibody designated LFG316 is fully human anti-C5 antibody that blocks terminal pathway activation in humans [16].

Emerging new biopharmaceuticals are focusing on inhibition of C5-C5a axis [72] (interference/blocking C5a or its major receptor C5aR1 (CD88)) [13, 73]. In this context, anti-C5a Abs are in the process of evaluation for the treatment of APS patients (NCT02128269) [16].

In addition to abovementioned Abs, it is interesting to mention several C5 inhibitors that are not yet approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and/or European Medicines Agency (EMEA) (i.e., they are in various stages of experimental/clinical investigations). Some of these C5 inhibitors are a recombinant anti-C5 minibody (Mubodina); C5-binding affibody (SOBI002; it has a protein A scaffold fused with an albumin-binding domain); conversin (protein that expresses binding sites for C5 and leukotriene B4 (LTB4)); RA101495 (macrocyclic peptide-like inhibitor that blocks C5 activation), zimura (PEG-ylated oligonucleotide that inhibits C5 activity), etc. [16].

# Conclusions

Considering that despite long-term anticoagulation patients with APS frequently develop recurrent thrombotic episodes, this kind of therapy confers serious risks of bleeding and that pathogenic mechanisms in APS are very complex, additional therapeutic options based on immunomodulation should be included in the treatment of APS patients. The involvement of complement in the pathogenesis of APS is evident, but larger studies that could confirm potential role of Abs against certain complement components (as potential biomarkers of APS) are lacking. In addition, lack of randomized clinical trials (that would provide complete data for confirmation of beneficial effects of biopharmaceuticals in complement inhibition) in APS is alarming.
