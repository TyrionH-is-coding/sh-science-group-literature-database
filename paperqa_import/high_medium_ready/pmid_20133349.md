# PAPER

# Arterial and venous thrombosis is associated with different angiogenic cytokine patterns in patients with antiphospholipid syndrome

DM Smadja $^{1,2,3}$ , P Gaussem $^{1,2,3}$ , C Roncal $^{4}$ , A-M Fischer $^{1,2,3}$ , J Emmerich $^{1,2,5}$ and L Darnige $^{3}$ $^{1}$ Université Paris Descartes, Faculté de Pharmacie, Paris, France; $^{2}$ Inserm U765, Paris, France; $^{3}$ AP-HP, Hôpital Européen Georges Pompidou, Service d'Hématologie Biologique, Paris, France; $^{4}$ Vesalius Research Center (VRC), VIB, 3000 Leuven, Belgium; and $^{5}$ AP-HP, Hôpital Européen Georges Pompidou, Service de médecine vasculaire et HTA, Centre de référence des maladies rares vasculaires, Paris, France

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is defined as a combination of antiphospholipid antibodies, arterial and/or venous thrombosis, and, in women, recurrent fetal loss. The mechanisms underlying this prothrombotic tendency are unclear. Here we determined plasma levels of the angiogenic growth factors vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), placental growth factor (PIGF) and stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF-1) in 34 patients with APS (median age 40 years) compared with 180 healthy controls and with 80 age-matched deep-venous thrombosis patients in whom the diagnosis of APS had been excluded. All of the patients met updated APS criteria and two-thirds of them were triply positive for antiphospholipid antibodies (lupus anticoagulant, anti- $\beta2$ -glycoprotein I and anti-cardiolipin antibodies). Angiogenic cytokines were quantified at least 6 months after an acute thrombotic event. VEGF levels were similar in the patients and controls, but were significantly higher in the patients with arterial thrombosis than in the patients with venous thrombosis. Plasma levels of SDF-1 and PIGF were significantly elevated in the patients, regardless of the arterial/venous nature of the thrombosis. Together, these results suggest that APS is associated with an angiogenic process, but that the angiogenic signal differs between patients with arterial and venous thrombosis. Lupus (2010) 19, 837-843.

Key words: angiogenesis; antiphospholipid syndrome; PlGF; SDF-1; VEGF

# Introduction

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a multisystem disorder defined by a combination of arterial and/ or venous thrombosis, recurrent fetal loss in women, and persistent presence of antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL) such as lupus anticoagulant (LA), anti- $\beta 2$ -glycoprotein I (anti- $\beta 2$ -GPI) and anti-cardiolipin antibodies (aCL). APS may be isolated (primary) or related to auto-immune disorders such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). The pathogenic mechanism underlying the prothrombotic tendency in APS is unclear, although it may involve platelet activation or monocyte tissue factor (TF) expression. Endothelial cell

(EC) activation has also been implicated, via aPL binding to EC, leading to endothelial activation and/or injury, and unmasking of prothrombotic surfaces. Dignat-George et al. obtained evidence that endothelial microparticles (EMPs) might contribute to the thrombotic complications of APS, and the EMP burden has been proposed as a surrogate biomarker of vascular dysfunction in APL. In vitro binding of aPL to cultured EC upregulates surface adhesion molecule and tissue factor expression and increases IL-6 production. In addition to direct involvement of ECs, angiogenic cytokines have been implicated in the pathophysiology of venous and arterial thrombosis. Indeed, increased vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) levels have been associated with venous thrombosis and peripheral arterial disease. VEGF promotes the growth of ECs and endothelial progenitors. It also enhances angiogenesis and vascular permeability, and induces EC to enter a prothrombotic

<!-- page 2 -->
state with increased TF expression. $^{10}$ During APS, elevated VEGF levels correlate with sTF levels in plasma. $^{11}$ To the best of the authors' knowledge, angiogenic growth factors other than VEGF have not been studied in patients with APS. We thus studied levels of placental growth factor (PlGF) and stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF-1). PlGF is a member of the VEGF family, and is a candidate target molecule for pro- or anti-angiogenic drug development, since it is not required during embryonic development, reproduction or normal adult life (as VEGF is) but is overexpressed and involved in the angiogenic switch during disease. $^{12}$ SDF-1 is involved in ischemia-mediated mobilization and homing of endothelial progenitor cell (EPC) and in vascular injury-mediated mobilization and homing of vascular smooth muscle progenitor cells. It is unclear whether SDF-1 plays a role in angiogenic process during wound healing or thrombotic disorders.

Here we compared plasma levels of VEGF, PlGF and SDF-1, in 34 patients with APS, in 180 healthy controls and in 80 patients with deep venous thrombosis (DVT) in the absence of APS diagnosis.

# Patients and methods

# Study population

The median age of the 34 patients was 40 years (range: 16-65), and 26 patients were female. Triple antibody positivity (LA, IgG anti- $\beta 2$ -GPI and IgG aCL), a profile associated with a high risk of thrombosis, was present in $67.6\%$ of cases.[13-15] Nine patients had arterial thrombosis (group 1) and 25 patients had DVT (group 2). The 100 healthy male controls were non-smoking Caucasians aged 18-35 years (median 24.3 years), recruited at the Clinical Investigations Centre of Georges Pompidou European Hospital, Paris.[16] They had unremarkable personal and familial medical histories and denied taking any medication for at least 10 days before blood collection. The 80 healthy female controls were aged 18-38 years (median 27.7 years).[17] Those using oral contraceptives stopped for at least 2 months prior to the study. Their personal and familial medical histories were unremarkable. Subjects with thrombophilia were excluded. Before inclusion, all of the volunteers had a physical examination and routine laboratory tests, with normal findings. We also compared angiogenic cytokine levels to a control group, free of APS, of 40 males and 40 females with DVT included in the FARIVE study.[18,19] They had a median age of 46.5 years (range: 20-93). The FARIVE study (FActeurs de RIsques et de récidives

de la maladie thromboembolique VEineuse) is a multicentre case-control study involving consecutive subjects treated as inpatients or outpatients for a first episode of proximal DVT and/or PE. Recruitment began in January 2003 and is still ongoing. Patients are not eligible for the study if they are younger than 18 years, have already had VTE, have a diagnosis of active cancer or a history of malignancy less than 5 years previously, or have a short life expectancy because of other causes. For this study, all patients with an APS suspicion were excluded. Angiogenic cytokine levels were measured at least 6 months after the acute thrombotic event. All gave their written informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the local ethics committee.

# Blood collection

Blood was collected in Vacutainer tubes (Becton-Dickinson Diagnostics, Le Pont-de-Claix, France) containing $0.105\mathrm{M}$ sodium citrate $(1\mathrm{vol}/9\mathrm{vol})$ and was processed within 2 hours. Plasma-free platelet (PFP) was obtained by double centrifugation at $2300g$ for 10 minutes and stored immediately at $-80^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ until use.

# Antiphospholipid antibodies

# Lupus anticoagulant

All of the patients' samples were tested as recommended by Standardization SubComitees of ISTH (International Society of Thrombosis and Haemostasis), with at least two LA screening assays (activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), dilute Russell's viper venom time (dRVVT)) and a phospholipid neutralization test. The dRVVT was performed as recommended by the manufacturer (Instrumentation Laboratory, USA) and was considered positive when the LAC (Lupus AntiCoagulant) ratio screen/confirm was greater than 1.20.

# Anticardiolipin antibodies

<!-- page 3 -->
IgG aCL antibodies were measured in serum by using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) method (BMD, Marne-la-Vallee, France). Samples were considered positive when above the 99th percentile, according to the recommendations of International Society of Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) and European forum on APS.[14,20]

# Anti- $\beta 2GPI$ antibodies

IgG anti-β2GPI antibodies were measured in serum with a homemade ELISA and human purified β2-GPI (Diagnostica Stago, Asnières, France).

Samples were considered positive when above the 99th percentile according to the recommendations of ISTH and European forum on APS. $^{14,20}$

# Plasma cytokine assays

Angiogenic cytokines were measured at least 6 months after the acute thrombotic event. Plasma levels of VEGF, PlGF and SDF-1 were measured with ELISA methods as recommended by the manufacturer (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA).

# Statistical analysis

The non-parametric Mann-Whitney $U$ -test was used to identify significant differences between the patients with arterial thrombosis, patients with venous thrombosis, and controls. Stat View or SAS statistical software was used for all analyses (Cary, NC 27513, USA) and $p$ -values below 0.05 were considered to denote significant differences.

Plasmatic PlGF and SDF-1 levels are increased in arterial and venous thrombosis in patients with APS

As shown in Figure 2, PlGF levels were elevated in the APS patients with arterial $(p = 0.0002$ and $p = 0.0058$ versus male and female healthy controls) and venous thrombosis $(p = 0.0015$ and $p = 0.0105$ respectively versus male and female healthy controls). Exogenous SDF-1 has also been shown experimentally to improve the perfusion of ischaemic tissues. In this study, we have described in APS an increase of angiogenic growth factors as compared with healthy controls and DVT patients without APS diagnosis. This DVT population of 40 males and 40 females was age-matched to the APS group. Noteworthy, comparable levels of VEGF, PlGF and SDF-1 were found in DVT controls compared with healthy controls. These results suggest an absence of modification of angiogenic growth factor according to age and sex. Thus, difference of sex ratio observed in APS patients is probably not a confounding factor. One of the limitations of our study is the absence of control group with atherosclerosis or ischemic heart disease. However, all of the plasma samples used for the quantification of angiogenic cytokines had been taken between 6 months and 1 year after the acute thrombotic event in APS and also in DVT groups (FARIVE study). We did not evaluate these levels at the time of the thrombotic event because of the potential interaction between inflammatory state and angiogenic growth factors such as PlGF.[21, 37]

Several studies have suggested that aPL in APS patients might interfere with the anticoagulant activity of activated protein C (APC), thus inducing acquired APC resistance, as determined with a thrombin-generation-based test.38 This phenomenon has also been incriminated in the hypercoagulability associated with APS. Moreover, one of the hypotheses concerning the increase of angiogenic growth factors during APS could be the increased thrombin generation that might activate ECs or their progenitors (via the PAR-1 receptor) and might thereby induce SDF-1 and/or PlGF release,30 in addition to monocyte and platelet activation. In APS patients under oral anticoagulant, one might speculate that the control of thrombin generation decreases the level of circulating angiogenic growth factors. The absence of anticoagulation might thus explain the higher levels observed in acute chest pain.21

Another possible explanation is a direct effect of aPL on EC. Indeed, aPL induces EMP generation or activate oxidative stress and P38 pathway in Ecs,[39] but also the expression of the adhesion molecule VCAM-1,[6] the concentration of which correlates with the circulating microparticle burden.[40] We suspect that endothelial activation induced by

aPL may be responsible for the increase in endothelial soluble mediators such as SDF-1 and PlGF.

$\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ , one of the main targets of antibodies responsible for thrombosis in APS, was recently shown to inhibit angiogenesis by interfering with VEGF pathways.41 Thus, antibodies against $\beta_{2}\mathrm{GPI}$ might block this process and contribute to angiogenesis.

The high VEGF levels found here in APS patients with arterial thrombosis are in line with those reported by Williams et al.11 However, ours is the first study to show elevated PlGF and SDF-1 levels in APS patients with a history of arterial and venous thrombosis. PlGF and SDF-1 release might reflect vascular damage and/or repair, pointing to a new potential pathogenic mechanism of aPL. Indeed, endothelial activation resulting from hypercoagulability is likely to promote PlGF and SDF-1 release, whereas VEGF release depends mainly on monocyte activation during arterial thrombosis. Interestingly, a subtype of monocytes differentiate into EPC with angiogenic potential, and EPC numbers have been shown to correlate with levels of several angiogenic growth factors, including VEGF and SDF-1, in various clinical settings.42 We are currently investigating whether other markers of endothelial dysfunction, such as circulating $\mathrm{ECs}^{43}$ and EPCs,42 are differently modulated by arterial and venous thrombosis in patients with APS. EPCs contribute to vascular repair, can migrate towards several chemokines, and can home to sites of neovascularization.44 Levels of circulating or cultured EPCs are inversely related to cardiovascular disease,45,46 and reduced numbers of EPCs have been observed in several autoimmune rheumatic diseases, including SLE,47,48 rheumatoid arthritis,49 and scleroderma.50,51 The elevated angiogenic growth factor levels observed here call for studies of other markers of vascular repair, such as EPCs, in APS patients.

In conclusion, we observed elevated angiogenic cytokine levels in APS patients, probably owing to a pathogenic effect of aPL. Angiogenic cytokine release might contribute to the thrombotic complications associated with APS. Studies of the balance between markers of endothelial damage and repair,[52] including EMPs, circulating ECs and EPCs, might help to determine the mechanisms, and the risk of arterial and venous complications in patients with APL.
