# Antiphospholipid Syndrome: An Antibody‐Mediated Disease With Emerging Therapeutic Opportunities

## Abstract

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an autoimmune thromboinflammatory disease characterized by vascular thrombosis, pregnancy morbidity, and other manifestations driven by antiphospholipid autoantibodies. In this review, we present a clinical case that illustrates some diagnostic and therapeutic challenges in managing severe, relapsing APS manifestations, including microvascular injury and diffuse alveolar hemorrhage. We then discuss recent advances in our understanding of APS pathophysiology, emphasizing the critical role of anti–β‐2 glycoprotein I antibodies in triggering cell‐specific inflammatory and thrombotic pathways. Finally, we speculate that achieving definitive disease control will ultimately require strategies that eliminate or effectively neutralize these pathogenic antibodies.

## Introduction

A 37‐year‐old woman is referred to our clinic for guidance about the next steps in antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) management. We briefly review her relevant history. At age 19, she had lower extremity deep vein thrombosis, which was attributed to the recent initiation of an oral contraceptive. She was treated with anticoagulation for six months and has since avoided estrogen‐containing agents. At age 31, she developed inflammatory joint pain, especially in her hands and wrists, which triggered a rheumatology referral. In addition to a mild elevation in the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (32 mm/hr), she had positive testing for anticardiolipin IgG and the lupus anticoagulant; testing for anti–β‐2 glycoprotein I (anti‐β 2 GPI) antibodies was not performed. Other lupus‐relevant laboratory tests were negative. She was prescribed hydroxychloroquine, but anticoagulation was not recommended, likely because her only thrombotic event had been 12 years before and was presumed to be provoked. Her antiphospholipid antibody (aPL) testing was found to be similarly positive several months later.

The following year, at age 32, she presented with acute abdominal pain that emerged as she was recovering from an influenza‐like illness. A computed tomography scan was concerning for colonic ischemia, requiring resection of the right colon. Colon pathology showed tiny organizing fibrin thrombi in superficial submucosal venules. Acute kidney injury required temporary support with dialysis, and a kidney biopsy demonstrated evidence of an acute thrombotic microangiopathy. Laboratory testing confirmed persistent anticardiolipin IgG and lupus anticoagulant positivity. She was also positive for anti‐β 2 GPI IgG and IgA. Inpatient treatment included unfractionated heparin, high‐dose glucocorticoids, and plasmapheresis, and she was ultimately discharged with prescriptions for a prednisone taper and warfarin (goal international normalized ratio 2.0–3.0). Hydroxychloroquine was continued. Creatinine stabilized around 2.0 mg/dL after discharge.

At age 37, she developed shortness of breath and was found to have ground‐glass pulmonary infiltrates. Over a few months, she completed several courses of antibiotics, but the dyspnea and infiltrates persisted. During an acute exacerbation of difficulty breathing, she was admitted to the hospital and was found to have a 3‐g/dL drop in her hemoglobin levels. Bronchoalveolar lavage confirmed a diagnosis of diffuse alveolar hemorrhage. She was treated with glucocorticoids and plasmapheresis, and her breathing improved. She was discharged with a prednisone taper and referred to our office for further management.

## Introduction to APS

APS is a currently incurable thromboinflammatory disease characterized by an increased risk of macrovascular thrombotic events in both venous and arterial beds, microvasculature injury, placental insufficiency, cardiac valve disease, thrombocytopenia, and other morbid features. 1 , 2 Systemic anticoagulation, typically with vitamin K antagonists such as warfarin, is the only approach to treatment proven to reduce thrombosis in APS; however, despite its use, up to one in five patients will still experience a breakthrough macrovascular thrombotic event. 3 Furthermore, anticoagulants fail to protect the microvasculature, leading to organ (brain, heart, kidney, etc) deterioration over time in some patients. 4 There is an unmet need for safe and effective therapies that can combat the inflammatory, anticoagulant‐resistant manifestations of APS.

Beyond clinical manifestations, formal APS classification criteria seek the stable presence of anticardiolipin or anti‐β 2 GPI autoantibodies. 5 The latter antibodies, which recognize the abundant phospholipid‐binding plasma protein β 2 GPI, are the best studied and appear to be particularly pathogenic. Furthermore, the lupus anticoagulant test—a series of coagulation assays that screen for relevant autoantibodies based on their ability to paradoxically prolong phospholipid‐dependent clotting times in vitro—is also part of the classification criteria. The lupus anticoagulant test is turned positive by high titers of a variety of relevant antibodies, most notably anti‐β 2 GPI antibodies and the more recently described anti‐phosphatidylserine/prothrombin (anti‐PS/PT) antibodies recognizing prothrombin. 6

In this review article, we will discuss the aPL‐mediated nature of APS, with a special emphasis on recent discoveries that have deepened our understanding of anti‐β 2 GPI antibodies. Furthermore, given the diverse and likely synergistic mechanisms downstream of APS autoantibodies, we will make the case that eliminating pathogenic autoantibodies is the only strategy likely to be fully effective in treating and eventually curing patients living with APS.

## An overview of APS pathophysiology

APS‐associated autoantibodies are not just biomarkers of a diseased state but also drivers of underlying pathophysiology. These aPL engage phospholipids and, most importantly, phospholipid‐binding proteins (eg, β 2 GPI and prothrombin) at cell surfaces to activate neutrophils, platelets, endothelial cells, monocytes, and the complement system—thereby tipping circulating blood toward thrombosis at times of local or systemic stress. 1 , 2

The fact that antibodies circulate continuously but thrombotic events are sporadic has led to the two‐hit hypothesis of APS‐associated thrombosis, namely that the circulatory system of patients with APS has a lower threshold for thrombosis when it encounters an environmental trigger. Although animal models will not be covered in detail here, there is a long history of researchers transferring total or affinity‐purified IgG fractions isolated from patients with APS into mice (and occasionally other laboratory species) to trigger a prothrombotic state. 7 Similar to the human condition, antibody‐mediated thrombosis does not happen spontaneously in animals, although thrombosis is reliably potentiated in the setting of mechanical or inflammatory vascular insults. 7

## β 2 GPI in health and disease

As discussed previously, antibodies targeting the plasma protein β 2 GPI, especially its domain I, 8 are particularly pathogenic. Originally designated apolipoprotein H (APOH), β 2 GPI contains five complement control protein‐like domains. In contrast to the other domains, domain V has a unique cationic region and a separate hydrophobic loop that together convey the proclivity of β 2 GPI for associating with negatively charged phospholipids. Present in circulation at approximately 200 μg/mL, β 2 GPI has apparent homeostatic properties, including the clearance of apoptotic debris, the blunting of excessive infection‐associated inflammatory signaling, and even the restraint of angiogenesis and cancer cell migration. Of relevance to APS, β 2 GPI may also have natural, albeit modest, antithrombotic properties. For example, by competitively blocking coagulation factors from associating with cell surfaces, β 2 GPI can potentially suppress excessive coagulation and platelet activation in the context of thrombosis. 8 Although β 2 GPI is conserved across species, it does not appear to be required for healthy human or mouse development. Historically, β 2 GPI was thought to exist in two conformations: circular in the bloodstream with its autoantigenic domain I closely associated with domain V and open when surface‐bound, taking on the appearance of an elongated fishhook. 9 In this schema, domain I is thought to only be accessible when the protein is in its open conformation, triggered by surface‐associated phospholipid binding. Most cell surfaces are relatively resistant to β 2 GPI accumulation, but this changes under times of stress. 10 Interestingly, placental endothelial and trophoblast cells appear to be an exception to this rule, as β 2 GPI is found on those surfaces at baseline. 10

Although this model explains the lack of detectable β 2 GPI/anti‐β 2 GPI complexes in circulation, recent work has provided an alternative explanation. These newer reports indicate that the elongated form of β 2 GPI is the dominant one in circulation and that β 2 GPI isolated from human plasma under native conditions is resistant to circularization. 11 Furthermore, although anti‐β 2 GPI antibodies can bind β 2 GPI in solution, they do so with a relatively weak affinity. 11 , 12 Taken together, these data suggest a model in which surface accumulation of β 2 GPI supports anti‐β 2 GPI recognition not through a forceful opening of the protein's structure but rather due to changes in local β 2 GPI abundance and orientation. Put more simply, antigen–antibody complexes may only be stable when each of the two Fab regions of the antibody has a β 2 GPI domain I with which to engage.

Other recent work has challenged common beliefs regarding the binding partners of β 2 GPI. Although β 2 GPI is best known as a phospholipid‐binding protein, we now know that it can also interact with cell‐free DNA and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), likely through the same lysine‐rich region of domain V that facilitates phospholipid adherence. 13 , 14 In APS, this might facilitate anti‐β 2 GPI recruitment to the NET surface, further intensifying the inflammatory properties of NETs. Such associations may also contribute to epitope spreading and the formation of antinuclear and anti–double‐stranded DNA autoantibodies in patients with APS who did not initially show features of a second systemic autoimmune disease such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). 15 , 16 This hypothesis is an update of the classic observation that patients with primary APS routinely develop nucleosome‐targeting autoantibodies. 17

## Anti‐β 2 GPI formation

Although there is no consensus explanation for what causes the break in tolerance to domain I of β 2 GPI, several potentially overlapping mechanisms may play a role. For example, APOH polymorphisms that lead to variants such as S88N, V247L, C306G, and W316S have been described. 18 , 19 Of these, V247L has been linked to an increased risk of aPL development and associated clinical manifestations. In contrast, the C306G and W316 variants have been reported to alter serum levels of β 2 GPI and surface‐binding capabilities, thereby reducing the likelihood of aPL production.

Posttranslational modifications of β 2 GPI and/or the formation of novel protein complexes may also be factors in anti‐β 2 GPI formation. β 2 GPI oxidation is increased in patients with APS, particularly those with anti‐β 2 GPI IgG targeting domain I of the protein. 20 Oxidation of the disulfide bond C288‐C326 enhances the surface‐binding of β 2 GPI, supporting aPL recognition. 21 Carbamylated β 2 GPI has also been suggested as a target of aPL. 22 Beyond posttranslational modifications, one group has described the unusual association of intact, unprocessed β 2 GPI with major histocompatibility complex class II in endosomes, with the two proteins then presented together on the cell surface. Antibodies targeting this novel complex have been observed in patients with APS and may contribute to disease‐associated thrombosis. 23

## Proinflammatory signaling pathways downstream of anti‐β 2 GPI antibodies

Clinically available testing routinely informs the clinician about three anti‐β 2 GPI isotypes: IgG, IgM, and IgA. Of these, anti‐β 2 GPI IgG is thought to be most relevant to APS diagnosis and pathogenesis. Although anti‐β 2 GPI IgM is also considered in the current classification criteria, 5 the functional relevance of IgM‐isotype aPL remains poorly understood, and some studies have even posited that such antibodies may serve a protective role in the context of SLE. 24 Our understanding of anti‐β 2 GPI IgA, especially at a functional level, is similarly poor. As such, the following mechanisms focus almost exclusively on the IgG isotype.

β 2 GPI dimers, stabilized by anti‐β 2 GPI IgG engagement, appear to interact with multiple surface coreceptors to exert pathogenic effects (Figure 2 ). One such factor is annexin A2, a cell surface protein that normally supports plasmin generation. For example, downstream proinflammatory effects are mediated by a complex including annexin A2, β 2 GPI, and anti‐β 2 GPI, likely by triggering Toll‐like receptor (TLR)–mediated signaling. 25 Indeed, the impacts of anti‐β 2 GPI autoantibodies, in multiple cell types, depend on MyD88‐associated MAPK and NF‐κB signaling downstream of TLR2 and TLR4. 26 , 27 Other potential β 2 GPI/anti‐β 2 GPI coreceptors include low‐density lipoprotein receptor‐related protein 28 , 29 , 30 (LRP)–2, LRP‐6, and LRP‐8, the last also known as apolipoprotein E receptor 2 (ApoER2). LRP‐family proteins are structurally similar to the low‐density lipoprotein receptor and participate in various physiologic processes, including lipid metabolism regulation, cross‐membrane transport, and signal transduction. 31 These proteins are implicated in p38 MAPK activation downstream of β 2 GPI/anti‐β 2 GPI. 30 Notably, annexin A2, TLRs, and LRP‐family proteins tend to be enriched in lipid rafts, emphasizing key roles for these signaling hubs in anti‐β 2 GPI–mediated thromboinflammation. 29 , 32 , 33

## Some examples of prothrombotic cell activation by anti‐β 2 GPI

Neutrophils have been implicated in APS pathogenesis through their release of NETs. NETs are extracellular web‐like structures composed of a chromatin backbone decorated with cytoplasm‐ and granule‐derived proteins that are released from neutrophils through a process known as NETosis. APS‐associated NETosis is activated by β 2 GPI/anti‐β2GPI surface complexes via TLR4‐dependent reactive oxygen species formation. 34 Many intracellular factors released during NETosis are proinflammatory and prothrombotic. For example, DNA and tissue factor can activate the coagulation cascade, whereas NET‐derived proteases inactivate anticoagulant enzymes. 34 Alarmins such as calprotectin are also present in NETs, potentially decreasing platelet viability. 35 NETs promote the production of downstream interferons and other inflammatory cytokines, which may support autoantibody repertoire expansion against nuclear and other antigens in the extracellular space. As such, NETs may not only play a role in anti‐β 2 GPI–associated thrombosis 36 but also in the self‐perpetuating inflammation that propels APS forward.

Platelets also play a role in APS pathophysiology, and β 2 GPI/anti‐β 2 GPI complexes may be stabilized on the platelet surface by platelet‐derived CXCL4 (platelet factor 4). 37 These surface complexes activate platelets through various receptors, including TLR2, ApoER2, GPIbα, and FcγRII. 38 , 39 , 40 Downstream of receptor activation, signaling by mechanistic target of rapamycin/Akt, appears to be particularly important. 41 It is also possible for β 2 GPI/anti‐β 2 GPI to activate platelets indirectly via interactions with neutrophils, endothelial cells, monocytes, and the complement system. 42 Once activated, platelets demonstrate enhanced aggregation, thrombin generation, and microparticle release.

Because the most common clinical manifestations of vascular APS are thrombotic events localized to specific points on the arterial or venous tree, local endothelial perturbation is undoubtedly a critical orchestrator of the involvement of circulating cell types in thrombosis. β 2 GPI/anti‐β 2 GPI surface complexes, which accumulate when the endothelium is stressed, activate endothelial cells through TLR‐ and LRP‐mediated signaling. 25 , 26 At the same time, anti–inflammatory Krüppel‐like factors 2 and 4 are inhibited, further supporting endothelial cell activation. 43 Activated endothelial cells contribute to a proinflammatory, prothrombotic milieu by presenting adhesion molecules and tissue factor on their surface and by releasing von Willebrand factor and inflammatory mediators into the local circulation. 44

## Other important considerations in APS pathogenesis

Beyond the cell types previously mentioned, monocytes are also key presenters of coagulation‐activating tissue factor during thrombotic events, a process that is further amplified in APS. 45 Notably, patients with APS with different risk profiles can be distinguished based on monocyte gene expression, 46 and various types of aPL, including cofactor‐independent antibodies, 47 have been shown to activate prothrombotic monocyte signaling. Trophoblasts are another cell type susceptible to β 2 GPI/anti‐β 2 GPI IgG–mediated activation, which has implications for the placental insufficiency commonly associated with APS. 48 There is also evidence that the complement system is aberrantly activated in APS through Fc‐dependent and ‐independent pathways, further bolstering cell activation and damage beyond what would be seen with surface β 2 GPI/anti‐β 2 GPI‐mediated signaling alone. 49 Finally, aPL may influence coagulation pathways in a cell‐independent manner, including triggering activated protein C resistance through mechanisms that remain incompletely understood. 50 Finally, it should be remembered that individuals with APS sometimes have other inherited or acquired thrombotic risk factors, a topic that is covered in detail in the 2023 American College of Rheumatology/EULAR criteria. 5

Anticardiolipin immunoassays were historically optimized to detect β 2 GPI‐dependent anticardiolipin antibodies. 51 In contrast, cofactor‐independent anticardiolipin antibodies have a less clear role in APS pathogenesis, explaining why patients with syphilis (who are typically anticardiolipin antibody–positive) do not have a significantly increased risk of thrombosis. 52 One story that emerged in recent years focused on cofactor‐independent antibodies targeting the complex of lysobisphosphatidic acid and the endothelial protein C receptor on cells such as monocytes and endothelial cells. 47 Traditional APS testing paradigms are unlikely to detect such antibodies, and more work is needed by independent research groups to assess their potential role in the clinic. Beyond the aPL covered in the latest classification criteria, 5 there is evidence that antiprothrombin IgG antibodies, detected via either lupus anticoagulant testing or anti‐PS/PT immunoassays (Figure 1 ), activate platelets in the presence of prothrombin, calcium, and FcγRIIA‐dependent signaling. 53

## Future directions in APS treatment

Vitamin K antagonists such as warfarin remain the gold standard for reducing the risk (albeit incompletely) of recurrent thrombosis in patients with APS. Adjuvant treatments sometimes employed in high‐risk or refractory clinical situations include antiplatelet agents such as low‐dose aspirin or clopidogrel, hydroxychloroquine, and hydroxymethylglutaryl‐coenzyme A reductase inhibitors (statins). The administration of direct oral anticoagulants is not currently favored, given evidence that some patients with APS are more likely to have breakthrough strokes when treated with such drugs in lieu of vitamin K antagonists. Immunosuppressive agents such as mycophenolate and B cell–depleting monoclonal antibodies may be used in individuals with nonthrombotic features of APS, for example, microvascular manifestations such as diffuse alveolar hemorrhage or hematologic abnormalities such as thrombocytopenia. Which immunosuppressive agent to select and how long to treat are not well defined.

Acknowledging that some individuals do relatively well while under treatment with vitamin K antagonists, conversations with patients living with APS quickly remind us that these agents come with notable quality‐of‐life‐impacting burdens, including dietary adjustments, time‐consuming monitoring, and, most importantly, the ongoing threat of bleeding. Furthermore, the stark paradox of treating an autoimmune disorder with blood thinners is immediately apparent.

There is ongoing interest in approaches that could target aPL‐mediated inflammatory events upstream of the coagulation system. Examples include blocking or dismantling NETs, quieting smoldering endothelial cell activation, and disrupting inflammation‐amplifying complement signaling. 2 A concern, however, is that the disparate mechanisms likely to be simultaneously at play in an individual patient will make full suppression of APS challenging when only a single pathway is targeted. Given compelling evidence from in vitro and animal model systems that aPL are directly pathogenic, coupled with emerging technologies that offer hope for a B cell compartment “reset,” we can speculate that more effective suppression of aPL production can improve therapeutic approaches in APS.

Investigators have proposed a number of autoantigen‐informed strategies for treating APS, including agents that would prevent pathogenic anti‐β 2 GPI antibodies from engaging with β 2 GPI. 54 , 55 Meanwhile, more global strategies that are actively under investigation across the spectrum of autoimmune disorders include antibody‐lowering neonatal Fc receptor blockers, CD20‐targeting antibodies that enable more profound B cell depletion than older agents such as rituximab that do not meaningfully lower aPL levels, 56 bispecific T‐cell engaging antibodies, and even cellular (eg, anti‐CD19 chimeric antigen receptor T cell and chimeric antigen receptor natural killer cell) therapies. Anti‐CD38 and anti‐BCMA therapies directed against the plasmablasts and plasma cells that produce pathogenic antibodies could also have a role in antibody‐mediated disorders such as APS. How to find the optimal balance of treatment intensity and relief from the burdens of APS has yet to be determined and likely will not be known until we begin to see the results of ongoing (Table 1 ) and forthcoming clinical trials. In the meantime, it must be noted that replacing anticoagulation with immunomodulation is an aspirational goal not yet supported by current evidence.

As we hopefully enter a decade of research that will finally allow us to treat APS at its autoantibody‐mediated source, challenges and opportunities remain (Table 2 ). The APS field has not so far conceived an easy or efficient path to clinical trials likely to lead to regulatory approvals, with the relatively crude readout of new thrombotic events remaining the current gold‐standard end point. However, given the strong association between autoantibody burden and future thrombotic risk, the case might be made for effective aPL depletion as a regulator‐vetted surrogate end point. In that scenario, the extent to which we fully understand the autoantigenome of APS (ie, any pathogenic antibodies beyond anti‐β 2 GPI and antiprothrombin) will be an important question when conceiving such end points. Similar questions will also arise when considering autoantigen‐specific therapies—for example, directed against β 2 GPI‐specific B cells—that would otherwise spare the B cell compartment. Finally, the pathogenicity of other aPL isotypes beyond IgG is a pressing question with implications for some potential therapeutic approaches, for example, IgG‐depleting Fc neonatal receptors.

## Conclusions

We elected to add a course of rituximab to the 37‐year‐old woman's prednisone taper and followed that with a second round of rituximab six months later. Warfarin and hydroxychloroquine were continued. Despite no change in aPL levels, she had no further flares of pulmonary hemorrhage. Unfortunately, over the next two years, her kidney function continued to deteriorate, and she is now being considered for a living‐donor kidney transplant. In pursuit of minimizing the risk of acute complications, a future transplant could be supported by the C5a‐cleavage inhibitor eculizumab, given its favorable track record in the context of APS‐associated vascular surgeries 57 and organ transplants. 58 Overall, this case highlights opportunities for improvement in the management of APS, including more proactive risk stratification and the need for immunomodulatory approaches that can supplement and eventually replace the anticoagulant medications that are both burdensome to, and not fully efficacious for, many patients living with APS.
