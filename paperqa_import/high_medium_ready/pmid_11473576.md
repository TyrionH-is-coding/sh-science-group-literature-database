# Review

# Induction and treatment of the antiphospholipid syndrome - lessons from animal models

Y. Shoenfeld, M. Blank and Y. Sherer

Sheba Medical Center, Tel-Hashomer, Israel and Tel-Aviv University, Tel-Aviv, Israel

# Abstract

The diagnosis of antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is based on a combination of clinical and serological criteria. This review summarizes the literature regarding animal models of APS. Animal models of this syndrome help to elucidate pathogenic roles of the characteristic antiphospholipid antibodies in pregnancy morbidity, vascular thrombosis, neurologic disease and atherosclerosis. Furthermore, these models also provide measures to evaluate novel therapies for APS. These include bone-marrow transplantation, intravenous immunoglobulin, anticoagulation, interleukin-3, in addition to other therapies. Experimental induction and treatment of APS largely contributes to our understanding of the disease and to better management of its clinical characteristics.

Keywords Animal model, anticardiolipin, antiphospholipid syndrome. Eur J Clin Invest 2001; 31(8): 736-740

# Introduction

The definition of the antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) includes the presence of both clinical and laboratory criteria. In order to diagnose APS, a patient should have at least one clinical manifestation (vascular thrombosis, pregnancy morbidity) and one typical serologic finding (medium to high titers of anticardiolipin antibodies (aCL) or a positive lupus anticoagulant (LAC) test on two or more occasions at least 6 weeks apart) [1]. The manifestations of this syndrome can include [2]: venous thrombosis (deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary thromboembolism, Budd-Chiari syndrome, renal vein thrombosis, and ocular thrombosis); arterial thrombosis (stroke, transient ischemic attack, amaurosis, myocardial infarction, and limb ischemia); recurrent pregnancy loss; thrombocytopenia and haemolytic anaemia; and other features (epilepsy, migraine, livedo reticularis, chorea, myelopathy, pulmonary hypertension, skin ulcers, Addison's disease, heart valve disease, and

Animal models of APS help to elucidate the pathogenic role of aPL, as well as novel therapeutic aspects for APS [3]. The animal models focus on the most typical clinical features of APS, i.e. pregnancy morbidity and vascular thrombosis. Nonetheless, there are also animal models that deal with other manifestations and associations of APS, such as neurologic disease and atherosclerosis.

# Animal models of the antiphospholipid syndrome

# Pregnancy

Spontaneously developed APS occurs mainly in mice prone to developing autoimmune diseases. Thus, APS can be considered secondary to another autoimmune disease, mainly systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). Representative examples include the MRL/lpr and the NZW × BXSB F<sub>1</sub> mice. The former are lupus-prone mice that have some characteristics similar to human APS. These included high levels of aCL of the IgG isotype, thrombocytopenia, and poor pregnancy outcome [4]. Moreover, these mice were found to have histologic evidence for thrombosis in the

<!-- page 2 -->
brain, occlusive vasculopathy and perivascular lymphocytic infiltrate in the choroid plexus [5]. Similar findings were observed in the NZW × BXSB $\mathrm{F}_1$ mice. These also included degenerative coronary vascular disease with myocardial infarction [6]. As opposed to both of these mice strains, the NZB mice are characterized with a higher titer of antiphosphatidylcholine antibodies rather than aCL, and in addition the only APS manifestation in these mice is haemolytic anemia [7].

Models of induced APS in mice test more reliably the pathogenicity of aPL, because the syndrome is induced in normal mice rather than being secondary to a preexisting autoimmune disease. Passive induction entails the infusion of either monoclonal or polyclonal aPL to mice in order to create disease manifestations. Infusion of aCL (derived either from mice or humans) to pregnant ICR mice resulted in lower fecundity rate, increased resorption index of embryos, lower number of embryos per pregnancy, and lower weights of embryos and placentae than in the control mice [8]. Similar observations regarding aCL passive induction of APS in mice were also reported by others [9]. Of a specific interest is the pathogenic role of antiphosphatidylserine antibodies that was demonstrated by passive induction of APS both by Vogt et al. [10] and by Blank et al. [11]. They have demonstrated the ability of both monoclonal and polyclonal aPL, respectively, to cause adverse effect on pregnancy outcome. These results are of a special interest because they emphasize the pathogenicity of passively transferred autoantibodies, which makes APS a classical autoimmune disease [12]. Moreover, intravascular deposition of IgG and fibrin in uteroplacental interface [13], absorption of aCL on placentae [14], and demonstration of thrombosis in placental vessels [15], further strengthen the pathogenic role of aPL in APS. Passive induction of APS has some limitations. Firstly, an induced model cannot be completely compared with the spontaneously developed disease. Additionally, in the injected antibody preparation there might be natural antibodies against the recipients' endothelial cells that may also trigger pro-coagulant activity. Finally, the recipient mice might have natural antibodies to the injected aPL.

Another way to induce APS is via active induction of disease. This method of induction [16] forms a new aspect for the idiotypic network theory suggested by Jerne. In short, when an antibody (Ab1) is either injected or generated in response to an autoantigen, its idiotype may stimulate the immune system to produce an antibody against it (Ab2). Consequently, anti-Ab2 (Ab3) may be generated too, and it can simulate the original autoantibody in its binding characteristics. Thus, following active immunization with a human pathogenic monoclonal IgM aCL (H-3), primary APS developed in BALB/c mice: the mice had high titers of aCL with clinical manifestations typical for APS [17]. Similarly, aPL obtained from patients with active APS, as well as monoclonal antibodies generated from mice with secondary SLE, succeeded both in active induction of primary APS [18-20]. In addition, not only did passive transfer of antiphosphatidylserine succeed in inducing APS, but active immunization

with this antibody also had the same effect [21]. Furthermore, active induction of APS was also carried out successfully by immunization with $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ [22]. The reported works include both immunization of lupus-prone mice [23], and immunization of normal mice [24]. In these models, the idiotypic-anti-idiotypic reactions exceed beyond their natural role (immune homeostasis) and cause dysregulation of the idiotypic network that results in an autoimmune disease.

# Thrombosis

Pierangeli et al. [25] have reported an animal model of thrombosis in APS. In this model, a thrombus is induced in the femoral vein of CD-1 mice, and its size and kinetics can be measured. Mice that were immunized with $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ developed anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ and aCL antibodies, whereas mice that were immunized with aCL developed only aCL but not anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ antibodies. In both groups the mean thrombus area and the mean time during which the thrombus persisted were significantly increased compared with control group [25]. These authors also suggested that the activation of endothelial cells by aPL might be the first pathophysiologic event in the thrombosis associated with APS. aPL significantly increased the expression of VCAM-1 on human umbilical vein endothelial cells, white blood cell sticking, and thrombus size compared with controls [26]. Recently, aPL that were induced by immunization with the phospholipid binding site of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ (GDKV) have been shown to be also capable of endothelial cell activation and thrombosis induction [27].

# Neurologic manifestations

Immunization of BALB/c mice with monoclonal aCL resulted in development of elevated levels of aPL, anti $\beta 2$ GPI and antiendothelial cell antibodies. The mice had the clinical characteristics of APS along with neurological dysfunction (performance of reflexes less accurately than controls), hyperactive behaviour, and impaired motor coordination [28]. aPL were also found to be involved in the deterioration of cerebral vasospasm after subarachnoid haemorrhage. In an animal model of subarachnoid haemorrhage in rabbits, intravenous injection of cardiolipin resulted in elevation of aCL, aggravation of neurological deficit, and reduction of caliber of the basilar artery [29].

# Atherosclerosis

Autoimmune factors have been shown to play a role in the atherosclerosis process. Oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL) and $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ are candidate autoantigens that are suggested to play a role. Animal models provide evidence for the association of aPL and atherosclerosis. Immunization of LDL-receptor deficient mice with $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ resulted in the production of high titers of anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ antibodies

<!-- page 3 -->
and enhancement of atherosclerosis [30]. Similarly, when these mice were injected with aCL they developed high titers of aCL and had increased atherosclerosis compared with controls [31]. The pro-atherogenic activity of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ has also been demonstrated in another animal model, and it has been demonstrated that atherosclerosis acceleration was accompanied by infiltration of CD4 lymphocytes into the aortic sinus of mice [32]. Similarly, in human atherosclerotic plaques, $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ resides in the subendothelium and also colocalizes with CD4 lymphocytes [32]. Taking into consideration the association of aPL with atherosclerosis, it is not surprising that oxLDL, another antigen associated with atherosclerosis, could aggravate the manifestations of experimental APS resulting in lower platelet counts, longer activated partial thromboplastin time, and higher fetal resorption rates [33]. Nonetheless, it should be kept in mind that there are important classical risk factors for atherosclerosis (i.e. diabetes mellitus, smoking, hypertension, etc.) which have the major role in inducing atherosclerosis.

# Immunomodulation of APS in animal models

The advantage of animal models is the ability to evaluate experimental treatments that cannot be tested directly on patients.

# Anti-coagulation

Two of the most commonly used anticoagulants in APS are heparin and low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), which do not cross the placental barrier. The effectiveness of both agents in the prevention of fetal resorptions in mice with APS was compared. Treatment with LMWH resulted in a more significant effect than did heparin [34]: resorption rates were $22\cdot 4\%$ , $41\cdot 4\%$ and $51\cdot 7\%$ in the LMWH, heparin and control groups, respectively. Furthermore, the superiority of LMWH over heparin was observed only when low-dose LMWH was used, while treatment with high-dose LMWH resulted in similar results to those of heparin [34]. Aspirin inhibits irreversibly the synthesis of thromboxane $\mathrm{A}_2$ , a potent platelet aggregate. The assumption that cyclooxygenase inhibition is the mechanism responsible for the antithrombotic effect of aspirin can be further strengthened by the ability of BMS 180,291 (a highly selective and potent thromboxane $\mathrm{A}_2/$ prostaglandin endoperoxidase receptor antagonist) to reduce fetal resorption rate, to increase mean placental and embryo weights, to increase platelets counts, and to decrease APTT in mice with experimental APS [35].

# Intravenous immunoglobulins and anti-idiotypes

In an animal model of APS induced by passive transfer of monoclonal aCL, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg)

treatment resulted in significantly less fetal resorptions in comparison to untreated mice [36]. As no prolongation of APTT values was noted in this model, no effect of IVIg on this parameter could be studied. The best results were achieved when IVIg was given 2 days after induction of the disease [36]. Similar results were obtained from IVIg treatment of experimentally induced SLE and primary APS [37]: following treatment with either IVIg or $\mathrm{F(ab')_2}$ fragments, but not with Fc fragments, a complete clinical remission of both diseases was achieved, and it was associated with a decrease in the respective autoantibodies titers. These results can be explained by the fact that IVIg binds to both mouse and human aCL, and therefore it contains natural anti-idiotypic autoantibodies to antidsDNA and aCL [36-37]. These findings were strengthened by the successful treatment of experimental SLE and APS with anti-idiotypic antibodies. APS mice were treated with anti-H-3 monoclonal antibodies, and this treatment resulted in a complete remission that was mediated via elevation of Th1 cells and abrogation of Th2 cells. However, it should be noted that the mechanisms of action of IVIg are diverse and include also, for example, enhanced rate of immunoglobulin degradation [38].

# Interleukin-3

Interleukin (IL)-3 and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) can promote placental development and fetal growth. IL-3 injection resulted in prevention of fetal loss as well as normalization of platelet counts in an experimental model of passive induction of APS [39]. Similar results were found when ciprofloxacin was administered to mice with experimentally induced APS: there was a decrease in the incidence of pregnancy loss and an improvement in the clinical manifestations of disease [40]. Ciprofloxacin administration was associated with increased serum levels of IL-3 and GM-CSF. Moreover, aspirin can act as a potent stimulator of IL-3 through its ability to raise leukotrienes production, which induces production of this cytokine [41].

# Bone marrow transplantation

Both transfer of the disease and development of tolerance to disease induction have been reported in APS. When total body-irradiated naive BALB/c mice received whole population bone marrow cells from mice with experimental primary APS, the recipient mice developed characteristic experimental APS [42]. It should be noted that T-cell-depleted bone marrow cells failed to transfer the disease [42]. Conversely, when BALB/c mice, in which experimental APS was induced, were lethally irradiated and transferred with T-cell-depleted marrow cells from syngeneic naive mice, there was a depletion of antibody forming cells, decrease in titer of autoantibodies, and

improvement in clinical parameters [43]. These mice also became tolerant to APS.

# Other therapies

Bromocriptine is a dopamine agonist that suppresses prolactin secretion, and has immunoregulatory properties. This hormone was found to have a suppressive effect on experimental APS since there was a reduction in in vivo autoantibodies production, as well as suppression in the disease manifestations [44]. Bromocriptine acts as an immunosuppressant of autoimmune phenomena via induction of natural nonspecific CD8 suppressor T cells. In another mouse model, of thrombosis induced by aPL, administration of hydroxychloroquine diminished both thrombus size and total time of thrombus formation [45].

Using a phage display library, peptides that correspond with the site of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ epitope recognized by three different anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ monoclonal antibodies, were produced [46]. These peptides inhibited the endothelial activation induced by anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ . Furthermore, infusion of anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ antibodies into BALB/c mice, followed by administration of the corresponding specific peptides, prevented the peptide-treated mice from developing experimental APS [46]. Induction of experimental APS following immunization of BALB/c mice with $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ was also prevented by feeding the mice with $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ and induction of oral tolerance to $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ [47].

# Conclusion

The association of various clinical manifestations with autoantibodies to phospholipids and phospholipid-binding proteins establishes the APS. Therefore, clinical conditions that were previously presumed as idiopathic were found to have a known underlying pathogenic mechanism. Nevertheless, the association of an autoantibody with a disease does not necessarily implicate that the antibody is pathogenic, as it might be merely an epiphenomenon of the syndrome rather its cause. Animal models of APS clearly emphasize that aPL are pathogenic, and define APS as a classic autoimmune disease. As the clinical picture of APS progressively expands [48], there is also an increase in therapeutic attempts, and animal models of the disease are naturally the first step for testing them.
