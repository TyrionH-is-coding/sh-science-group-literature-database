# Review Article

# Anticoagulation Management of Patients with Antiphospholipid Syndrome Undergoing Cardiopulmonary Bypass in Cardiac Surgery: Challenges and Current Strategies

Siqi Jiao $^{1,\ddagger}$ , Shujie Yan $^{1,\ddagger}$ , Yang Zhang, PhD $^{2}$ , Yuan Teng $^{1}$ , Gang Liu $^{1}$ , Zhenzhen Li $^{1}$ , Zhou Zhou $^{2}$ , Bingyang Ji $^{1}$ Affiliation addresses are listed at the end of the article.

# ABSTRACT

The antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a complex autoimmune disease that causes a state of hypercoagulability that can result in recurrent venous and arterial thromboses. APS may lead to cardiac manifestations requiring cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). Perioperative anticoagulation management in APS patients is complex. This complexity arises from both the prothrombotic nature of APS and the interference of antiphospholipid antibodies (aPLs) with phospholipid-dependent coagulation assays like activated clotting time. Given that current literature on CPB management in APS patients is largely limited to isolated case reports and lacks a comprehensive synthesis, this review summarizes the cardiac manifestations of APS, challenges posed by CPB, and current strategies for intraoperative anticoagulation management, including heparin dosing, anticoagulation monitoring methods, and protamine reversal practices. We further highlight gaps in evidence and propose a practical three-category framework for managing aPL-positive patients undergoing CPB.

Keywords antiphospholipid syndrome, cardiopulmonary bypass, anticoagulation management

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an autoimmune systemic disorder defined by thrombotic or obstetrical events that occur in patients with persistent antiphospholipid antibodies (aPLs)—namely, lupus anticoagulant (LA), anticardiolipin antibody (aCL), and anti $\beta 2$ glycoprotein-I antibody (anti- $\beta 2$ GPI). Among these antibodies, LA deserves particular attention, as its functional interference with phospholipid-dependent coagulation assays can falsely prolong clotting times and complicate anticoagulation monitoring.

Being a multisystem disease, APS can affect the heart via direct or indirect pathological mechanisms, causing valve abnormalities or coronary artery disease (CAD), which may sometimes require cardiac surgery under cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). During CPB, systemic anticoagulation with heparin is essential to prevent circuit thrombosis and ensure perioperative safety. However, in patients with APS, the underlying prothrombotic tendency and the interference of LA with phospholipid-dependent coagulation monitoring make anticoagulation management particularly challenging, and inappropriate management may increase the risk of perioperative thrombotic events and other complications.

Despite these challenges, there are currently no consensus guidelines, and most published evidence was case reports or small

series. This review, therefore, aims to summarize the diagnosis and cardiovascular manifestations of APS, discuss intraoperative anti-coagulation strategies during CPB, propose a novel risk-stratified management protocol, and highlight existing evidence gaps and directions for future research.

# Diagnosis and Risk Stratification of Antiphospholipid Antibody-Related Conditions: Implications for Cardiac Surgery

aPLs-related conditions are best conceptualized as a disease spectrum, extending from individuals with transient or low-titer aPLs to patients with definite APS characterized by recurrent thrombosis or pregnancy morbidity. This spectrum reflects both the immunologic activity of aPLs and their clinical thrombotic expression, which directly influence perioperative coagulation management, particularly during CPB.

To date, no diagnostic criteria for APS have been established. The diagnosis is based on clinical judgement, encompassing a thorough evaluation of the patient's symptoms, medical history, laboratory test results, and exclusion of other possible conditions.[7] To

<!-- page 2 -->
standardize patient selection and improve specificity in research settings, the 2023 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) and European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) classification criteria were introduced. These criteria were developed primarily for research purposes rather than for routine clinical diagnosis. Nevertheless, when interpreted in the appropriate clinical context, they may assist clinicians as a supportive framework within the diagnostic process, although they should not be regarded as a substitute for clinical judgment. The ACR/EULAR classification system includes six clinical domains (macrovascular venous thromboembolism, macrovascular arterial thrombosis, microvascular thrombosis, obstetric, cardiac valve, and hematologic) and two laboratory domains (LA and solid-phase enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays for IgG/IgM aCL and/or IgG/IgM aβ2GPI). Classification of APS requires at least one clinical and one laboratory criterion within 3 years of each other, with overall scores of $\geq 3$ points in both clinical and laboratory domains, not the diagnosis.

It is noteworthy that not all individuals with aPLs develop thrombotic manifestations. Risk stratification is crucial for identifying aPL-positive individuals at high risk for future thrombosis. Several risk stratification scoring systems have been developed to help quantify thrombosis risk, including the antiphospholipid score, the Global Antiphospholipid Syndrome Score (GAPSS), the Adjusted Global Antiphospholipid Syndrome Score (aGAPSS), aGAPSS specific for cardiovascular disease (aGAPSS-CVD), and modified diabetes GAPSS (mdGAPSS). The 2019 EULAR recommendations on the management of APS in adults underline risk profiles. Low-risk profile assumes anti-CL or anti-2GPI at low-medium titers, also present transiently. The high-risk profile is described by the persistent high titer of APLAs or LA present twice within a 12-week gap, or double-positive APLAs profile (any anti-CL, anti-2GPI, LA combination), or triple-positive (all of the mentioned aPL types).

From the perspective of cardiac surgery and CPB management, we proposed a conceptual yet practical three-category classification framework for aPL-related patients awaiting cardiac surgery. This framework is based on pathophysiological considerations and published clinical experience. Accordingly, aPL-related patients can be broadly grouped into three categories based primarily on the presence of LA and definite APS, reflecting their potential impact on anticoagulation monitoring and thrombotic risk during CPB:

1. aCL or anti- $\beta 2$ GPI positivity at low or medium titers, usually transient or clinically silent, with minimal impact on intraoperative coagulation monitoring, and can generally be managed using standard CPB anticoagulation protocols.   
2. LA-positive individuals, whose antibodies interfere with phospholipid-dependent assays, leading to falsely prolonged phospholipid-dependent activated clotting time (ACT) monitoring, thus requiring more accurate anticoagulation assessment.   
3. Definite APS patients, who carry a significant intrinsic thrombotic risk during CPB and require both appropriate anticoagulation monitoring and individualized anticoagulation strategies to prevent thrombotic events.

This framework is intended to assist perioperative decision-making during CPB and should not be interpreted as a comprehensive risk stratification tool for APS; future prospective studies are warranted to validate its clinical utility.

# Cardiac Involvement in APS and Implications for Cardiac Surgery

The cardiac involvement in APS is multifactorial and primarily driven by thrombosis and immune-mediated injury. $^{16}$ The most common cardiac manifestations are valve abnormalities, observed in nearly one-third of patients with primary APS. $^{16,17}$ These abnormalities include valvular thickening and the presence of vegetations, also known as Libman-Sacks endocarditis. Such valvular lesions can result in valvular insufficiency, mixed lesions characterized by both insufficiency and stenosis, or, less commonly, isolated stenosis. $^{18}$ Notably, approximately 4 to $6\%$ of patients with valvular dysfunction progress to severe valvular regurgitation, ultimately requiring surgical valve intervention. $^{16}$

In addition to valvular involvement, CAD is also a common cardiac manifestation in APS, with most clinical events attributed to thrombotic events related to thromboinflammation.[18,19] Evidence implicated that approximately 2.8 to $5.5\%$ of cases with acute myocardial infarction in young individuals are secondary to APS.[20] Young patients with ST-elevation myocardial infarction and APS should be ideally managed with primary percutaneous coronary intervention and thromboaspiration if necessary.[21] Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) is indicated in some APS patients with CAD, and efforts should be directed at assuring adequate perioperative anticoagulation to avoid complications.[22]

Other APS-associated cardiac manifestations include myocardial dysfunction, pulmonary hypertension, and intracardiac thrombus. In severe cases, left ventricular dysfunction may progress to heart failure requiring left-assistance devices or even cardiac transplant. $^{3}$ For progressive chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH), surgical thromboarterectomy is recommended as a frontline therapy to prevent irreversible pulmonary vascular resistance. $^{23}$ In the context of intracardiac thrombus, early surgical intervention is equally critical to prevent recurrent thrombotic events. The 2003 expert consensus states that assessment for intracardiac thrombi should be performed in APS cases. If identified, intensive warfarin anticoagulation should be initiated, and consultation with cardiac surgeons should be sought when appropriate. $^{24}$

From a cardiac surgical perspective, recognizing patients with undiagnosed APS before CPB is critical. APS should be suspected in young or middle-aged patients presenting with unexplained valvular thickening, nonbacterial vegetations, early-onset CAD, left ventricular thrombus, or CTEPH, particularly when conventional risk factors are absent.[25] In such cases, preoperative screening for aPLs is recommended to confirm or rule out APS. Identifying these patients before surgery allows for optimized anticoagulation management, thereby reducing thrombotic complications during and after CPB.

# Intraoperative Challenges of Cardiac Surgery Under CPB in Patients with APS

Anticoagulation management of patients with APS undergoing CPB during cardiac surgery can be especially challenging.

First, CPB inherently involves a complex and paradoxical hemostatic process, characterized by the coexistence of both thrombosis and bleeding risks: the procoagulant effect induced by

<!-- page 3 -->
contact activation of the extracorporeal circuit and the bleeding tendency resulting from systemic anticoagulation, hemodilution, coagulation factor consumption, enhanced fibrinolysis, and platelet dysfunction. In patients with APS, these challenges are further exacerbated by the inherent prothrombotic tendency, which significantly complicates the management of CPB.[26] Intraoperative coagulopathy increases the risk for postoperative hemorrhagic and thrombotic complications, with prior case series reporting complication rates as high as $63\%$ and perioperative mortality rates as high as $60\%$ in APS patients undergoing cardiac surgery.[22,27-30]

Second, in cardiac surgery with CPB, the adequacy of anti-coagulation is routinely assessed using ACT, a phospholipid-dependent coagulation assay.31 However, this standard monitoring approach is particularly problematic in APS patients with functional LA positivity, as the circulating LA binds to the phospholipid surface primarily provided by platelet membranes, disrupts the formation of phospholipid-dependent coagulation complexes, and impairs thrombin generation in vitro. As a result, the ACT time is artificially prolonged, which can be dangerously misleading if clinicians reduce heparin dosing based on these distorted results.32 Importantly, similar assay interference may also occur in patients who are LA-positive but do not fulfill the diagnostic criteria for APS, underscoring the need for careful interpretation of ACT values whenever functional LA is present.

Third, surgery, particularly procedures involving CPB, can potentially trigger catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (CAPS) in APS patients, a severe condition marked by simultaneous small vessel thromboses and multi-organ failure.[33] Due to its rapid progression and widespread thrombotic effects, CAPS is associated with a high mortality rate of nearly $50\%$ , underscoring the critical need for early recognition, aggressive management, and meticulous perioperative care in APS patients undergoing cardiac surgery.[34]

# Anticoagulation Strategies for APS Patients in Cardiac Surgery

Given the significant intraoperative anticoagulation challenges described above, anticoagulation strategies for APS patients in cardiac surgery under CPB consist of two critical domains: (1) precise anticoagulation monitoring to achieve and verify adequate systemic heparinization in the presence of LA-related assay interference, and (2) comprehensive thromboprophylactic strategies aimed at mitigating intraoperative and perioperative thrombotic risk.

To summarize existing clinical approaches, we reviewed the literature on anticoagulation management in APS patients in cardiac surgery.

# Literature Search and Overview

We searched PubMed for English-language studies published from January 1, 1997, through September 6, 2025, with the (APS or LA) and (CPB) and (anticoagulation or heparin), which yielded 48 initial results. We also manually searched the references of selected articles, and articles were selected for inclusion based on agreement by the authors. We included 77 articles, consisting

of 3 guidelines, 1 clinical trial, 13 clinical studies, 23 narrative reviews, 1 systematic review, 34 case reports, and 2 basic science studies. Furthermore, we specifically reviewed case reports involving APS patients who underwent CPB and extracted detailed data regarding their anticoagulation management and clinical outcomes (Table 1). Of these 35 cases, 2 reported by Massoudy et al had not been diagnosed with APS preoperatively, whereas all others were definitively diagnosed before surgery. Among them, five patients died, including three from cardiac failure and two from thrombotic events,[29] while the remaining patients exhibited satisfactory clinical outcomes. Regarding anticoagulation monitoring, approximately half of the cases $(n = 18)$ relied on ACT as the sole monitoring method, while 10 cases utilized a heparin level monitoring system, seven reported employing anti-Xa assays, and four reported employing thromboelastography (TEG). In terms of thromboprophylactic strategies, the principal approaches involved the intraoperative prolongation of ACT targets and the restricted use of protamine for heparin reversal. The empirical prolongation of ACT targets was a widely reported tactic, being explicitly documented in a large subset of the reviewed cases $(n = 24)$ . A restrictive protamine strategy was implemented in 13 patients, who received either a half-dose or an omitted protamine dose. Among these, one patient with concomitant heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was managed with aprotinin as an alternative neutralizing agent. It is noteworthy that in two additional cases, the precision of protamine reversal was refined by calculating the dose based on a heparin/protamine titration curve.

# Precise Anticoagulation Monitoring to Achieve and Verify Adequate Systemic Heparinization in the Presence of Lupus Anticoagulant-Related Assay Interference

# ACT Monitoring

Despite the potential interference of LA with ACT measurements, several case reports have described the use of ACT for intraoperative anticoagulation monitoring in APS patients. Different ACT assays show varying degrees of susceptibility to LA-related interference. Some reports also suggest that preoperative heparin titration may help anticipate this effect and guide more appropriate intraoperative ACT thresholds during CPB.

# ACT Assay Selection

In patients with APS, the ACT is significantly influenced by functional positive LA. Celite-based assays demonstrate less interference than kaolin-based methods, as celite activates coagulation primarily through contact activation (FXII-dependent) with lower phospholipid dependence, thus being less susceptible to LA disruption. Therefore, celite ACT is the preferred method for coagulation monitoring in these patients.[26,35]

Additionally, devices incorporating exogenous phospholipid may partially mitigate this artifact as its cartridge was preloaded with a sufficient dose of phospholipid, which could neutralize the effect of LA. # Heparin Titration

Among the case reports we reviewed, four cases further proposed a more controlled approach by performing preoperative heparin-ACT titration on each patient.[38-41] In this approach, incremental heparin concentrations are added to the patient's whole blood, with the corresponding ACT values measured to generate a dose-response curve that defines the target ACT for the intended intraoperative heparin level. Most of these studies selected a target heparin concentration of $3\mathrm{U / mL}$ , which consistently yielded target ACT values exceeding the conventional threshold of 480 seconds, with reported ranges between 550 and 800 seconds.[39-41] In one case, however, a preoperative thrombotic event involving the cephalic vein and inferior vena cava prompted the use of a higher heparin concentration of $5\mathrm{U / mL}$ , resulting in a target ACT of 500 to 600 seconds.[38] The majority of patients underwent uneventful procedures.[38,40,41] A notable exception was one patient who developed severe postoperative hypotension, suspected to be due to CAPS or autoimmune myocarditis, and required support with extracorporeal membrane oxygenation and an intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) alongside therapeutic plasma exchange, ultimately achieving full recovery.[39]

# Heparin Level-Based Anticoagulation Monitoring

# Heparin-Protamine Titration

Given that the ACT is significantly influenced in patients with APS, Hogan et al conducted preoperative testing, demonstrating the reliability of protamine titration. Consequently, the heparin protamine titration assay was selected for the intraoperative heparin monitoring, with a target heparin concentration of $3.4\mathrm{U / mL}$ $(2.5\mathrm{mg / kg})$ established for CPB management. Ultimately, the patient underwent AVR successfully and made a good recovery after the surgery.[42] In other reported cases, strategies similar to this technique have been used effectively.[43-45]

# Anti-Xa Assay for Heparin Monitoring

Anti-Xa monitoring is generally considered the "gold standard" laboratory measure of heparin therapy, as it allows for monitoring the end effect. The anti-Xa assay quantifies anticoagulant activity by measuring the inhibition of a known concentration of exogenous factor Xa in heparinized plasma.[46] After inhibition of factor Xa by heparin-antithrombin complexes, a chromogenic substrate is added, and residual factor Xa is quantified spectrophotometrically to determine heparin concentration using a standard curve.[47] Hogan et al measured the kaolin and celite ACTs, heparin protamine titration test, and anti-Xa monitoring both preoperatively and during CPB, showing that the heparin protamine titration assay and chromogenic anti-Xa did not seem to be adversely affected by LA. Arachchillage et al similarly demonstrated in a cohort of 19 patients that ACT results were unreliable due to LA

interference, and recommended anti-Xa monitoring for accurate heparin management during CPB. $^{48}$

The standard turnaround time of 30 to 60 minutes limits the use of this technique for rapid intraoperative guidance,[38] In the previous case reports, it was primarily employed for confirmatory purposes,[35,40] rather than a real-time intraoperative point-of-care monitoring strategy. However, with good coordination between surgical theater staff and laboratory staff, the turnaround time can be substantially reduced. Moreover, once an appropriate anti-Xa target range is established for an individual patient and correlated with a stable ACT response, repeated frequent measurements may not be necessary.

# Other Adjunctive Methods for Anticoagulation Monitoring

TEG is useful for monitoring coagulation status during cardiac surgery, as it graphically displays the clotting process, including both the rates of fibrin polymerization and the overall clot strength.[49] In a 45-year-old APS female during mitral valvuloplasty for infective endocarditis, Tomomi et al used a heparin-ACT titration line in combination with synergistic, intraoperative TEG to monitor coagulation activity. During the initial phase of CPB, suboptimal anticoagulation was identified, prompting an adjustment in heparin dosage to prevent microthrombus formation.[50] Thus, TEG may be used to check the heparin action in APS patients.[41]

As mentioned previously, there have been several proposed strategies for monitoring anticoagulation during CPB in patients with APS; however, these strategies are not without potential limitations: heparin concentration assays fail to detect heparin resistance or antithrombin levels; TEG cannot titrate heparin for CPB; and anti-Xa assays require $>30$ minutes for results, delaying decision-making.[51] Currently, there is no consensus regarding the optimal anticoagulation monitoring strategy—whether a single method or a combination of methods—for APS patients. Furthermore, evidence-based studies are needed to establish well-defined therapeutic thresholds for each anticoagulation monitoring method.

# Comprehensive Thromboprophylactic Strategies Aimed at Mitigating Intraoperative and Perioperative Thrombotic Risk

# Empiric Doubling of ACT Targets

Prior studies recommended a higher target ACT of twice the baseline or twice the upper limit of normal through additional heparin doses to ensure CPB safety,[52] with some studies empirically doubling the ACT to more than 600 to 999 seconds.[53-56] Correspondingly, heparin dosing exceeded conventional levels in nearly all these cases, with doses ranging from 409 to $775\mathrm{U / kg}$ . However, a conventional regimen (approximately $365\mathrm{U / kg}$ ) was successfully applied in one instance, which was guided by sophisticated point-of-care monitoring for precise titration. This case highlights a critical problem: ACT is an unreliable metric. Indeed, Ducart et al reported that despite an $\mathrm{ACT} > 999$ seconds during CPB, the patient's plasma heparin level measured by the Hepcon System dropped to $1.5\mathrm{mg / kg}$ at one point.[43] Similar results have been reported in previous studies, indicating that ACT values are dependent on multiple variables and do not correlate well with

heparin concentration, especially in the APS patient population.[35,42,57,58] Consequently, while empirical management using supranormal ACT targets remains common, sole reliance on it for heparin dosing in LA-positive patients is potentially hazardous.

# Protamine Restriction Strategy

A restrictive strategy of protamine application seems to be advisable in cases of known APS, as rapid and full reversal of heparin may abruptly shift the hemostatic balance toward a procoagulant state, thereby increasing the risk of circuit thrombosis, microvascular embolization, or even CAPS. In the retrospective case series conducted by Massoudy et al,[29] comprising five cases of APS, only two patients who applied a modified protamine strategy experienced uneventful postoperative recovery: one received half-dose protamine for heparin reversal, while the other received no protamine at all. Notably, in a fatal case where APS was undiagnosed preoperatively, the extracorporeal circuit clotted, leading to microembolisation in the patient's periphery, and the whole bypass circuit had to be replaced. This highlights the vital importance of thorough preoperative diagnosis.

Notably, review of published cases reveals a consistent trend toward reduced or omitted protamine dosing in APS patients undergoing CPB. Among the 33 cases summarized (see Table 1), the majority employed either half-dose protamine or no protamine at all for postoperative heparin reversal. Notably, Greco et al determined the protamine dose precisely based on a heparin/protamine titration curve.[59] This restrictive approach was often associated with favorable outcomes and minimal thrombotic complications.

# Special situation: Anticoagulation Strategies for Patients with Concomitant APS and HIT

HIT and APS are both immunothrombotic disorders characterized by distinct antibody-mediated mechanisms—anti-PF4/heparin antibodies in HIT and aPLs in APS.[60,61] Notably, these antibodies may exhibit synergistic effects,[62] and cases of concurrent HIT and APS have been reported.[60,61]

In patients with suspected or confirmed HIT, all heparin products must be discontinued and replaced with nonheparin anticoagulants such as direct thrombin inhibitors (e.g., bivalirudin) or factor Xa inhibitors (e.g., danaparoid).[63,64] In the setting of concurrent APS and HIT during CPB, bivalirudin has been most frequently used, with ACT maintained between 500 and 700 seconds and no reported thrombotic complications.[65-67] In another reported case, danaparoid was monitored using anti-Xa assays—yet massive circuit thrombosis still developed despite therapeutic anti-Xa levels $(1.20\mathrm{IU / mL})$ .[68] Another approach in a patient with APS and HIT involved preoperative and postoperative bridging with fondaparinux, with intraoperative use of unfractionated heparin combined with epoprostenol under strict monitoring.[69]

Notably, nonheparin anticoagulants are rarely used in APS patients without HIT, as unfractionated heparin remains the preferred agent for CPB due to its short half-life, reversibility, and clinical familiarity. While bivalirudin appears to be a feasible alternative, further comparative studies are needed to establish its role in APS patients undergoing cardiac surgery.[69]

# Anticoagulation Strategies for LA-Positive, Non-APS Individuals in Cardiac Surgery

While the LA is the primary driver of both unreliable heparin monitoring and thrombotic risk in patients with APS—a population with relatively well-documented management—the optimal strategy for LA-positive individuals who were not diagnosed with APS remains contentious and necessitates specific discussion. In a prospective study of 19 LA-positive CPB patients by Arachchillage et al, where $84\%$ $(n = 16)$ did not meet APS criteria, anti-Xa-guided heparin monitoring (target: $4.0~\mathrm{U / mL}$ ) was successfully implemented without circuit thrombosis or significant bleeding. Additionally, the study revealed a poor correlation between heparin anti-Xa level and ACT, further substantiating the requirement for direct heparin concentration measurement in this patient population.[48] Pooja et al also documented a successful case where anti-Xa assay was employed to guide heparin management in an LAC-positive patient undergoing CPB.[70]

# Current Deficiencies and Prospects

Based on our comprehensive review, we have synthesized a management strategy for APS patients undergoing CPB; however, several critical aspects remain inadequately supported by high-quality evidence. Key unresolved issues include the optimal intraoperative heparinization target, the appropriate degree of protamine restriction, whether LA titers should guide more personalized anticoagulation strategies, and the exact thrombotic risk profile of LA-positive patients without definitive APS.

Although various intraoperative anticoagulation strategies have been documented in case reports, most are based on empirical clinical decisions rather than robust evidence. The absence of standardized, evidence-based guidelines underscores the necessity for a multidisciplinary approach involving surgeons, anesthesiologists, perfusionists, hematologists, and rheumatologists to optimize individualized patient management.

It is also noteworthy that some reports suggest routine heparin and protamine use may be feasible, particularly when APS is undiagnosed preoperatively. However, such observations may be influenced by publication bias, as fatal thrombotic events in unrecognized APS patients are likely underreported. # Conclusion

Managing APS during CPB is challenging. This review has summarized strategies, including empirically prolonging ACT targets, a heparin concentration-based assay, combined use of multiple monitoring techniques, and low-dose protamine infusion for heparin reversal. Based on this, we propose a risk-stratified protocol to guide management, a concept that requires validation in future registry studies.
