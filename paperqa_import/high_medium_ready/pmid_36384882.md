# SUMMARY

A patient in her late 50s with antiphospholipid syndrome presented to general ophthalmology clinic for annual hydroxychloroquine retinopathy screening. She had taken $400\mathrm{mg}$ hydroxychloroquine daily for over a decade. She denied any visual changes and visual acuity was 20/20. Her examination and fundus photos were normal, but macular optical coherence tomography of the right eye demonstrated inner retinal atrophy and visual field tests revealed a corresponding paracentral scotoma, consistent with a prior cilioretinal artery occlusion. Prior testing from visits with other ophthalmologists revealed that this occlusion had occurred previously, but she had only been informed of not having hydroxychloroquine retinopathy. The possibility of vision loss prompted her to reconsider her prior decision to discontinue anticoagulation. This case demonstrates how anchoring bias may lead clinicians astray, and how the risk of blindness is a strong motivator for patients regarding anticoagulation.

# BACKGROUND

Antiphospholipid syndrome can cause recurrent thrombosis, which may be venous, arterial or microvascular in nature. It is estimated to affect $0.5\%$ of the population, and is generally treated with anticoagulation medication, especially in higher risk cases.

This report describes a triple-positive antiphospholipid syndrome patient who, despite having had regular eye examinations, had a cilioretinal artery occlusion missed. Further, though she was resistant to anticoagulation for years even after several life-threatening thromboembolisms, the threat of blindness was the impetus for her to reconsider.

# CASE PRESENTATION

A patient in her late 50s arrived in ophthalmology clinic for annual hydroxychloroquine retinopathy screening. She had been previously diagnosed with systemic lupus erythematosus and triple-positive antiphospholipid syndrome in her 30s and had been taking $400\mathrm{mg}$ of hydroxychloroquine daily for lupus nephritis since her 40s. She had a history of multiple complications related to antiphospholipid syndrome, including two deep vein thromboses in her left upper extremity, a pulmonary embolism and recurrent miscarriages. She began anticoagulation with warfarin shortly after her antiphospholipid syndrome diagnosis but stopped after 6 months due to concerns regarding bleeding. At the time of presentation to the ophthalmology clinic, she was taking only $325\mathrm{mg}$ of aspirin daily.

She tolerated her hydroxychloroquine well. She had no known history of hydroxychloroquine retinopathy based on prior evaluations with other ophthalmologist, and had no other known ocular diagnoses. Her family ocular history was unremarkable. She did not complain of any visual changes.

# INVESTIGATIONS

The patient was 20/20 on best-corrected visual acuity and had normal intraocular pressures in both eyes. She had no pupillary defects, extraocular movements and colour vision were full, and her confrontation visual fields were normal. Slit lamp examination of the anterior segment was unremarkable. No obvious signs of hydroxychloroquine retinopathy were detected on dilated fundus examination, which classically appears as a 'bull's-eye' retinopathy, though presentation may vary. Her fundus photos were normal (figure 1).

Because hydroxychloroquine retinopathy may not necessarily result in visible abnormalities on dilated fundus examination or colour fundus photos, further investigation was pursued with optical coherence tomography and visual field testing per clinical guidelines issued by the American Academy of Ophthalmology. # DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

On initial hydroxychloroquine retinopathy screening our patient had a normal examination with 20/20 best-corrected visual acuity and no obvious findings on fundus examination. However, with abnormalities detected on macular optical coherence tomography imaging in the right eye, a paracentral scotoma detected on formal visual field testing, and a known history of long-standing hydroxychloroquine use (over 12 years), an initial diagnostic consideration was possible hydroxychloroquine retinopathy. Yet the pattern of atrophy was not consistent with hydroxychloroquine retinopathy, which typically involves the outer retina and retinal pigment epithelium. This patient's eye demonstrated inner (not outer) retinal atrophy, and the retinal pigment epithelium was completely intact (figure 3). This decreased the likelihood of hydroxychloroquine retinopathy.

The other primary entity on the differential was retinal artery occlusion, which can also result in retinal atrophy and scotoma. Retinal artery occlusion is also more commonly associated with inner retinal atrophy rather than outer retinal atrophy. Further, the distribution of this patient's retinal atrophy in the en face image (figure 2) and corresponding paracentral scotoma (figure 4) was consistent with the anatomical region of the cilioretinal artery. This combined with a known history of antiphospholipid syndrome and several documented prior thromboembolic events made a prior cilioretinal artery occlusion event most likely. Colleagues from the retina service were consulted and agreed with this assessment.

# TREATMENT

Our patient was positive for many factors that further increased her risk for thrombosis, including being positive for all three antiphospholipid syndrome antibodies, having a history of systemic lupus erythematosus, venous thromboembolism, pulmonary embolism, recurrent miscarriages and cilioretinal artery occlusion, which places her firmly in the high risk category of patients.[2] [8]

First line therapy for patients with antiphospholipid syndrome includes reduction of risk factors (hypertension, smoking, hormone replacement therapy, etc) and warfarin as the preferred medication. Several trials have compared the efficacy of direct oral anticoagulants such as rivaroxaban, and found them to be no better or worse at preventing recurrent thrombosis than warfarin, especially in cases of triple positivity. Further, routine ophthalmological examinations should be performed to track visual fields and other measures of ischaemic damage.

Our patient was recommended to restart anticoagulation as her antiphospholipid syndrome put her at risk for further embolic events in the retina and potential subsequent vision loss. She expressed great concern over losing vision and was open to restarting warfarin therapy. She agreed to discuss restarting anticoagulation with her primary care doctor, rheumatologist and haematologist, the latter of whom was managing her antiphospholipid syndrome.

# OUTCOME AND FOLLOW-UP

Our patient initiated a discussion about restarting anticoagulation at her next haematology appointment 1 month after her ophthalmology visit during which the cilioretinal artery occlusion was discovered. It was emphasised that aspirin alone was insufficient to prevent recurrent thrombosis in antiphospholipid syndrome and that anticoagulation with warfarin would be indicated. After few months of consideration, she remained hesitant about going back on anticoagulation, particularly warfarin as it would interfere with her lifestyle and preferred diet. She is currently seeking a second opinion from another physician for other anticoagulation options.

# DISCUSSION

The cilioretinal artery is a vascular anomaly that is present in up to $50\%$ of eyes.15 It is largely clinically insignificant, except in cases of central retinal artery occlusion, where retained cilioretinal artery perfusion may preserve vision as it stems from the distinct ciliary circulation.15 In all cases of perfusion loss to the retina, ischaemic whitening may be seen acutely on fundoscopic examination; however, these changes may return to normal appearance after a few days. Long-term damage in the form of retinal atrophy can be seen on cross-sectional optical coherence tomography imaging.

Cilioretinal artery occlusion as a complication of antiphospholipid syndrome has been reported in the literature,[16] and is a known but less common ocular complication of antiphospholipid syndrome.[9] Patients often present with visual symptoms, however pathology may only be detected through visual field testing.[9] This patient was asymptomatic and had a normal initial examination other than some retinal atrophy, which did not fit the picture of hydroxychloroquine retinopathy.[18] This diagnosis was likely missed as her ophthalmology visits were in the context of hydroxychloroquine toxicity screening. # Patient's perspective

I follow through on many things to maintain my health. I maintain a healthy weight and eat in a healthy manner. I live an active lifestyle and exercise/walk on a regular basis. I monitor that my blood pressure remains in normal range. I quit smoking cigarettes in my 20's. I have routine exams to monitor my visual health, and other body systems. I continue to discuss anticoagulants with my care providers beyond my daily aspirin dose. Eating healthy for me means eating a large number of greens and other foods with large amounts of vitamin K once and often twice a day. As much as I'm concerned about risks to my eye sight and other potential risks of my condition, I am equally concerned about the eating and lifestyle changes required to leverage warfarin. That, along with my personal experience with more than one person who died due to use of warfarin, I have not chosen to include it in my treatment at this time. I continue to do what I can, except for this step, and I hope another option will arise.

# Learning points

$\triangleright$ Clinicians should be aware of cognitive biases when performing an examination for a specific screening to avoid missing other diagnoses.   
Retinal arterial occlusions are possible complications of antiphospholipid syndrome and patients should be screened and monitored for ischaemic vision loss.   
The prospect of losing vision is a powerful motivator for patients, possibly stronger than life-threatening clots such as pulmonary embolism, and may convince a patient with antiphospholipid syndrome or another coagulopathy to consider anticoagulation.

Further, it is important for clinicians to be frank about the full scope of dangers related to thrombosis. Thrombotic complications such as stroke and pulmonary embolism are the most common causes of mortality among antiphospholipid syndrome patients,[22] however, these potential complications may not be enough to motivate a patient to start anticoagulation. Studies have shown that the risk of blindness is the single greatest health concern among Americans,[5] and that individuals fear losing eyesight more than losing speech, hearing, memory or even a limb.[23] Therefore, clinicians in the position of making a case for anticoagulation for their patients at high risk for thromboembolisms should remember that they are not only life-threatening but also vision-threatening. Patients should be reminded of potential visual complications, which may allow one to reconsider beginning anticoagulation.
