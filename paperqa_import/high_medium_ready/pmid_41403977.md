## Abstract

Aortic and celiac trunk thrombosis is an exceptional manifestation of antiphospholipid syndrome (APS). A 36‐year‐old woman with triple‐positive APS, prior ischemic strokes, and chronic hypertension discontinued her anticoagulation therapy with acenocoumarin 1 week before presenting sudden, severe, transfixing epigastric and mesogastric pain radiating to the back, nausea, vomiting, and hypertension (174/118 mmHg). Initial tests showed anemia (9.8 g/dL), leukocytosis, elevated LDH, and an INR of 1.44. The abdominal CT angiography revealed an aortic stenosis of 54% caused by a thrombus extending into the celiac trunk (95% stenosis) and subacute splenic infarction. Anticoagulation was resumed alongside analgesia. One week later, celiac stenosis reduced to 44%. She was discharged on enoxaparin, hydroxychloroquine, and nifedipine. After 1 month, blood pressure was controlled, and a small residual aortic thrombus persisted without celiac trunk thrombosis evidence. This case highlights the importance of early recognition of APS and its complications, including rare vascular manifestations, and illustrates the potential role of conservative treatment in managing the rare vascular manifestation of APS, where standardized treatment guidelines are lacking.

Aortic and celiac trunk thrombosis is an extremely rare and severe manifestation of antiphospholipid syndrome (APS). However, there is insufficient information or standardized treatment guidelines; early recognition and high clinical suspicion are critical. This case highlights conservative management as a feasible and effective alternative to invasive approaches in selected patients.

## Introduction

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a systemic autoimmune disorder and the most common cause of acquired thrombophilia. It is characterized by recurrent arterial or venous thrombosis and obstetric complications in the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies [ 1 ]. Its estimated prevalence is 40–50 cases per 100,000 individuals [ 2 ].

Among its clinical manifestations, venous thrombosis is the most common, occurring in an estimated 40% to > 60% of cases. Of these, deep vein thrombosis accounts for approximately 30%–40% [ 3 ].

Regarding arterial thrombosis, transient ischemic attacks and ischemic strokes are the most common presentations of APS. Although the literature reports that arterial thrombosis can affect vessels ranging from small capillaries to the aorta [ 4 ], cases involving thrombosis of the aorta and celiac trunk are exceedingly rare, and evidence regarding their optimal management remains limited.

## Case History

A 36‐year‐old woman was classified as having APS according to the ACR/EULAR 2006, based on both clinical and laboratory findings in 2021. She fulfilled the immunologic domain with triple antibody positivity (IgG anti‐beta 2 glycoprotein I antibodies 31.7 UA/mL) (positive > 20.1), IgG anticardiolipin antibodies 71.3 (positive > 20), lupus anticoagulant 1.47 (positive > 1.2), and the clinical domain was met by a history of two prior thrombotic events involving the central nervous system. She also had a diagnosis of systemic arterial hypertension for the past 8 years, classified as primary; secondary causes (including renovascular disease) were ruled out. Given that this is a young patient with chronic hypertension, assessment of renal vasculature remains a key aspect in the patient's management. In 2021, the patient underwent renal scintigraphy, which showed perfusion and glomerular filtration rates without evidence of renovascular hypertension, and renal doppler ultrasound demonstrated preserved ultrasonographic characteristics in both kidneys.

Prior to admission, she was treated with a vitamin K antagonist, although she had reported difficulty achieving therapeutic INR levels. The anticoagulant had been discontinued 1 week before presentation, as an indication of a physician outside our institution. She presented with a 1 day history of nausea and gastric content vomiting and severe epigastric and mesogastric pain (VAS 9/10), transfixing and radiating to the back. She also experienced cardiovascular symptoms including tinnitus, phosphenes, blurred vision, and occipital headaches, and she denied other clinical features such as livedo reticularis.

On physical examination, she was hypertensive (174/118 mmHg); the rest of her vital signs were within normal limits, weight: 63 kg, height: 158 cm, BMI: 25.2 kg/m2. Abdominal examination revealed tenderness to superficial palpation in the epigastrium and right hypochondrium, without signs of peritoneal irritation. Laboratory tests showed anemia (hemoglobin 9.8 g/dL, normal > 13 g/dL), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) of 329 U/L (normal < 220 U/L), leukocytosis (17.43 × 10 9 /L, normal < 10.2 × 10 9 /L), thrombocytosis (477 × 10 3 /μL, normal 150–450 × 10 3 /μL), prothrombin time (PT) 15.1 s / 10.6, activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) 66.2 s/33.3, INR 1.44, creatinine 0.53 (normal 0.57–1.11), urinalysis: density 1.015 (normal 1.01–1.025), pH 7 (normal: 5–6.5), leucocyte esterase: negative, nitrites: negative, proteins 25 mg/dL (normal: negative), glucose: negative, albumin to creatinine ratio: 7.98. Due to the clinical presentation, a contrast‐enhanced abdominal CT angiography was performed, revealing a focal thrombus attached to the anterior wall of the abdominal aorta causing 54% stenosis and extending into the proximal third of the celiac trunk with 95% luminal stenosis, with subacute splenic infarctions (Figure 1 ).

## Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

An echocardiogram was performed showing the left ventricle with concentric remodeling, LVEF 58%; there was no evidence of elevated filling pressures, valvular abnormalities, masses, or shunts. The right ventricle was not dilated and demonstrated preserved systolic function, with a low probability of pulmonary hypertension. Despite normal serum creatinine levels and the absence of proteinuria, a renal Doppler ultrasound was repeated to rule out renovascular involvement. The study showed both kidneys of normal size and morphology, with normal resistive indices and reno‐aortic ratios within normal limits bilaterally, consistent with the absence of renovascular compromise.

Analgesic management with opioids was initiated, and anticoagulation therapy was reintroduced using low‐molecular‐weight heparin (LMWH) 60 mg every 12 h during her 17‐day hospital stay. A follow‐up CT at day 7 showed a 24% reduction in the degree of aortic stenosis and a 44% reduction in celiac trunk stenosis. Although she continued to report abdominal discomfort, pain management was maintained, leading to significant improvement after 5 days.

During hospitalization, the patient developed thrombocytosis up to 1305 × 10 3 /μL, a condition associated with pseudo‐von‐Willebrand disease; this phenomenon occurs because excessive platelets bind and accelerate the clearance of Von Willebrand factor, thereby reducing its functional availability and creating a paradoxical bleeding tendency despite thrombocytosis [ 5 ]. For this reason, the administration of aspirin was deferred until normalization of the platelet count, since initiating antiplatelet therapy during this phase could have increased the risk of hemorrhage.

## Outcome and Follow‐Up

She was discharged on hydroxychloroquine 200 mg once daily, nifedipine 30 mg every 24 h, LMWH 60 mg twice a day, and acetylsalicylic acid 150 mg per day. During the 1 month follow‐up, she showed clinical improvement with blood pressure within the target range. Follow‐up CT imaging revealed a focal thrombus adherent to the anterior aortic wall with residual stenosis of 19%. The celiac trunk had no thrombotic evidence, and chronic splenic infarcts were evident (Figures 2 and 3 ).

## Discussion

This case shows a rare presentation of a young woman with triple‐positive APS and large vessel involvement in the aortic and celiac trunk. Symptom improvement and significant reduction of the thrombus occurred after conservative treatment using LMWH and analgesics. This outcome suggests conservative management may be a suitable option in patients with hemodynamic stability and without signs of acute ischemia or organ compromise.

Large arterial involvement is uncommon in APS; the involvement of aortic thrombosis has been reported in approximately 18 cases, most frequently affecting the infrarenal [ 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 ], thoracic [ 11 , 12 ] and ascending aorta [ 13 , 14 ]. The most common symptoms include claudication [ 6 , 8 , 10 , 15 ], limb coldness [ 7 ], paresis [ 14 ], or abdominal pain [ 16 , 17 ], as in this case. We consider that the initial hypertension was related to the sympathetic activation associated with severe visceral pain.

On the other hand, celiac trunk thrombosis is extremely rare, with only one prior case reported in a 56‐year‐old woman presenting with postprandial abdominal pain, diarrhea, and weight loss [ 18 ]. She was managed with unfractionated heparin and warfarin (Table 1 ). The celiac trunk thrombosis may be underdiagnosed; however, the extensive collateral circulation provided by the superior mesenteric artery through pancreaticoduodenal arcades and the dorsal pancreatic artery may help prevent any clinically significant ischemic bowel disease [ 19 ].

The literature suggests two pathways of treatment for arterial thrombosis in APS: Conservative and invasive. The first line for anticoagulation is heparin [ 11 , 20 , 21 , 22 ] or vitamin K antagonists [ 6 , 11 , 20 , 23 ] and the second, thrombolysis [ 7 ], endovascular procedures, mechanical thrombectomy [ 14 ], balloon angioplasty [ 24 ], stent placement, and revascularization surgeries [ 6 , 8 , 9 , 10 ]. However, invasive approaches may be required in cases with acute tissue ischemia or embolic complications. In this case, the conservative management was successful due to the opportunity of the treatment and the absence of severe comorbidities and complications related to thrombosis. Close monitoring is mandatory whether conservative and invasive management is implemented.

Once an arterial thrombotic event related to APS occurs, long‐term anticoagulation remains the standard of therapy; however, despite appropriate anticoagulation, there is still a risk for recurrent thrombosis. Recent clinical trials have documented recurrence rates ranging from 0% to 6.8% over a median follow‐up of 1–1.5 years [ 25 ].

In this case, the patient was discharged on aspirin since antiplatelet therapy is a recognized strategy in the management of arterial thrombosis, particularly in patients for whom anticoagulation may be challenging or associated with a high‐risk profile.

This case shows the relevance of patient education regarding adherence to anticoagulation treatment, especially in high‐risk individuals. It also adds evidence on large vessel thrombosis in APS and supports the potential role of conservative management when clinically appropriate. It highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for atypical thrombotic events in APS and contributes valuable data to help guide management decisions in this rare presentation.

## Conclusions

Aortic and celiac trunk thrombosis is a rare but potentially serious manifestation of APS, in which early recognition is essential to prevent complications. Clinical presentation varies depending on the location and extent of aortic involvement, making a high index of suspicion necessary in patients with a known diagnosis of APS, in which conservative management of aortic thrombosis may be a feasible option in selected cases, potentially avoiding the risks associated with invasive procedures.
