# Antiphospholipid Syndrome Treated with Prednisolone, Cyclophosphamide and Double-Filtration Plasmapheresis

Shigeru OTSUBO, Kosaku NITTA, Wako YUMURA, Hiroshi NIHEI and Noriko MORI*

# Abstract

A case of antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is reported. A 48-year-old man visited our hospital because of proteinuria. He had suffered from thrombosis and had high titers of antibodies to $\beta 2$ -glycoprotein I (a $\beta$ 2GPI) and anticardiolipin antibodies (aCLIgG) and thrombocytopenia. We started anticoagulation therapy using warfarin combined with prednisolone. Although platelet count was improved, the titers of anti- $\beta$ 2GPI and aCLIgG still remained high. Therefore, double-filtration plasmapheresis (DFPP) was carried out to remove the antibodies. After the treatment with DFPP, cyclophosphamide was administered. These therapies resulted in lower titers of a $\beta$ 2GPI and aCLIgG and no more thrombosis occurred. A combination therapy using warfarin, prednisolone, cyclophosphamide and DFPP might be effective for the treatment of patients with APS.

(Internal Medicine 41: 725-729, 2002)

Key words: $\beta 2$ -glycoprotein I antibody, anti-cardiolipin antibody, thrombosis, plasma exchange

# Introduction

The high correlation between the IgG isotype of anticardiolipin antibodies (aCLs) and clinical thrombosis was first documented in 1983 (1), and the frequency of fetal loss and thrombocytopenia was increased in this group of patients. These findings led in 1986 to recognition of the so-called anticardiolipin syndrome (2), later termed the antiphospholipid syndrome (APS). APS is an autoimmune disorder characterized clinically by arterial and venous thrombosis, fetal loss, cardiac and neurological manifestations, and livedo reticularis (3-5). The most consistent laboratory abnormality is the presence of anti-phospholipid antibodies (aPLs) characterized by positive serological assays for syphilis, the presence of a lupus anticoagulant

(LAC) and positive test for aCLs (6). We describe a case of APS successfully treated with warfarin, prednisolone, cyclophosphamide and double-filtration plasmapheresis (DFPP).

# Case Report

A 48-year-old man visited our hospital because of proteinuria. He had suffered from deep venous thrombosis of the lower limbs at the age of 27. His mother died because of cerebral infarction. He was hospitalized for cerebral infarction 2 months before our first visit. At that time, he had proteinuria and renal insufficiency (serum creatinine $1.7\mathrm{mg / dl}$ ) and treated with ticlopidine $(100 - 200\mathrm{mg / day})$ .

On admission, laboratory tests revealed the presence of thrombocytopenia (platelet count, $92,000 / \mathrm{mm}^3$ ). Blood coagulation test demonstrated the activated partial thromboplastin time at 115.4 seconds. LAC was positive and the serum level of antibody to $\beta 2$ -glycoprotein I (a $\beta$ 2GPI) was above $125~\mathrm{U}/\mathrm{ml}$ , and that of aCLIgG was above $120~\mathrm{U/ml}$ . A false-positive veneral disease research laboratory test for syphilis was not present. The serum levels of D-dimer and thrombin antithrombin III complex (TAT) were high, $1.6~\mu \mathrm{g/ml}$ and $10.0~\mu \mathrm{g/l}$ , respectively. In addition, the serum thrombomodulin level was $5.5~\mathrm{FU/ml}$ , which indicated the existence of endothelial injury. The summary of clinical laboratory data is shown in Table 1. As a result, a diagnosis of primary APS was given. Needle renal biopsy was carried out. Double contour of the glomerular capillaries and ischemic reaction of glomerular tufts were seen (Fig. 1). In addition, severe thickening of arterial wall (Fig. 2) in association with thrombosis and occlusion of arterioles (Fig. 3) were observed. Elastic-Van Gieson's staining showed that elastic lamella was intact (Fig. 4). By immunohistochemical staining, IgM was localized in the parts of sclerotic glomerular lesion. IgG, IgA, C1q and C3 were not detected. Electron microscopy revealed subendothelial edema of glomerular basement membrane. No electron dense deposits were found in the glomerular mesangium and basement membrane. Therefore, the existence of lupus nephritis was ruled out. We therefore attempted to perform DFPP three times. Each DFPP therapy was done using the following plasma separators: OP-05W (Asahi Medical Co., Ltd., Tokyo) and AC-1740 (Asahi Medical Co., Ltd.) as previously described (7). In each session of DFPP, $900\mathrm{ml}$ of patient's plasma was replaced with $12.5\%$ albumin solution. Three times of DFPP therapy resulted in an increased platelet count. This suggested that this case was complicated with hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) and thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP). However, there was no sign of hemolytic anemia (no fragmentation of erythrocytes, no elevation of serum AST, LDH, bilirubin) or acute renal failure. There was no episode of fever or psychical disturbance. Thus, he had neither HUS nor TTP. After DFPP therapy, LAC turned negative. Two days after DFPP therapy, serum titers of a2GPI and aCLIgG were remarkably decreased to $19.9\mathrm{U / ml}$ and $48\mathrm{U / ml}$ , respectively. About one month later, serum a2GPI titer increased to $36.5\mathrm{U / ml}$ but the serum aCLIgG titer remained low. Due to decreasing dose of corticosteroid, cyclophosphamide was added to prevent recurrence of APS. Consequently, titers of a2GPI and aCLIgG remained rather low and serum levels of D-dimer and TAT were normalized, $0.9\mu \mathrm{g / ml}$ and $0.8\mu \mathrm{g / l}$ , respectively. Serum creatinine and proteinuria did not change during the treatment. No more infarction occurred in the patient.

# Discussion

We report herein a male case of APS with high titers of aPLs. This patient had some aspects of thrombosis including deep vein thrombosis, old myocardial infarction and cerebral infarction. The exact thrombogenic mechanisms of APS remain unclear. Several hypotheses previously implicated direct endothelial damage, platelet activation, and inhibition of endogenous anticoagulation factors, such as the complex thrombomodulin, protein C, protein S, antithrombin III, prekallikrein, and prostacyclin (8, 9). It has recently been reported that high titers of IgGaPLs have been related to a greater incidence of thrombotic events (10, 11).

Since aβ2GPI acts as a cofactor for aPL binding to cardiolipin and other phospholipids, this antibody has attracted interest as a causal factor (12). Many recent studies indicate that one of predominant antibodies has been realized to be aCL in APS patients and the antibody against β2GPI seems to be more important than any of the negatively charged phospholipids (13). aPLs combine with platelet membrane phospholipids, causing increased platelet adhesion and aggregation, resulting in thrombosis. aPLs also combine with endothelial cell membrane phospholipids in combination with β2GPI, inducing endothelial cell damage, impaired prostacyclin production, increased platelet

<!-- page 5 -->
adhesion and aggregation, and resultant thrombosis.

The fundamental treatment for patients with APS is anticoagulation. In patients with the classical symptoms of APS, low dose aspirin is generally the treatment of choice. These patients are thought to be at significant risk for thrombotic events and if they have any risk factors, warfarin or low molecular weight heparin should be considered. Since our patient had some aspects of thrombosis, warfarin was administered. Caution must be taken when stopping warfarin therapy, since withdrawal from warfarin is associated with increased risk of a recurrent thrombotic event (14).

Autoimmune thrombocytopenia may develop in as many as $30\%$ of patients with aPLs (15). Stasi et al (16) identified aPLs in $43\%$ of patients with newly diagnosed immune thrombocytopenic purpura. Corticosteroids are the treatment of choice for patients with autoimmune thrombocytopenia and aPLs (17). One troublesome dilemma is the patient with autoimmune thrombocytopenia, aPLs, and a thrombotic event. One approach recommended for these patients is initial treatment with corticosteroids to achieve a stable platelet count $>50,000 / \mathrm{mm}^3$ , then warfarin can be cautiously initiated (17).

While the effectiveness of plasmapheresis has been confirmed in the treatment of thrombocytopenic purpura (18), the value of this therapy is not clearly established in APS (19, 20). A previous report (21) showed a 23-year-old woman with APS and recurrent retinal artery occlusions, in whom thrombocytopenia and microangiopathic hemolytic anemia developed after plasma exchange. Lacueva et al (22) reported a case with thrombotic microangiopathy and acute renal failure associated with APS treated by plasma exchange, aspirin and prednisone with a favorable course. Recent reports (23-25) have shown several cases of APS successfully treated with plasmapheresis. We succeeded in lowering serum titers of aβ2GPI and aCLlG by DFPP in combination with warfarin, prednisolone and cyclophosphamide. Neuwelt et al (26) reported that serum titers of aβ2GPI and aCLlG were lowered after DFPP treatment, suggesting that combination therapy with prednisolone and cyclophosphamide may be effective for lowering the titers. However, a precise prospective study has not been carried out. It will be necessary to determine the efficacy of plasmapheresis in many cases with APS.
