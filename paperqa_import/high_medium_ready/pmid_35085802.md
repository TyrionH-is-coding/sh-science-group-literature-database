# ABSTRACT

Objectives: To describe the real-world experience of eculizumab use in patients with catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (CAPS) according to the information provided by the "CAPS Registry".

Methods: We analyzed the demographic, clinical and immunological data from all the patients included in the "CAPS Registry" treated with eculizumab and described the indications for eculizumab administration, dose, outcome, use of prophylactic vaccines and adverse effects.

Results: The "CAPS Registry" currently includes 584 patients from whom 39 $(6.7\%)$ were treated with eculizumab (it was used as a rescue therapy in 30 cases while in 6 cases it was used as first line therapy). Mean age of eculizumab treated patients was 39 years $(\mathrm{SD} = 14.6)$ , $72\%$ were female, $77\%$ had a primary APS and $79\%$ had a precipitating factor before the CAPS event. Thrombocytopenia was present in 28 $(72\%)$ cases and features of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia were present in 15 $(38.5\%)$ . Twenty-nine $(74.4\%)$ patients recovered from the episode of CAPS (four showed only partial remission). Symptoms worsened in 9 patients, from whom 5 finally died despite the treatment. There was only one relapse after a median follow up of 10.7 months. The most common treatment regimen was $900~\mathrm{mg}$ weekly for four weeks and $1200~\mathrm{mg}$ fortnightly.

Conclusion: According to the real-world experience provided by the "CAPS Registry", eculizumab can be considered in some patients with CAPS refractory to previous therapies, especially if they present with features of complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy.

# 1. Introduction

The catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (CAPS) is a life-threatening variant of the antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) characterized by multiple organ involvement in a short period of time, mainly due to multiple small vessel occlusions [1]. The low incidence (less than $1\%$ of all APS patients) precludes prospective randomized studies. Given the high mortality rate, an international registry of CAPS patients ("CAPS

Registry") was created in 2000 by the European Forum on Antiphospholipid Antibodies (aPL).

Registry analysis has allowed important advances in the treatment of CAPS. Current knowledge supports the combination of high doses of glucocorticoids (GC) and anticoagulation (AC) with heparin as the first-line treatment, adding plasma exchange (PE) and/or intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIG) in those with this life-threatening situation [2]. Furthermore, the addition of intravenous cyclophosphamide (CYC) can

<!-- page 2 -->
be associated with decreased mortality in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) patients [3], and adding rituximab (RTX) might be useful in those cases refractory to first line therapy [4,5]. However, although the mortality rate of this condition has decreased during the last two decades from $50\%$ to $30\%$ , this is still an unacceptable mortality. This unmet need forced clinicians to look for other therapeutic options.

Complement pathway has been identified as a critical mechanism in the development of obstetric and thrombotic manifestations in patients with APS. [6] Several publications have pointed out an important role of complement in the development of CAPS [7]. Eculizumab is a humanized monoclonal antibody that binds to the C5 complement protein, inhibits its cleavage to C5a and C5b and prevents the generation of the terminal complement complex C5b-9 [8]. Since the description of its use as a prophylactic treatment in a CAPS patient undergoing a renal transplant [9], there has been an increasing interest in the use of eculizumab in CAPS. Thus, the aim of this study is to review the clinical cases treated with eculizumab included in the "CAPS Registry".

# 2. Patients and methods

This study is based on the data of patients included in the "CAPS Registry" treated with eculizumab. This registry contains data from patients with CAPS from April 1992 to December 2021. All patients meet the current classification criteria for probable or definite CAPS [10,11,12]. Patients were included in the "CAPS Registry" based on a) published case reports identified through periodic Medline search; and b) clinician reports to the "CAPS Registry" coordinators using a systematic data collection form. All cases are checked by the "CAPS Registry" data manager to confirm CAPS classification and to rule out duplication.

For the purpose of this study, we reviewed demographic, clinical, and immunological features of each CAPS patient reported to receive eculizumab. Also, medications used during the episode, the precise indication of eculizumab (when available) as well as dose, outcome, use of prophylactic vaccines, and adverse effects were reviewed. In addition, we contacted the authors or physicians for incomplete cases. Results from continuous variables are presented as mean $\pm$ standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]) and categorical data as percentages.

# 3.2. Treatment and outcome

Data on treatments other than eculizumab were as follows: AC were used in 30 $(76.9\%)$ patients, GC in 33 $(84.6\%)$ , PE in 29 $(74.4\%)$ , IVIG in 11 $(28.2\%)$ , RTX in 15 $(38.5\%)$ , and CYC in 2 $(5.1\%)$ . Twenty-three patients $(59\%)$ received triple $(\mathrm{AC} + \mathrm{GC} + \mathrm{PE}$ and/or IVIG) therapy.

The reasons for eculizumab infusion were reported in 36 cases: in six $(16.7\%)$ cases eculizumab was used as first line therapy while it was used as a rescue therapy in 30 $(83.4\%)$ cases due to the refractoriness to previous treatment. No information was provided in remining three cases. Of the 30 patients who received eculizumab as a rescue therapy, 25 $(75\%)$ received AC, 28 $(93.3\%)$ received GC, in 26 $(87\%)$ PE was performed, IVIG were administered to 10 $(34.5\%)$ patients, RTX in 11 $(36.7\%)$ , CYC in 2 $(6.7\%)$ cases, and immunoadsorption was used in a single case.

The eculizumab regimens were described in 34 patients and they were very heterogeneous (Table 2). The complete regimen (induction and maintenance phase) was used in 20 $(35.9\%)$ patients, while 14 $(51.5\%)$ received only induction phase (2 of them due to their death). The dose used in the induction phase was $900~\mathrm{mg}$ weekly in 24 $(61.5\%)$ patients, 4 $(10.3\%)$ patients were treated with $600~\mathrm{mg}$ , 3 $(7.7\%)$ received $1200~\mathrm{mg}$ and 4 $(22.2\%)$ received mixed doses in the induction. The lowest dose used was $600~\mathrm{mg}$ twice weekly (this patient died due to complications of CAPS).

Twenty-nine $(74.4\%)$ patients treated with eculizumab recovered from the CAPS episode: 25 $(64.1\%)$ presented complete remission and 4 $(10.3\%)$ had partial remission. Symptoms worsened in 9 cases, from whom 5 finally died despite the treatment. The median follow-up time of these patients who survived was 10.7 months $\pm 7.9$ months (range, 1-26 months). One of the patients who survived the acute episode developed a CAPS relapse (See Tables 3 and 4).

Considering safety, sepsis due to Staphylococcus aureus was reported in one patient who was treated successfully. Granulocytopenia was reported in two patients - in one of these patients, granulocytopenia was attributed to dalteparin, and it improved after decreasing the dose of this drug. The administration of prophylactic vaccine against encapsulated bacteria was reported in only 5 patients, all of them against $N$ . meningitidis but only one against $S$ . pneumonia and one of them received also antibiotic prophylaxis.

# 4. Discussion

The high mortality rate found in CAPS has stimulated the continuous investigation of new therapies. In 1992, Davis and Brey [30] showed a relationship between aPL and complement activation, and suggested that complement activation may play a pathogenic role in APS patients. Eculizumab binds to the complement protein C5, inhibits its cleavage to C5a and C5b and prevents the generation of the terminal complement complex C5b-9, interrupting complement mediated proinflammatory events and, subsequently, inhibiting the generation of the prothrombotic environment. It binds to C5 on smooth muscle (intrinsic/vascular) and skeletal muscle, myoepithelium, myofibroblasts, reticulum cells and renal tubular epithelium and eculizumab deposits have been demonstrated in vessel walls and mesangial for up to 5 months after the last eculizumab infusion [30,31]. According to the "CAPS Registry", different regimens have been used in CAPS patients, and the optimal dose is unknown. Some patients' may need only an induction dose. This heterogeneity of regimens affects the outcome, although there was a greater tendency to prescribe $900\mathrm{mg}$ weekly for four weeks and $1200\mathrm{mg}$ fortnightly. Unfortunately, it is not clear how long the treatment should continue.

Due to the inhibition of the distal complement components, an increased incidence of infections by encapsulated bacteria is expected. Literature describes an increased susceptibility to Neisseria in patients with terminal complement deficiencies, either acquired or secondary complement deficiencies [32]. In this study, there were no cases of infections by encapsulated germs even when only five patients were reported to be vaccinated. However, due to the mechanism of action of eculizumab, the risk of infections is high, and guidelines strongly recommend prophylactic vaccination. Pneumococcal or Haemophilus influenzae infections are not described as increased in the literature as opposed to patients with early complement components (C1-C4). Nevertheless, although with a less empirical basis, general infections are considered as a relevant potential risk. In this study, a S. Aureus infection resolved with standard antibiotics. In addition, 2 patients reported granulocytopenia (one case due to dalteparin).

The main limitation of this study is that data was obtained from published reports in the literature and cases reported to the registry coordinators; therefore, results can be affected by publication bias and

some reports might be incomplete or inaccurate. In this sense, it was not possible to evaluate the time elapsed since the beginning of CAPS episode until the first dose of eculizumab, nor to evaluate neither the isolated antithrombotic effect of eculizumab nor the immunological (complement proteins or aPL) effects caused by the drug. However, the compilation of CAPS cases in the "CAPS Registry" made possible to increase our current knowledge on the eculizumab use in this condition in real-world, although the low number of cases treated worldwide with this monoclonal antibody limits the experience.

# 5. Conclusion

In this study we described the real-world experience of 39 CAPS patients treated with eculizumab, a humanized monoclonal antibody that binds to the C5 complement protein. Despite the limitations of this analysis, we suggest considering eculizumab if patients present with atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome features, i.e., complement-mediated TMA. Dose regimens and duration of treatment in this indication is still a matter of debate. However, we think that the established regimen for atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome that states a $900\mathrm{mg}$ weekly dose for the first 4 weeks, followed by $1200\mathrm{mg}$ for the fifth dose one week later, and then $1200\mathrm{mg}$ every two weeks thereafter might be the best regimen. Due to a complement inhibition, a strong consideration on infection prophylaxis against meningitis infections should be provided. Discontinuation of eculizumab should be attempted after clinical improvement and disappearance of laboratory features of TMA.
