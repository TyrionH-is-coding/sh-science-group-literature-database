# Abstract

Objectives: APS-associated heart valve disease (HVD) is well described. Nonetheless, limited data exist on clinical parameters associated with the course of primary APS (pAPS) patients with HVD. The goal of this study was to assess clinical features and related outcomes in patients with APS-associated HVD.

Methods: In this multicentre retrospective study, we identified 33 pAPS patients with HVD (pAPS-HVD group) and compared their clinical course with 128 pAPS patients with normal heart valves on echocardiography (pAPS-control group).

Results: pAPS-HVD patients had more cerebrovascular events (56.3% vs 25%, $P = 0.005$ ) and livedo reticularis (24.2% vs 7.8%, $P = 0.013$ ) than pAPS-controls. Furthermore, catastrophic-APS (CAPS) (12.1% vs 2.4%, $P = 0.034$ ), recurrent thrombosis (33.3% vs 4.7%, $P < 0.001$ ) and need for advanced therapy (i.e. IVIG, plasmapheresis or rituximab) were more frequent in pAPS-HVD patients. Anti- $\beta_{2}$ -glycoprotein 1 IgG (84.8% vs 63.2%, $P = 0.034$ ), anti-cardiolipin IgG (90.9% vs 64.8%, $P = 0.005$ ) and triple positive aPL (75.8% vs 56.5%, $P = 0.047$ ) were commoner in pAPS-HVD patients vs pAPS-controls. Ten of the 33 patients with pAPS-HVD underwent valve surgery, which was associated with male gender, smoking, arterial limb ischaemia and livedo reticularis.

Conclusion: pAPS-HVD patients had a more severe APS clinical course including CAPS and thrombotic events as well as a specific serology, namely IgG isotype aPL antibodies and triple positivity. Our data suggest that pAPS-HVD represents a high-risk subgroup of APS patients.

Keywords: APS, aPL, SLE, heart valve, Libman-Sacks endocarditis.

# Rheumatology key messages

- Heart valve disease in primary APS is associated with a more severe clinical course.   
- Heart valve disease among APS patients is associated with livedo reticularis, triple positive anti-phospholipids antibodies and thrombotic recurrence.

# Introduction

APS is an autoimmune disease characterized by thrombotic and/or pregnancy morbidity both of which are closely related to the persistent presence of aPL [1]. APS either as a primary condition or secondary to other autoimmune conditions (e.g. SLE) affects a variety of organs including the heart and particularly the heart valves. The latter, referred to as Libman-Sacks endocarditis (was described in 1924 by Emmanuel Libman and Benjamin Sacks [2], primarily in relation to SLE [3], and later also to primary APS (pAPS) [4, 5] and catastrophic APS (CAPS).

In the 21st century, given the diminishing incidence of rheumatic heart disease, the definition of heart valve disease (HVD) has changed and now encompasses all pathologies related to the valve [6, 7]. Particularly, in relation to autoimmune diseases, heart valve disease is mainly referred to vegetation and/or thickening $>3\mathrm{mm}$ and/or the presence of irregular nodules on the valve [8]. HVD has been reported in $10 - 50\%$ of SLE patients [9-14], and in approximately one-third of pAPS patients according to the Galveston task force [15]. aPLs are considered pathogenic and causative of many APS-related manifestations [16]. In this context, aPLs were

<!-- page 2 -->
found to bind directly to the affected valves [5]. Moreover, aPL titres correlate with the presence of valve disease in SLE patients [9, 10, 17], while IgG aCL antibody titres were linked with valve dysfunction as vegetation or insufficiency [18].

Currently, the available data on clinical course, outcomes and mortality related to HVD among pAPS patients is limited. In this study we describe a cohort of pAPS patients with HVD and compare them with pAPS patients without HVD.

# Methods

Patients with pAPS according to the APS classification criteria [1] with no evidence of other autoimmune diseases were included in this retrospective study. pAPS patients were followed in three medical centres in Israel from 2008 to 2019. The presence of cardiac valve disease was confirmed by transthoracic echography or transoesophageal echography in 33 patients (pAPS-HVD group) while no cardiac involvement on cardiac imaging was confirmed in 128 consecutive patients that represent the APS control group (pAPS-controls). APS patients that did not undergo echocardiography or were diagnosed with alternative cause of heart valve disease (i.e. disease including rheumatic heart disease, valvular calcification, infectious heart disease or congenital valve) were excluded from this study.

Clinical features analysed included gender, age at APS presentation, the presence of hypertension, dyslipidaemia, diabetes mellitus and smoking, APS clinical criteria, non-criteria manifestations, and the occurrence of CAPS defined according to the 2003 international consensus statement [19]. All patients were treated according to the treating physician's discretion. Data regarding medical and surgical interventions and recurrence of thrombotic events during follow-up were collected.

Echocardiographic evaluation was performed at the discretion of the treating clinician. The echocardiographic evaluation included valve structure and function (aortic, mitral, tricuspid and pulmonary valves were assessed separately), ejection fraction, the right ventricular function defined by the operator as being normal, mildly, moderately or severely abnormal, and pulmonary hypertension (approximated according to the flow velocity across the tricuspid valve and defined as mild: $25 - 40\mathrm{mmHg}$ ; moderate: $41 - 55\mathrm{mmHg}$ ; and severe: $>55\mathrm{mmHg}$ ) [20]. Patients developing HVD during the follow-up period (while having normal echocardiography upon APS diagnosis) were classified as pAPS-HVD. Symptoms associated with heart failure were assessed according to the New York Heart Association [21].

The global APS score (GAPSS) was developed to assess the risk of thrombosis in APS [22]. In this study, we used the recently validated adjusted GAPSS (aGAPSS) for this purpose [23, 24]. Scores were calculated for each patient as follows: 3 points for dyslipidaemia, 1 points for arterial hypertension, 5 points for the presence of aCL antibodies IgG/IgM, 4 points for anti- $\beta_{2}$ -glycoprotein IgG/IgM, and 4 points for lupus anticoagulant.

Serological markers that were collected included complement levels (the lowest value recorded during the study period), lupus anticoagulant ratio (the highest level recorded during the study periods) defined as a positive dilute Russel's viper venom time or the silica clotting time, and aPL titres that were defined as normal (titre $\leq$ upper limit as per the manufacturer's recommendation), low positive (up to twice the designated upper limit as per the manufacturer's recommendation), moderate (titre 2-4 times the upper normal limit as per the manufacturer's recommendation) and high (titre $>4$ times the upper normal limit as per the manufacturer's

recommendation). All tests were positive on two occasions at least 12 weeks apart according to the guidelines of the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis [25].

This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and with institutional review board approval; all three centres (Helsinki committee of Sheba Medical Center, Meir Medical Center and Shamir Medical Center) provided separated approval specifically for this study. Written consent was not obtained since the study was based upon clinical records, and thus data acquired remained anonymized.

Statistical analysis was performed using BMDP software (BMDP Statistical Software, University of California Press, Los Angeles, CA, USA). Pearson's $\chi^2$ test or Fisher's exact test (two-tailed) was used to determining between-group differences for discrete variables, and analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparing continuous variables. Variables found to be significant $(P < 0.10)$ on univariate analysis were analysed by multivariate stepwise logistic regression. A $P$ -value of $\leq 0.05$ was considered significant.

# Results

In our cohort of 312 pAPS patients followed for up to 11 years, 161 $(52\%)$ underwent at least one echocardiographic evaluation. Among these patients, we identified 33/161 $(20.5\%)$ patients with HVD (pAPS HVD group). The remaining 128 patients with no evidence of HVD on echocardiography were designated as pAPS-controls.

# Clinical and laboratory manifestations of pAPS-HVD vs pAPS-control groups

There were some differences between pAPS-HVD and pAPS controls (Table 1). The former had a longer follow-up period (14.3 vs 9.8 years, $P = 0.003$ ) and showed a trend towards older age. However there was no difference in cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension, smoking, diabetes and dyslipidaemia) between the groups. pAPS-HVD was associated with a higher incidence of stroke (56.3% vs 25%) and livedo reticularis (24.2% vs 7.8%) vs pAPS-controls. The presence of hypertension in pAPS-HVD patients was associated with the occurrence of stroke (9 of 18 patients, 50%); in comparison in the control group only 2 of 15 (13.3%) who were diagnosed with hypertension were diagnosed with stroke during follow-up ( $P = 0.034$ ). Finally, anti- $\beta_{2}$ -glycoprotein1 ( $\alpha \beta_{2}\mathrm{GP1}$ ) and anti-cardiolipin (aCL) antibodies of the IgG isotype, lupus anticoagulant and triple aPL positivity were all more common in the pAPS-HVD patients (Table 1).

In our cohort of 33 pAPS-HVD patients, the mitral valve was the most frequently involved in disease 20/33 (61%) patients while two (6%) had isolated aortic valve disease, one (3%) had tricuspid valve disease, nine patients (27%) had combined mitral and aortic valve and seven patients (21%) had mitral and tricuspid disease three (9%). HVD diagnosed by echocardiography developed during the follow-up period in 28 patients. Improvement of valve function was observed in 13/28 (46%) patients, deterioration in 7/28 (25%) and stable valve dysfunction in 8/28 (29%) patients.

# Cardiac outcomes in pAPS patients

Approximately one-fourth (24.2%, 8/33) of pAPS-HVD patients were NYHA class $>1$ compared with none among controls $(P < 0.001$ ; Table 3). Similarly, both pulmonary hypertension (29% vs 17.8%) and right heart dysfunction (32.3% vs 6.3%) were significantly more prevalent in echocardiographic studies of pAPS-HVD vs pAPS-controls $(P < 0.001$ ; Table 3). Ten pAPS-HVD patients (30.3%) underwent valve replacement or repair, and of these patients two required repeat valve replacement. Valve surgery (Table 4) was more common in men than in women (40% vs 8.7%, $P = 0.032$ ), smokers vs non-smokers (30% vs 4.3%, $P = 0.039$ ) and patients with vs without livedo reticularis (50% vs 13.8%, $P = 0.022$ ). Significantly, all patients with valve disease who had concomitant arterial limb ischaemia required heart valve replacement.

In several smaller cohorts, a prevalence of HVD of $20 - 50\%$ taking into account any valve thickening has been reported, while Libman-Sacks endocarditis was identified in $\sim 10\%$ of patients [18, 28-31]. Thus, while it is difficult to define the precise prevalence of pAPS-HVD, $\sim 10 - 20\%$ of pAPS patients may develop significant HVD. In this report, we have chosen an inclusive definition of HVD, i.e. all forms of heart valve disease that were deemed clinically relevant by previous reports [15, 32].

Our cohort of pAPS-HVD patients had a more severe APS clinical course with more adverse outcomes compared with APS patients without HVD. The most common thrombotic presentation in our cohort was stroke while in other cohorts comprising both primary and secondary APS deep vein thrombosis was the commonest clinical presentation [30]. This difference may reflect the selection of only APS patients that underwent echocardiography in our cohort. Our results also indicate that hypertension in pAPS-HVD patients increases the risk of stroke, highlighting the importance of identifying hypertension in these patients. Livedo reticularis, which when occurring with cerebrovascular events is known as Sneddon syndrome [33], was also more prevalent in our pAPS-HVD subgroup. A similar association between Sneddon syndrome and mitral valve regurgitation has been reported in other cohorts [34]. In line with this finding, CAPS and overall mortality were more common among pAPS-HVD compared with the pAPS-control patients. This is reflected by the use of advanced immunosuppressive therapy among pAPS-HVD patients in our cohort and is consistent with evidence that complications related to valve disease may not be prevented by simply using antiplatelet or anticoagulant treatments [18, 29, 35]. Expert opinion suggests that rituximab should be used in refractory disease [36, 37], which is consistent with our observation that in our cohort significantly more patients with HVD received this treatment compared with pAPS patients without HVD.

Patterns of APS serology differed between patient groups in our study with elevations of both aCL IgG and $\mathsf{a}\beta_{2}\mathsf{GP1}$ IgG as well as the triple positive pattern being more frequent in the APS-HVD group. We postulate that the association of heart valve disease with other non-criteria manifestations and resistance to anticoagulation therapy supports the notion that specific aPL antibodies may be pathogenic in heart valve disease and that antibody deplete therapy may be necessary to abrogate this element of pAPS.

The aGAPSS has recently been validated for estimating risk for thrombotic recurrence [40, 41] and thrombosis in obstetric APS [42, 43] and has also been evaluated in patients non-criteria disease manifestations albeit with only a small representation of pAPS-HVD patients of only $3\%$ [44]. In our cohort the mean aGAPSS was 12.73 (95% CI 9.6, 15.4) in the HVD group compared with 11.02 (95% CI 7.4, 14.6) in the control group $(P = 0.082)$ . It remains to be seen whether aGAPSS may predict thrombosis in association with HVD in prospective studies.

As expected HVD was associated with adverse cardiac outcomes in our cohort. pAPS-HVD patients were in higher NYHA classes, had higher pulmonary hypertension and had reduced right ventricular systolic function. Interestingly, the ejection fraction did not differ between HVD and non-HVD patients, which is consistent with previous reports [29, 30]. Several studies that focused on heart failure in APS demonstrated an increased prevalence of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction in APS patients compared with controls [45, 46]; our data correlate with this findings. Further prospective studies are needed to determine the effect of HVD on cardiac outcomes. In the largest published cohort of APS patients undergoing cardiac valve surgery, adverse outcomes were common, with $50\%$ of patients experiencing a serious complication around or after surgery [47]. An additional recent analysis of 23 APS patients showed similar outcomes [48]. While reporting on the outcome of HVD surgery is outside of the scope of this study, given its prognostic importance we did focus on specific clinical presentations that may be related to the need for surgical intervention in pAPS-HVD. Our data suggest that male gender, smoking, livedo reticularis and arterial ischaemia were associated with the need for valve surgery.

Although our study is retrospective, limited in size and subject to bias by indication, it seems prudent to regard HVD as a risk for adverse outcomes and target pAPS-HVD patients for vigilant observation and cardiovascular risk factor management. In our entire cohort of 312 pAPS patients, only $52\%$ underwent echocardiography. Given the relatively high percentage of HVD among patients with pAPS and the possible severe consequences of APS-associated HVD, it seems reasonable to recommend performing serial cardiac echocardiography in all pAPS patients. Broader use of this simple modality is reflected in the APS consensus committee statements [32]. The 2003 criteria include moderate to severe valve dysfunction or the presence of vegetation for cardiac involvement in pAPS; however, these were not considered APS diagnostic criteria [49]. In an updated statement, heart valve involvement has been included as a diagnostic criterion for pAPS [8]. Our data also suggest that the strong association between HVD and a more aggressive course of pAPS, and particularly the occurrence of thrombotic events warrants the study of more active preventive therapy (antithrombotic, anticoagulative or immunomodulatory). We therefore would assume that yearly echocardiography would be sensible, while in patients at high risk (i.e. who have had stroke, livedo reticularis or suffer from hypertension) a more vigorous follow up might be considered.

There are several limitations to our study that are inherent to its retrospective nature. First, patients in the pAPS-HVD group were older and had longer follow up. However similar rates of cardiovascular risk factors and absence of other causes of heart valve disease in patients in both groups limit this bias. Second, patient follow-up started from APS diagnosis and not from diagnosis of HVD disease; however, we believe that the resultant risk of bias is limited because of the long duration of follow-up in this study. Third, we were unable to assess the effects of treatment and interventions in our study because these were not assessed. Finally, echocardiography was performed in a non-blinded manner, which may have led to over-interpretation of the findings; however, this does not appear to have been a major problem because the incidence of HVD in our cohort was similar to or lower than that reported in other studies.

# Conclusions

In our study, $\sim 20\%$ of pAPS patients suffered from clinically relevant HVD. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to suggest an association between pAPS-HVD and a more severe APS disease course, a higher incidence of thrombotic events, CAPS, overall mortality, high-risk aPL profile and a higher cardiac morbidity. We identified four parameters associated with the need for heart valve surgery, namely male gender, smoking, arterial limb ischaemia and livedo reticularis. Our data suggest that it is reasonable to suggest routine heart valve assessment by cardiac echocardiography in all APS patients and to provide aggressive intervention to mitigate co-existent cardiovascular risk factors.
