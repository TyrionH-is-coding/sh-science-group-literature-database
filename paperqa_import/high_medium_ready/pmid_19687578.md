# Study of Phosphatidylserine-dependent Anti-prothrombin Antibody in Cerebral Infarction

Hirohisa Okuma<sup>1</sup>, Yasuhisa Kitagawa<sup>2</sup>, Tatsuya Ishikawa<sup>3</sup> and Shigeharu Takagi<sup>3</sup>

# Abstract

Purpose This study was carried out to clarify the relationship of IgG phosphatidylserine-dependent anti-prothrombin antibody (aPS/PT), IgG beta 2 glycoprotein I-dependent anticardiolipin antibody (beta 2-GPI aCL), and lupus anticoagulant (LA) to cerebral infarction, using data from 93 patients who visited our hospitals.

Materials and Methods We computed the positive rates for each of IgG aPS/PT, beta 2-GPI aCL, and LA in the 93 patients with cerebral infarction, and carried out logistic regression analysis with IgG aPS/PT as the outcome variable and with beta 2-GPI aCL, LA, and each risk factor as predictor variables in order to assess the relationship of IgG aPS/PT with each factor.

Results IgG aPS/PT was more highly correlated with LA than beta 2-GPI aCL in IgG aPS/PT-positive patients with cerebral infarction. IgG aPS/PT itself appears to have high specificity as a marker for antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), because there were patients who were IgG aPS/PT-positive but both beta 2-GPI aCL- and LA-negative.

Conclusion In IgG aPS/PT-positive patients with cerebral infarction, IgG aPS/PT is more highly correlated with LA than beta 2-GPI aCL. It is also strongly associated with APS. Measurement of IgG aPS/PT in patients with cerebral infarction could be of diagnostic value.

Key words: phosphatidylserine-dependent anti-prothrombin antibody, cerebral infarction

(Inter Med 48:1351-1355,2009)

DOI: 10.2169/internalmedicine.48.2232)

# Introduction

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), an autoimmune disease defined by the presence of antiphospholipid autoantibodies, is clinically characterized by a predisposition to thrombosis and pregnancy complications, and is the leading cause of thrombosis in patients with no acquired risk factors. When APS is clinically suspected, the current practice is to test for the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies, namely, IgG beta 2 glycoprotein I-dependent anticardiolipin antibody (beta 2-GPI aCL), or lupus anticoagulant (LA).

Recently, autoantibodies for prothrombin as well as beta 2-GPI aCL have been shown to be associated with LA (1, 2), and IgG phosphatidylserine-dependent antiprothrombin antibodies (aPS/PT) were also reported to be strongly asso

ciated with LA (3). However, the relationship between IgG aPS/PT and cerebral infarction is poorly understood. This study was carried out to clarify the relationship between IgG aPS/PT and cerebral infarction in 93 cerebral infarction patients who visited our hospitals. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our university, and all patients gave written informed consent.

# Materials and Methods

The subjects were 93 cerebral infarction patients (53 men and 40 women, mean age $69 \pm 12$ ), who were hospitalized between November 2006 and October 2007. Among them, 59 had lacunar infarction (mean age, $68 \pm 13$ ), 25 had atherothrombotic infarction (mean age, $71 \pm 8$ ), and 9 had cardiogenic cerebral embolism (mean age, $73 \pm 14$ ).

<!-- page 2 -->
We measured IgG aPS/PT, beta 2-GPI aCL, and LA in the control group of 90 healthy adults, consisting of 45 men and 45 women with an average age of $67 \pm 12$ . Informed written consent to undergo measurements of each antibody was obtained from each of these healthy adults, to whom the purposes and procedure of the study had been fully explained. The measurement values were $5.5 \pm 1.1$ for IgG aPS/PT, $1.2 \pm 0.6$ (based on the nonparametric approach) for beta 2-GPI aCL, and $0.82 \pm 0.11$ for the LA ratio. These data were compared with those of the cerebral infarction group.

# Assay of Antiphospholipid Antibodies

Assessment of the presence of various antibodies was carried out in patients in the chronic stage, at least one month after the onset of cerebral infarction. IgG aPS/PT was measured by ELISA using phosphatidylserine-prothrombin complex as an antigen immobilized on ELISA plates in the presence of $\mathrm{CaCl}_2$ as described. Briefly, nonirradiated microtiter plates (Sumilon type, Sumitomo Bakelite, Tokyo, Japan) were coated with $30~\mu \mathrm{L}$ of $50~\mu \mathrm{g / mL}$ of phosphatidylserine and dried overnight at $4^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ . To avoid nonspecific binding of protein, wells were blocked with Tris-buffered saline containing $1\%$ fatty acid-free bovine serum albumin (BSA) (A6003; Sigma, St Louis, MO) and $5\mathrm{mM}$ $\mathrm{CaCl}_2$ (BSA-Ca). After three washes, $50~\mu \mathrm{L}$ of $10~\mu \mathrm{g / mL}$ of human prothrombin (Diagnostic Stago, Asnieres, France) in BSA-Ca was added to the wells. After 1 hour of incubation at $37^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ , plates were washed and $50~\mu \mathrm{L}$ of patient's serum diluted 1: 100 in BSA-Ca was added in duplicate. Plates were incubated for 1 hour at room temperature followed by the addition of alkaline phosphatase-conjugated goat anti-human IgG substrate, and a value equal or more to $12\mathrm{U / mL}$ was defined as positive. Beta 2-GPI aCL was measured by using ELISA (Yamasa kit), and a value of not less than $3.5\mathrm{U / L}$ was defined as positive. LA was detected by means of clotting assay. Three clotting tests were performed for LA determination according to guidelines recommended by the SSC of ISTH (4) for the APTT test, and a sensitive reagent with low phospholipid concentration was used for screening (Diagnostica Stago). A mixing test (plasma sample/normal pooled plasma 1:1, 1:4 and 1:9) and phospholipids addition test were used to confirm the presence of LA. The kaolin clotting time (KCT) test was also used and confirmation via a mixing study was done similarly. The dilute Russell viper venom time (dRVVT) test was used to screen and confirm the presence of LA, using RVV-screen and RVV-confirmed reagents (America Diagnostica, Greenwich CT). A T1/T2 ratio of not less than 1.3 was defined as positive.

# Data Analysis

In the groups of patients who developed cerebral infarction, many were expected to have relevant risk factors concurrently. Therefore, we decided to use logistic regression analysis. In the present study, logistic regression analysis

was performed using IgG aPS/PT as the dependant variable with beta 2-GPI aCL, LA, and each risk factor as independent variables.

# Results

The observed risk factors and results for each antiphospholipid antibody in the 93 patients with cerebral infarction are shown in Table 1. The prevalences of concomitant risk factors were hypertension $55.9\%$ (52/93), diabetes $43.0\%$ (40/93), hyperlipidemia $38.7\%$ (36/93), and atrial fibrillation $8.6\%$ (8/93). Types of cerebral infarction in each of the 93 investigated cases were classified into lacunar, atherothrombotic, or cardiogenic cerebral infarction. The most prevalent type was lacunar infarction, which accounted for $63.4\%$ of all cases. Complication rates for hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and atrial fibrillation are presented in Table 1. Of the 93 patients who had cerebral infarction, 7 tested positive for IgG aPS/PT, 3 tested positive for beta 2-GPI aCL, and 10 tested positive for LA; the prevalence rates were thus $7.5\%$ for IgG aPS/PT, $3.2\%$ for beta 2-GPI aCL, and $10.8\%$ for LA. Of these patients, the 7 who tested positive for IgG aPS/PT are presented in Table 2. Case No.1 (29-year-old woman) and case No.4 (84-year-old woman) had no risk factors other than the antiphospholipid antibodies.

The normal range of IgG aPS/PT is less than $12\mathrm{U / mL}$ , and the averages in the IgG aPS/PT-positive group and the IgG aPS/PT-negative group were $21.91\pm 8.88$ (mean $\pm{\mathrm{SD}}$ ) and $5.78\pm 4.81$ (mean $\pm{\mathrm{SD}}$ ), respectively. The normal range of LA is less than 1.3, and the averages in the LA-positive group and the LA-negative group were $1.62\pm 0.41$ (mean $\pm{\mathrm{SD}}$ ) and $1.00\pm 0.09$ (mean $\pm{\mathrm{SD}}$ ), respectively. In addition, $7.5\%$ (7/93) were IgG aPS/PT-positive, $3.2\%$ (3/93) were beta 2-GPI aCL-positive, and $10.7\%$ (10/93) were LA-positive.

The results of the logistic regression analysis were as follows. LA had a $\rho$ -value of 0.0051 and an odds ratio of 89.9, and showed a significantly greater contribution than the other predictor variables. None of the other factors exhibited a statistically significant contribution to IgG aPS/PT (Table 3).

Not all of the 93 patients could be followed up: 12 patients changed hospitals, 3 moved and 2 died from other causes. However, in the 76 patients whom we followed up one year after the end of the present study, the recurrence rate of cerebral infarction was $6.6\%$ (5 patients). Among these 5 patients, only 2 patients tested positive for IgG aPS/PT.

Prothrombin-phospholipid binding is dependent on calcium and is phosphatidylserine-specific. IgG aPS/PT is an autoantibody directed against phosphatidylserine-prothrombin complex (7, 8), and is thought to be significantly related to APS (2).

Amengual et al (9) examined the sensitivity and specificity of a variety of antiphospholipid antibodies in 219 patients with autoimmune diseases, including 82 patients with APS, and reported that IgG aPS/PT had an extremely high specificity of $92\%$ to APS and therefore should be useful in

diagnosing APS. Here, we aimed to examine the relationship among beta 2-GPI aCL, LA, and IgG aPS/PT in 93 patients who developed cerebral infarction.

Previously, we investigated the prevalence of antiphospholipid antibodies in 253 patients with cerebral infarction, and observed frequencies of $2.8\%$ for beta 2-GPI aCL, $7.2\%$ for IgG aPS/PT, and $9.2\%$ for LA (7). Beta 2-GPI aCL had the lowest positive rate. The distributions of IgG aPS/PT, beta 2-GPI aCL and LA in patients with cerebral infarction are shown in Fig. 1.

However, it is important to note that the definition of LA includes loss of anticoagulation activation upon addition of excess phospholipids, and we can not ex

plain the relationship of such phospholipid dependency to the development mechanism of thrombosis.

Recently, Ieko et al (5) reported that IgG aPS/PT recognizes prothrombin bound to phosphatidylserine on platelets and endothelial cells and, directly or via Fc-gamma receptors, activates a variety of procoagulant agents. If this is the case, then despite the fact that antiphospholipid antibody-specific antigens were consistently present in the plasma and did not induce thrombus formation a possible reason as to why our two IgG aPS/PT-positive patients developed cerebral infarction would be that thrombus formation was triggered by some other cause, such as infection.

IgG aPS/PT did not appear to play a dominant role in our patients with cerebral infarction, but might play a role in modulating arteriosclerosis. In this study, however, 2 of the IgG aPS/PT-positive patients with cerebral infarction were both beta 2-GPI aCL- and LA-negative, and it seems likely that they might have had APS pathology, although the diagnostic criteria were not met. Further investigation of the role of IgG aPS/PT in cerebral infarction is needed.
