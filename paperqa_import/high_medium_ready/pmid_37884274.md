Supporting Information

ABSTRACT: Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an autoimmune disease characterized by recurrent arterial, venous, and microvascular thrombosis where activated neutrophils play a determinant role. However, neutrophils are challenging to target given their short lifespan in circulation and spontaneous activation. Screening of a small library of gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) led to the discovery of a formulation capable of targeting activated neutrophil attachment and has demonstrated that star-shaped, anti-PSGL-1-antibody-coated AuNPs (aPSGL-1-AuNPs) were more

efficacious compared with other shapes of AuNPs. Our findings further revealed an exciting and safe targeting mode toward activated neutrophils in the APS mouse model induced by human-patient-derived antiphospholipid IgGs. Our studies demonstrate that targeting is dependent on the specific topographical features of the highly segregated PSGL-1 on the activated neutrophil surface for which a high affinity shape-driven nanomedicine can be designed and implemented. As such, star-shaped aPSGL-1-AuNPs serve as a promising nanoimmunotherapy for immunothrombosis associated with neutrophil adhesion in APS.

KEYWORDS: gold nanoparticles, thrombosis, neutrophil targeting, PSGL-1, antiphospholipid syndrome

![](images/a7faeb825ff0b982a3c888979e70ae368e75748431620c6365913c450d77512d.jpg)

Antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (APS) is a rare systemic autoimmune disorder that is characterized by a high risk of recurrent arterial, venous, and microvascular thrombosis in the presence of persistent antiphospholipid antibodies (aPLs). Presently, these devastating thrombotic events are managed with long-term anticoagulation therapies. Most such treatments can be effective, but they impede the essential hemostatic system and pose a high bleeding risk.

Attempting to develop effective and safe antithrombotic treatments, researchers have leveraged the observed relationships between inflammation and coagulation.2,3 Cells of the innate immune system, such as neutrophils, are important players in clot generation, otherwise called "immunothrombosis".4-6 Together with studies done by others, our recently published work showed that activated neutrophils serve as critical mediators of both arterial and venous thrombosis in APS and this effect is dependent on a neutrophil-specific surface ligand, p-selectin glycoprotein ligand-1 (PSGL-1).7-9 Using anti-PSGL-1 antibodies and other pharmacotherapeutic approaches, our group and other researchers have shown that PSGL-1 blockade alleviated both arterial and venous thrombosis.7-9 Ongoing clinical trials are testing the use of PSGL-1 antibodies to treat venous thrombosis (NCT03812328) and bronchial asthma (NCT04540042). Small molecule inhibitors of PSGL-1 have also been shown to mitigate venous thrombosis.9 However, PSGL-1 is widely

expressed on all immune cells, endothelial cells, and platelets;[10-13] therefore, these approaches pose the risk of side effects from nonspecific blockade of essential cellular elements. Thus, the main challenge is to identify and target inflammatory neutrophils that beget immunothrombosis while sparing sentinel neutrophils and other immune cells that are important for innate immunity.

Mechanistically, inflammatory neutrophil activation is associated with PSGL-1 clustering on neutrophil uropods facilitating neutrophil–endothelial cell adhesion as a forerunner to both arterial and venous thrombosis.[8,14,15] We previously tested a polystyrene-nanoparticle-based approach to selectively target only activated neutrophils that harbor PSGL-1 clusters.[7] While the concept of reducing thrombosis via targeting neutrophil PSGL-1 clusters is achievable, we aim to develop a more sufficient biocompatible nanoparticle to accomplish the goal. Our prior studies demonstrated that PSGL-1 clusters on activated neutrophils facilitated attachment to p-selectin causing pro-thrombotic events. $^{7}$ In this study, we designed and tested nanoparticles that selectively target activated neutrophils identified by clustered PSGL-1 present on uropods. As compared with our prior studies, we modified the composition and size of the nanoparticles. Our group has used polystyrene-based nanoparticles to investigate their preferential binding to activated neutrophils in vitro and in vivo. However, this material has limited versatility to form nanoparticles in unique shapes and is poorly biodegradable. Here, we opted for gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) as a versatile material for which many synthetic routes are known to achieve various geometries while ensuring the precise control of size and structural attributes. Additionally, many studies indicated that the shape of nanoparticles governs unique targeting efficacy and immune responses. In this study, we designed a screening assay to examine various shapes of AuNPs. The shapes of AuNPs were determined under transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Figure 1a) and the dimensions were assessed using an opne-source software, ImageJ. Besides the larger hex plates $(650 \pm 117\mathrm{nm})$ and smaller rods $(46 \pm 1.2\mathrm{nm})$ , other shapes were approximately $100\mathrm{nm}$ : high aspect ratio rods $(114 \pm 28\mathrm{nm})$ , cubes $(108 \pm 2.3\mathrm{nm})$ , triangles $(81.0 \pm 3.0\mathrm{nm})$ , spheres $(104 \pm 3.8\mathrm{nm})$ , and stars $(107 \pm 5.7\mathrm{nm})$ . Further, monoclonal anti-PSGL-1 antibodies or IgG antibodies were covalently attached to the surface of various nanoparticles (aPSGL-1-AuNPs or IgG-AuNPs) by carbodiimide chemistry

while maintaining the same antibody concentration per particle for all AuNPs. The size, zeta potential, and stability in human plasma were measured with DLS (Supp. Table 1), and the protein content on the AuNP surface was examined by agarose gel electrophoresis and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) (Supp. Figure 1). Differently shaped aPSGL-1-AuNPs or IgG-AuNPs were screened for their ability to block neutrophil attachment to p-selectin using an attachment screening assay, as shown in Figure 1b. Primary neutrophils were isolated from wild type (WT) mice, activated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS), and treated with aPSGL-1-AuNPs or IgG-AuNPs. The neutrophils were plated on a p-selectin-coated 96-well plate, and unbound cells were washed away. The remaining attached neutrophils within each well were counted. Irrespective of the shape (Figure 1c), all AuNPs drastically reduced the number of activated neutrophils attached by 1.4-2.3-fold as compared to no AuNPs treatment. Coating AuNPs with aPSGL-1 antibodies further reduced activated neutrophil attachment compared to IgG coating. Star-shaped aPSGL-1-AuNPs reduced the number of attached neutrophils by 1.3-fold $(p = 0.041)$ as compared to the IgG-coated counterparts (Figure 1c). Another lead from this screening assay was spherical AuNPs, displaying a 1.1-fold difference in cell attachment between IgG- and aPSGL-1-AuNPs, yet this was not statistically significant. Nevertheless, we selected spherical AuNPs as NP controls in the following experiments. Further investigation of activated neutrophils under TEM (Figure 1d) displayed accumulation of star-shaped aPSGL-1-AuNPs within the LPS-activated neutrophil uropods, which are known to be enriched with PSGL-1.[37,38] In contrast, accumulation of star-shaped IgG-AuNPs was absent in LPS-activated neutrophils. To further demonstrate this targeting selectivity, we labeled AuNPs with an Atto 647 fluorophore followed by the incubation with activated neutrophils and subsequent immunofluorescence studies. The results show that star-shaped aPSGL-1-AuNPs co-localized with PSGL-1 protein clusters on the surface uropods of activated neutrophils (Figure 1e). This was in contrast to spherical AuNPs and IgG-AuNPs of both spherical and star shapes (Supp. Figure 2). Together, our data suggested that the

To the best of our knowledge, our studies are the first to use nanoparticle topology to identify a specifically shaped AuNPs that most efficiently reduce the level of activated neutrophil anchoring.

Similar adhesion assay was performed with neutrophils obtained from the APS mouse model. The APS mouse model was induced by injecting aPLs obtained from APS human patients. Human IgG isotype was injected to produce a control mouse model. Isotype or aPL was administered intravenously daily for 5 days (Figure 2a). In the previous study, the neutrophils isolated from human aPL treated mice demonstrated increased attachment to p-selectin as compared to isotype treatment. In this study, we isolated neutrophils from aPL or IgG injected mice and then treated them with lead NP candidates (i.e., spherical- and star-shaped AuNPs) as determined from the screening assay above. As compared to the star-shaped IgG-AuNPs, star-shaped aPSGL-1-AuNPs significantly decreased neutrophil attachment to p-selectin-coated plates by 1.75-fold $(p = 0.046)$ as shown in the representative fluorescence micrographs (Figure 2b) and by quantification of adherent neutrophil counts (Figure 2c). Further, no significant change in neutrophil attachment was observed when neutrophils were incubated with spherical AuNPs irrespective of the coating with IgG or aPSGL (Figure 2c). No significant changes in attachment were noted with neutrophils isolated from isotype injected mice (Supp. Figure 3a). The screening assay suggested that the star shape has

higher affinity to PSGL-1 clusters on the activated neutrophil surface as spherical aPSGL-1-AuNPs had no effect on neutrophil attachment. Consequently, the star-shaped aPSGL-1-AuNPs blocked the attachment of the activated neutrophils isolated from the APS mouse model. This data suggests that nanoparticle topography is a determinant of their selectivity toward activated neutrophils.

Based on these promising data, further in vivo examination of star-shaped aPSGL-1-AuNPs in thrombosis models with aPL-injected mice was carried out. Throughout the in vivo studies, star-shaped IgG-AuNPs were used as controls. As indicated in Figure 3a, after two injections of aPL, carotid artery thrombosis was induced by the injection of Rose Bengal. Rose Bengal is a photochemical dye that can be taken up by the endothelial cells. When exposed to green light at $540\mathrm{nm}$ , it releases various reactive oxygen species causing endothelial dysfunction and occlusive thrombus formation. In our previous study, time to occlusive thrombus occurred faster in a prothrombotic state such as that in APS mice. The shortened thrombosis time is dependent on the increased neutrophil activation and adhesion to the endothelial surface. In this study, star-shaped aPSGL-1-AuNP treatment prolonged the time to occlusive thrombosis in APS mice by 1.91-fold compared to star-shaped IgG-AuNPs (34.8 s vs 18.2 s, $p = 0.00034$ ) (Figure 3b), although the star-shaped aPSGL-1-AuNP treatment did not restore the occlusion time to the same level of the occlusion time in WT mice (34.8 s vs 37.7 s, $p = 0.3652$ ). This data suggested the star-shaped aPSGL-1-AuNPs did not exacerbate inflammation-induced pro-thrombotic phenotypes; instead, the particles were able to ameliorate inflammation-induced thrombosis.

The effects of star-shaped AuNPs on venous thrombosis in the APS mouse model were also investigated. The experiments were carried out as illustrated in Figure 3c in aPL treated mice injected with AuNPs. Venous thrombosis was induced by complete inferior vena cava (IVC) ligation, and the IVC thrombi were collected after $48\mathrm{h}$ . Our prior study demonstrated that in this model the aPL injection was associated with a significantly larger IVC thrombi weights as compared to isotype injection. As shown in Figure 3d,e, star-shaped aPSGL-1-AuNP treatment significantly decreased IVC thrombi weights in the aPL-injected mice as compared to star-shaped IgG-AuNP by 1.37-fold $(27.5\mathrm{mg}$ vs $37.8\mathrm{mg}, p = 0.0037)$ (Figure 3d).

We previously showed that the deletion of Kruppel-like factor 2 (KLF2) unleashed activated neutrophils and resulted in larger IVC thrombi. Using KLF2 $^{-/-}$ mice as a model in which neutrophils are perpetually activated, we investigated whether star-shaped AuNPs are efficacious even in the genetically driven pro-inflammatory state. Indeed, the injection of star-shaped aPSGL-1-AuNP in these mice showed a significant reduction in IVC thrombi weights of 1.34-fold $(28.8\mathrm{mg}$ vs $38.7\mathrm{mg}, p = 0.0037)$ (Supp. Figure3d,e) as compared to star-shaped IgG-AuNP. Notably, the star-shaped aPSGL-1-AuNP also mitigated arterial thrombosis in KLF2 $^{-/-}$ mice as compared to star-shaped IgG-AuNP (data not shown).

We posit that this is likely a result of reduced neutrophil adhesion to endothelial p-selectin via the neutrophil PSGL-1 blockade, which is also in line with our in vitro screening data.

While neutrophils and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) are a first line of defense against pathogens, accumulating research over the past decade has established the important role of neutrophils and NETs in both arterial and venous thrombosis.40 NETs contribute to thrombus generation by forming a scaffold for cellular elements such as platelets and red blood cells and activation of the coagulation cascade. To further investigate neutrophil recruitment to the site of injury, IVC thrombi (shown in Figure 3e) were stained for neutrophil markers and NETs (Figure 4a,b). The following immunofluorescence staining was used to identify neutrophils and NETs: neutrophils were identified as Ly6G and MPO positive cells, while citrullinated histone H3, MPO, and extracellular DNA were indicative of the underlying NETs. As compared to star-shaped IgG-AuNPs, the star-shaped aPSGL-1-AuNP treatment reduced the neutrophil count by 1.88-fold (416 neutrophils/slice vs 222 neutrophils/slice, $p = 0.048$ ) and decreased the NET content in IVC thrombi by 3-fold (6 NETs/slice vs 2 NETs/slice, $p = 0.0047$ ). These observations further supported that the star-shaped aPSGL-1-AuNPs decreased the level of neutrophil recruitment to the site of injury (i.e., thrombus) and suppressed neutrophil activation.

Finally, to test the safety of AuNPs, we performed bleeding assays in WT mice untreated or preinjected with star-shaped AuNPs. In these assays, the bleeding is induced by tail-tip amputation of $2\mathrm{mm}$ followed by the recording of the time to bleeding cessation. Simultaneously, all blood is collected for quantitative analysis of hemoglobin as an alternative indicator of bleeding severity. Importantly, there were neither changes in the bleeding time (Supp. Figure 4a) nor hemoglobin content (Supp. Figure 4b) among all three conditions. To understand

<!-- page 6 -->
whether the injection of AuNPs may have influenced the composition and numbers of white blood cells in these mice, we performed complete blood count (CBC) analysis. Notably, no significant changes in CBC were observed among all three conditions (Supp. Figure 4c-g). This data and insights from the literature indicate that AuNPs are likely to be safe with low bleeding risk, because these NPs do not target the major hemostatic pathways as most anticoagulants do.[41]

In conclusion, star-shaped aPSGL-1-AuNPs represent a novel approach to target PSGL-1 clusters on activated neutrophil surface. This targeting likely occurs through PSGL-1 clusters on activated neutrophil uropods. The star shape is a critical determinant of the efficacy of aPSGL-1-AuNPs in reducing neutrophil recruitment and thrombus formation. Importantly, this benefit occurs in the absence of alterations in hemostasis. Finally, this thromboprotection is noted in both arterial and venous thrombosis without any significant effect on tail bleeding.

In this study, we demonstrated for the first time that topographical features may guide therapeutic candidate selection for neutrophil targeting. Neutrophils play an important role not only in thrombosis but also in a wide variety of infectious, inflammatory, and autoimmune conditions, such as inflammatory bowel disease, systemic lupus erythematosus, and even cancer. Therefore, our approach has broad therapeutic potential. Future studies will focus on reducing the cost of these nanoparticles by substituting gold with cheaper synthetic polymers or lipids.
