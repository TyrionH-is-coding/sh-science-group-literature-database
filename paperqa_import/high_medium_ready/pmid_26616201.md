# ABSTRACT

Objectives: The antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an autoimmune disorder of acquired hypercoagulability characterized by vascular thrombosis, increased pregnancy morbidity, and elevated levels of antiphospholipid antibodies. Cardiac involvement in APS may be presented as heart valve disease affecting approximately a third of patients or less frequently as intracardial thrombosis, pulmonary hypertension, right or left ventricular dysfunction, micro-vascular thrombosis, coronary artery, or microvascular disease with overt or silent clinical presentation.

Methods: Noninvasive cardiovascular imaging plays a crucial role in the evaluation of heart involvement in APS. Transthoracic or transoesophageal echocardiography enable early, accurate diagnosis and severity assessment of HVD as well as of ventricular dysfunction and pulmonary hypertension. Studies by echocardiography and nuclear imaging have detected abnormalities in myocardial perfusion in approximately $30\%$ of primary APS. CT scan is the technique of choice for the assessment of pulmonary embolism and can effectively detect intracardiac thrombi. Myocardial perfusion defects have been detected by 13N-ammonia PET in $40\%$ of APS. Cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) has identified an unexpectedly high prevalence of occult myocardial scarring and endomyocardial fibrosis in APS, and is the technique of choice, if quantification of heart valve disease and stress myocardial perfusion-fibrosis is needed.

Results: Noninvasive, nonradiating imaging techniques, such as echocardiography and CMR are superior to CT or nuclear techniques and are of great value for the diagnosis and follow-up of both clinically overt and silent cardiac disease in APS.

Conclusions: The high incidence of cardiac involvement in APS demands early diagnosis/treatment and multimodality cardiovascular imaging is of great importance.

# Introduction

The antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an autoimmune disorder characterized by venous, arterial, and/or small vessel thrombosis, pregnancy morbidity, and elevated levels of antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL), including lupus anticoagulant (LAC) and/or anticardiolipin antibodies (aCL), and/or anti-beta2-glycoprotein I (anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ ) antibodies [1]. The syndrome can be either primary or secondary, associated with an underlying condition, most commonly systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

Cardiac involvement in APS may be presented either as heart valve disease (HVD) or less frequently as nonvalvular disease, including intracardial thrombosis, pulmonary hypertension, right or left ventricular dysfunction, and ischemic heart disease either due to coronary artery or micro-vascular disease with overt or asymptomatic clinical presentation [2-4]. Heart valve thickening and vegetations are the most common cardiac manifestations in patients with primary or SLE-related APS. Mitral valve is most frequently affected, followed by the aortic valve; regurgitation is more common than stenosis, but usually without severe hemodynamic significance. In most prospective echocardiographic studies in APS, valvular disease was persistent or progressive over time, independently of the use of anticoagulant or antiplatelet treatment [3-5]. Most patients remain asymptomatic for years, while heart

failure and valve replacement surgery are some of valve disease complications [6]. Libman-Sacks valvular lesions are sterile fibro-fibrinous vegetations that may develop anywhere on the endocardial surface of the heart, especially in the ventricular surface of the mitral valve [5,7,8] and may lead to significant regurgitation, and eventually to pulmonary hypertension. Libman-Sacks vegetations have been detected in 1 of 10 patients with SLE, and correlated with disease duration, disease activity, and the presence of anticardiolipin antibodies and antiphospholipid syndrome [8].

Right or left ventricular diastolic dysfunction has also been reported in the context of primary or SLE-related APS, regardless of the presence of valvular disease or systolic dysfunction [9-11]. Prospective echocardiographic studies have shown a progression of ventricular dysfunction during a long-term follow-up period [10,11]. Intracardiac thrombosis, in the form of single or multiple thrombi, is a rare cardiac manifestation of APS that may cause pulmonary and systemic emboli [12,13]. Pulmonary hypertension is another rare manifestation of APS, associated with pulmonary artery thrombosis or micro-vascular thrombosis and endothelial remodeling [9,14].

Myocardial involvement in APS includes myocardial infarction, or myocardial ischemia due to coronary vasospasm or microvascular thrombosis. In a European cohort of 1000 patients with APS, myocardial infarction was diagnosed in $5.5\%$ of APS patients and was the presenting manifestation in $2.8\%$ of them [2]. In a recent systematic review, the estimated frequency of antiphospholipid antibodies among patients with myocardial infarction in the general population was $11\%$ [15]. Additionally, coronary vasospasm, known as Prinzmetal's angina or syndrome X, has been described in APS and may lead to myocardial ischemia without thrombosis [16,17]. Myocardial ischemia can also be caused by thrombotic cardiac micro-vascular disease; as a consequence, the ischemic subendocardium could act as a trigger for clot formation, especially, if there is concurrent LV dysfunction [18]. Kattwinkel et al. [19] described the case of a patient with primary APS, who was diagnosed with cardiac necrosis and normal coronary arteries. Endomyocardial biopsy showed a noninflammatory microvascular disease, characterized by bland thrombi and lack of inflammatory cells in the vessel wall. Finally, endomyocardial fibrosis (EMF) is another nonvalvular cardiac manifestation that has been reported in some cases with APS [20], with uncertain pathogenesis.

# Noninvasive imaging techniques to assess cardiovascular involvement in APS

# Echocardiography/ultrasonography

Echocardiography is the cornerstone of cardiac imaging and gives reliable information about valvular integrity, wall motion changes, right and left ventricular function, and assessment of pulmonary hypertension. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) and/or transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) enable early and accurate diagnosis and severity assessment of HVD. The prevalence of valve disease in APS is about $30 - 40\%$ in TTE studies, reaching the level of $82\%$ in TEE studies [3-5,21]. In the context of APS, valve lesions are defined by (a) valve thickness $>3\mathrm{mm}$ ; (b) localized thickening involving the proximal or middle part of the leaflets; or (c) irregular nodules on the atrial face of the mitral valve and/or the vascular face of the aortic valve [22]. The application of real-time 3D echocardiographic imaging (RT3D) for the evaluation of Libman-Sacks vegetations' size can strengthen the already well established role of TTE and TEE; furthermore, the exact estimation of vegetations' size may influence the therapeutic interventions [23]. A prospective study

including a 10-year echocardiographic follow-up of patients with APS and patients with SLE with or without aPL, documented that the coexistence of SLE and APS, and the disease duration were independent factors for valvular disease progression. In addition, ventricular diastolic dysfunction, primarily of the left ventricle, also progressed over the 10-year period while anticoagulation could not arrest the progression of both valvular disease and myocardial dysfunction [11].

TTE can also detect complex and irregular masses adherent to the endocardial surface of any cardiac chamber or great vessels, and TEE can provide details about their anatomy and pathophysiology [12,21,24,25]. For the detection of left ventricular thrombi, TTE has a sensitivity and a specificity of $86 - 95\%$ [26-28]. TTE can not reliably visualize the left atrium or left atrial appendage (LAA), and has a sensitivity of only $39 - 63\%$ for the detection of left atrial thrombi [29-31]. The diagnostic accuracy of TEE has been evaluated in patients undergoing surgery or autopsy. For the diagnosis of left ventricular thrombus, TEE has a sensitivity and specificity similar to those of TTE [32]. However, for the diagnosis of left atrial thrombi, TEE has a sensitivity of $100\%$ and a specificity of $99\%$ [33].

The presence of pulmonary hypertension is based on hemodynamic parameters estimated by TTE and confirmed by right heart catheterization [3,21]. In a recent study including 53 patients with primary and SLE-associated APS pulmonary hypertension was diagnosed using TTE in $11.3\%$ of patients and it was the most common nonvalvular cardiac manifestation. Left myocardial disease and intracardiac thrombi were rare manifestations with a prevalence of $3.8\%$ and $1.8\%$ respectively [10].

A high level of agreement between contrast echocardiography and nuclear imaging for the assessment of myocardial perfusion was observed in a prospective study of patients with primary APS reporting a $30\%$ prevalence of myocardial perfusion abnormalities by both techniques [34].

Ultrasonography is particularly useful for the assessment of common carotid arteries, where it is up to 10-fold more sensitive than magnetic resonance, displaying a resolution of $0.1 - 0.2\mathrm{mm}$ . Therefore, it can identify peripheral vessels' thrombosis, due to APS. Furthermore, premature atherosclerosis expressed as increased carotid intima media thickness (IMT), is a clinical feature of thrombotic primary APS and can be easily assessed by ultrasonography [35].

Echocardiography is a bedside, inexpensive, widely available, nonradiating technique. It is ideal for diagnosis and follow-up of cardiovascular involvement in APS patients. However, it is an operator-dependent technique and carries the limitation of poor acoustic window in a significant percentage of patients. Ultrasonography is also a highly operator-dependent method. Furthermore, while imaging is optimal in the common carotid and vertebral arteries, the assessment of proximal subclavian and distal internal carotid arteries is limited, due to overlying tissues. High-resolution US of the aorta could be an excellent alternative. However, high-resolution probes still have disadvantage of pour penetration and can not be used for evaluation of deep thoracic or abdominal vessels, especially in obese patients [36].

# High-resolution computed tomography (CT)

A high-resolution cardiac computed tomography (CT) can confirm the presence of pseudotumours with or without partial calcification and the absence of contrast enhancement in cases of intracardiac thrombi in patients with APS [12,37]. CT scan is the technique of choice for the assessment of pulmonary embolism and pulmonary hypertension due to recurrent pulmonary emboli. CT scan may reveal bilateral patchy areas of ground-glass attenuation and pulmonary angiography moderately dilated central pulmonary arteries with or without pulmonary emboli.

Cardiac CT is an accurate method for the detection of cardiac thrombi. According to a recent study, the overall sensitivity, specificity, positive, and negative predictive value of CT for the detection of thrombi in the LAA were $89\%$ , $100\%$ , $100\%$ , and $99\%$ respectively [38].

CT is widely available and radiologists are very familiar with its applications. The main limitations of CT include the need for iodinated contrast and the radiation exposure. The current resolution of CT is most effective in the assessment of the aorta and its proximal branches, and relatively limited of distal aortic branches [39].

# Nuclear cardiology

PET is a noninvasive technique that allows the assessment of myocardial perfusion with the use of different radiotracers, such as ammonia (13N-ammonia), 82-rubidium or oxygen (H215O) and can quantify the coronary blood flow in $\mathrm{ml / g / min}$ using 13N-ammonia [40].

In APS, even without clinically overt cardiovascular disease, myocardial perfusion defects can be detected by 13N-ammonia PET in nearly $40\%$ of patients [41]. Another study, evaluating endothelial function in primary APS using PET, found an important reduction in myocardial flow reserve, endothelial-dependent vasodilation index, and percent change of myocardial blood flow in primary APS compared to healthy controls [42].

However, the low-spatial resolution of PET and other nuclear techniques do not allow the detection of small myocardial perfusion-fibrosis defects. Additionally, lack of availability, high cost, high expertise needed, and significant radiation exposure remain serious disadvantages for routine application of nuclear techniques in APS.

# Cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging (CMR)

It is an accurate noninvasive, nonradiating imaging technique, ideal for evaluation of cardiac anatomy, function, inflammation, myocardial perfusion, and fibrosis [43,44]. Stress perfusion-fibrosis CMR demonstrated subendocardial perfusion defects (PD) in patients with cardiac syndrome X, with a much higher sensitivity than conventional perfusion techniques [45] and has been already applied in coronary artery [46] and micro-vascular disease [47]. CE-MARC, the largest, prospective, real world evaluation of CMR, has established its high diagnostic accuracy in coronary artery disease and superiority over SPECT for the detection of myocardial ischemia and scar [48]. Finally, CMR is the most reliable imaging

technique to detect and quantify scar or fibrotic tissue (viability study). Both acute and old fibrotic/scar lesions retain contrast agent and therefore appear bright [49-53]. The preferred imaging time for scar detection is between 10 and $20\mathrm{min}$ after contrast agent administration, when the differences between scar, normal myocardium, and blood pool are maximal. This method is referred in the literature as late gadolinium enhanced CMR (LGE) and is the gold standard for the in vivo assessment of myocardial scar. CMR can detect scar in as little as $1\mathrm{cm}^3$ of tissue, substantially less than other in vivo methods and has shown excellent agreement with histology [52-58]. CMR can also detect subendocardial myocardial infarction, missed by SPECT and/or PET [59,60].

Stress myocardial perfusion-fibrosis evaluation has been already applied for the evaluation of patients with scleroderma [44], sarcoidosis [61], and systemic lupus erythematosus [62]. Additionally, LGE has been successfully used to clarify cardiac pathophysiology in connective tissue diseases [63,64]. Significant and unexpectedly high prevalence (11.1%) of occult myocardial scarring has been documented in patients with APS, indicating the usefulness of LGE for the identification of silent myocardial disease in these patients [65] (Fig. 1). Additionally, endomyocardial fibrosis (EMF), a rare complication in APS, has been described by Azeem et al. [20] as diffuse subendocardial LGE and had a good correlation with histopathology in patients with EMF. Therefore, the detection of this LGE pattern in the absence of obstructive lesions in epicardial coronary arteries reflects an early manifestation of EMF in APS [66,67] (Fig. 2).

For evaluation of HVD during APS, CMR is the technique of choice if quantification of valve disease and myocardial scar tissue are needed [68].

Compared with TTE, CMR is more sensitive for the detection of left ventricular thrombi. Additionally, the characterization of thrombi by CMR may predict the risk of embolism, that is higher for subacute than for well organized thrombi [69]. Although contrast echo in high-risk patients can significantly improve the detection of LV thrombi, it does not detect a substantial number of thrombi identified by contrast enhanced CMR (DE-CMR). These thrombi detected by DE-CMR but not by contrast echo were mural and/or small in volume [70]. Finally, according to another study, DE-CMR provides the highest sensitivity and specificity for LV thrombi detection, compared with TTE and TEE, and should be considered as the best approach in patients at high-risk for LV thrombus formation [71].

The main CMR limitations include:

(a) it is time consuming   
(b) it is not widely available

(c) it is expensive   
(d) it needs high expertise   
(e) Patients with metallic clips, pacemakers, and devices can not be scanned. However, new MR-safe devices are available and are recommended in cases likely to require serial CMR evaluations [72].   
(f) Chelates of Gadolinium are the CMR contrast agents. Although there is no evidence of direct nephrotoxicity, some agents have been linked to systemic fibrosis (known as nephrogenic systemic fibrosis—NSF) in patients with severe renal dysfunction. NSF is so rare that there is no exact estimation of its prevalence; it provokes skin fibrosis, leading to contractures, joint immobility, internal organ involvement, and finally death. To the best of our knowledge, NSF has occurred only in patients with glomerural filtration rate (GFR) of $< 30 \mathrm{ml} / \mathrm{min} / 1.73 \mathrm{~m}^2$ or patients on hemodialysis. Effective treatment of NSF is not currently available; importantly no new cases have been reported since specific recommendations regarding clinical gadolinium use have been implemented [68].   
(g) There is no known risk for use of this imaging technique during pregnancy. However, it is recommended that CMR should be avoided during the first trimester. Chelates of gadolinium should not be used, as no data are available on possible teratogenic effects. Breastfeeding women must discard their milk for $24\mathrm{h}$ after contrast agent injection [68].

# Suggested algorithm for evaluation of cardiovascular disease in APS

Although it is not easy to establish an imaging algorithm for APS evaluation, we should always keep in mind the following approach:

(1) Echocardiography and ultrasonography remain the cornerstones of cardiovascular assessment in APS, because they are widely available, nonradiating, inexpensive techniques, excellent for bedside diagnosis, and serial follow-up. Echocardiography performs very well for valvular and left or right ventricular function evaluation. However, if there is a need for quantification of valvular regurgitation, stress myocardial perfusion-fibrosis defects, and EMF detection, CMR is superior, compared to all other techniques.   
(2) Use as less radiation and iodinate contrast media as possible. That means avoid CT, if CMR is available and there is enough experience. Also avoid gadolinium in patients with low-GFR.   
(3) PET is useful, if it is positive. However, PET has low-spatial resolution and therefore, a negative PET does not exclude myocardial involvement, especially in cases of subendocardial myocardial perfusion defects, small scars, and EMF.

# Practical points

(1) In patients with either primary APS or SLE-associated APS, an echocardiographic study should be performed in every patient at initial evaluation. The main evaluation should be performed using transthoracic echocardiography. Transesophageal assessment should be applied only if there are specific indications such as thrombi assessment, aortic dissection, endocarditis, or valvular disease. If cardiac dysfunction is detected, a cardiologist's consultation and an echocardiographic follow-up is recommended, based on the severity of the findings and the presence of symptoms. If there are queries regarding the etiology of cardiac dysfunction and/or quantification of valvular regurgitation, CMR can be of great value.

(2) CMR is recommended for the differential diagnosis and age assessment of intracardiac masses.   
(3) For patients with symptomatic ischemic disease, CMR is also recommended as a gatekeeper for invasive coronary angiography. Additionally, it is recommended in those with negative findings in coronary angiography to assess the potential of micro-vascular disease.   
(4) If there is a mismatch between patients' symptoms and routine clinical, or imaging evaluation, CMR can often give the final answer.   
(5) CMR is the only technique that can perform quantification of valvular regurgitation, detection of EMF, and stress myocardial perfusion-fibrosis defects, and should be considered in cases with detected abnormalities in the first evaluation by echocardiography.

# Conclusions

The high incidence of cardiac involvement in APS demands early diagnosis and treatment to avoid serious complications. Noninvasive, nonradiating imaging techniques, such as echocardiography/ultrasonography, and CMR are superior to nuclear techniques and CT can be of great value for cardiovascular assessment and follow-up of APS patients.

Echocardiography is ideal for HVD and ventricular function evaluation. However, CMR can offer a combination of anatomy, function, inflammation, quantification of valvular lesions, and stress myocardial perfusion-fibrosis with excellent reproducibility that facilitates both early diagnosis and serial follow-up, and may motivate further cardiac assessment and early cardiac treatment.
