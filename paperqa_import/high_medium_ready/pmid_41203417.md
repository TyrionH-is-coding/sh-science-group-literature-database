# Proteins upregulated in thrombotic antiphospholipid syndrome linked to platelet function in contrast with non-antiphospholipid-related thrombosis: insights from two systematic reviews

## Abstract

Background: Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a systemic autoimmune disease characterised by thrombosis, pregnancy complications and other non-thrombotic manifestations in the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL). Since its identification in 1983, research on APS has progressed, but no targeted therapies other than anticoagulation are yet available, with a mortality rate of 9.3% after a 10-year follow-up.

Objective: The aim of the current systematic literature reviews (SLRs) is to identify and compare upregulated proteins in patients with thrombotic APS (tAPS) and non-aPL thrombosis, thereby providing useful insights into APS pathogenesis.

Methods: We conducted two SLRs in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines to identify proteins upregulated in tAPS and non-aPL-related thrombosis. Eligible studies included observational controlled research and focused on proteomics. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment and network analyses were performed on the identified proteins.

Results: Of 108 and 209 records identified, seven and 13 were included in the SLR for tAPS and non-aPL thrombosis, respectively. The review identified 118 upregulated proteins in tAPS and 319 in non-aPL-related thrombosis. GO analysis revealed distinct biological process enrichment: platelet aggregation, platelet activation and blood coagulation were predominant in tAPS, while haemostasis, coagulation and wound healing were central in non-aPL-related thrombosis. Molecular functions in tAPS centred on receptor and protein complex binding, while enzymatic activities dominated in non-aPL thrombosis.

Conclusion: Our findings highlight the central role of platelet-related processes in tAPS pathogenesis, distinguishing it from non-aPL thrombosis. By elucidating the unique proteomic and functional characteristics of tAPS, this study provides a foundation for future research into targeted therapies that address platelet involvement in APS pathogenesis.

## Introduction

The spread of proteomics has been fuelled by advancements in cutting-edge technologies, such as mass spectrometry and bioinformatics tools, which have significantly enhanced our ability to study and understand the proteome in unprecedented detail. These innovations have enabled the high-throughput analysis of protein structures, functions and interactions, making proteomics an indispensable tool in scientific research. 1 It has also emerged as a valuable tool in understanding pathogenesis and driving drug development.

Recently, several studies have explored proteomic analyses in patient samples from antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), a systemic autoimmune disease characterised by arterial, venous or microvascular thrombosis, pregnancy morbidity or non-thrombotic manifestations in patients with persistent antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL). 2

Despite growing insights into APS pathogenesis, secondary prevention still relies on long-term vitamin K antagonists (VKAs). 3 Recurrent thrombosis remains common, with an annual risk of 2–5% under VKA therapy. 4 A meta-analysis reported 2-year recurrence rates of 5% for venous and 23.6% for arterial events, 4 while a 10-year multicentre cohort documented 9.3% mortality, with severe thrombosis accounting for 36.5% of deaths. 5

Current management is therefore restricted to anticoagulation, which does not prevent all new events and lacks targeted or personalised options. Importantly, APS should not be regarded solely as a thrombotic disorder, but as a systemic autoimmune condition. Although aPL-related and non-aPL thrombosis may share overlapping features, thrombotic events in APS characteristically occur at atypical sites, recur with high frequency and are often accompanied by non-thrombotic manifestations driven by immune dysregulation rather than by a purely haemostatic imbalance. 6

Novel therapies are under investigation, targeting immune system (chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells, tumour necrosis factor-alpha blockers, daratumumab, belimumab) and pathogenic mechanisms such as NETosis (defibrotide, dipyridamole). 6 Consequently, APS should be approached as an autoimmune disease, with a focus on identifying features that distinguish thrombotic APS (tAPS) from non-aPL-related thrombosis to improve treatment strategies.

To date, however, no studies have directly compared the proteomic profiles of APS and non-aPL-related thrombosis. The aim of this systematic literature review (SLR) is therefore to compare upregulated proteins in tAPS and in non-aPL-related thrombosis, with the goal of providing insights into disease pathogenesis and identifying potential therapeutic targets.

## Methods

We conducted two SLRs in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines, 7 focusing on proteomics in tAPS and non-aPL-related thrombosis. Non-aPL-related thrombosis was defined as macrovascular arterial or venous deep thrombosis resulting from atherosclerotic or thromboembolic events in patients without autoimmune diseases. Thrombotic events associated with vaccination; infections were excluded to ensure a homogeneous comparison.

Research strategies are reported in online supplemental material S1 . For quality and risk of bias assessment, we integrated key elements from the Joanna Briggs Institute and the Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research, as well as the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme; details are reported in online supplemental material S2 .

### Study selection

Two independent investigators (SM and CC) searched MEDLINE and Embase for studies published up to 31 October 2024. Grey literature was not assessed. After excluding duplicates, the extracted studies were initially selected by screening the titles and abstracts. Subsequently, a comprehensive review of the full texts was performed. Any disagreements were resolved through consensus.

### Inclusion criteria

Eligible studies included observational controlled research on biological samples (eg, sera, plasma, urine, fibrin clots, cells), as well as observational research on thrombi, all published in English. We excluded case reports, brief communications, conference abstracts, study protocols and articles without original data.

We excluded studies from obstetric APS, as its pathogenesis differs from tAPS. We also excluded studies on secondary APS.

Thrombosis associated with vaccines, infections or neoplasms was also excluded to ensure a control group unaffected by distinct pathogenic mechanisms. These conditions are additionally known to trigger autoantibody production or autoimmune diseases. We excluded studies on microvascular thrombosis or superficial thrombosis.

We considered as acceptable those studies that assessed protein activity using validated biochemical assays.

### Data extraction

Two independent researchers (SM and CC) identified the upregulated proteins and tools used for the proteomic analysis. We obtained the gene name and protein function by UniProt.

### Statistical analysis

Upregulated proteins were used as input for gene cluster analysis, followed by gene enrichment analysis using Gene Ontology (GO). The functional enrichment analysis was run selecting false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05 in features GO: Molecular Function, GO: Biological Process, GO: Cellular Compartment.

We reported the results as strength , calculated as:

Strength=log10 (observed proportion/expected proportion).

Observed proportion=fraction of your proteins in the network that fall under that GO term.

Expected proportion=fraction of proteins in the whole background database with that GO term.

Network analysis was conducted with Cytoscape software (V.3.8.2) and the stringAPP plugin (V.2.0.3).

## Results

The SLR selection process is illustrated in figure 1 . Out of 108 and 209 articles found in electronic database searches, seven and 13 were included in the review for tAPS and non-aPL-related thrombosis, respectively.

The tAPS SLR included proteins identified from plasma (one study), platelets (one study), monocytes (two studies), fibrin clots (one study), urine (one study) and neutrophil extracellular traps (one study).

The non-aPL-related thrombosis SLR included proteins from plasma (two studies), platelets (two studies), fibrin clots (two studies) and cerebrospinal fluid (one study), as well as five studies on stroke thrombi and one study on acute myocardial infarction thrombi. The main characteristics of the studies included in the SLR on tAPS are summarised in online supplemental material S3 , whereas those for non-aPL-related thrombosis are detailed in online supplemental material S4 .

Proteomics data obtained from studies on non-aPL-related thrombi ( online supplemental material S3 ) were excluded from the GO enrichment analysis due to a lack of equivalent studies on tAPS.

A total of 118 proteins were upregulated in patients with tAPS, whereas 319 were upregulated in non-aPL-related thrombosis. In both groups, proteins functionally interact with each other ( figure 2 ).

Cellular component enrichment revealed that the upregulated proteins in tAPS originate from blood microparticle, membrane attack complex (MAC) and vesicle lumen. Similarly, the upregulated proteins in non-aPL-related thrombosis originate from blood microparticle, vesicle lumen and secretory granules ( figure 3 ).

Notably, the top three enriched biological process terms among the upregulated proteins in tAPS were platelet aggregation (strength=1.69, FDR=1.08×10 −14 ), platelet activation (strength=1.39, FDR=4.44×10 −14 ) and blood coagulation (strength=1.24, FDR=1.08×10 −14 ). This corresponds to enrichment levels of approximately 49-fold (10ˆ1.69≈48.98) for platelet aggregation, 25-fold (10ˆ1.39≈24.55) for platelet activation and 17-fold (10ˆ1.24≈17.37) for blood coagulation compared with expectation.

In contrast, in non-aPL-related thrombosis, the upregulated proteins were most strongly associated with haemostasis (strength=1.18, FDR=1.76×10 −31 ), blood coagulation (strength=1.19, FDR=6.01×10 −31 ) and wound healing (strength=1.35, FDR=4.61×10 −18 ) ( figure 3 ) ( online supplemental material S5 ).

The molecular functions of upregulated proteins in tAPS involve receptor or protein complex binding, while in non-aPL-related thrombosis, they focus on enzymatic activity ( online supplemental material S5 ).

## Discussion

In the present study, we conducted two SLRs to identify upregulated proteins in patients with tAPS and non-aPL-related thrombosis.

To gain a deeper understanding of the biological relevance of these proteins, we employed GO enrichment analysis, which allowed us to identify and interpret the most significant biological functions associated with upregulated proteins in both tAPS and non-aPL-related thrombosis. This analysis allowed for a comparative evaluation of the underlying mechanisms in these conditions.

In patients with tAPS, the 118 proteins found to be upregulated are mainly involved in platelet aggregation and activation, suggesting their prominent role in platelet functioning. Thus, while these biological processes represent the two highly ranked categories in tAPS, they only appear in seventh place in non-aPL-related thrombosis. Of note, in patients with tAPS, the enhanced expression of proteins involved in platelet functions is present even at a distance from the acute phase.

Considering that samples for proteomic studies were not systematically collected in the acute phase of thrombosis, it is possible to hypothesise that the greater involvement of platelet function detected in tAPS is linked to a role of platelets that goes beyond the thrombotic event alone.

The prominent role of platelet dysfunction in tAPS pathogenesis emerged soon after the first APS definition. It has been demonstrated that aPL bind platelet membrane phospholipids, causing their activation, aggregation and eventually thrombosis. 8

Fanelli and colleagues revealed that circulating activated platelets can be detected in almost all patients with APS. 9 aPL can activate platelets by stimulating several receptors on the platelet surface, including αIIbβ3, 10 ApoER2, 11 12 GPIbα 11 13 and Fcγ-receptor IIa. 14

Interestingly, β2GPI has also been shown to prevent the binding of the von Willebrand factor A1 (VWF A1) domain to the GPIbα receptor on platelets, thereby inhibiting platelet aggregation. 15 Furthermore, aβ2GPI was found to suppress ADAMTS-13 activity, thereby limiting the degradation of ultra-large VWF in vivo, eventually increasing the risk of thrombosis. 16

In previous studies, our group showed that aβ2GPI can trigger an in vitro signal transduction pathway in human platelets, which causes Interleukin-1 Receptor-Associated Kinase (IRAK) phosphorylation, nuclear factor kappa-B activation and the subsequent expression of tissue factor. 17

Also, in a mouse model of APS, platelets are recruited into the injured endothelium, where they contribute to fibrin generation within the thrombus. 18 It has been observed that circulating platelet-leucocyte aggregates are elevated in these patients. 19

Recently, as further proof of the importance of platelet dysfunction in APS, thrombocytopenia was included in the latest classification criteria. 2 It is estimated that 36% of patients with APS display thrombocytopenia, which has been found to be linked to a higher thrombotic risk. Furthermore, persistent low to moderate thrombocytopenia is associated with reduced long-term survival. 20

Another systemic autoimmune disease associated with an elevated risk of thrombosis is SLE, although aPL alone only partially accounts for this risk. An SLR highlighted the central role of platelets in mediating thrombosis in patients with SLE, both with and without aPL. Indeed, aPL and other autoantibodies—including anti-dsDNA, immune complexes and immunoglobulin G—can directly activate platelets via receptors such as FcγRIIA and CD40/CD40L, priming them for aggregation and thrombus formation. Chronic or repeated activation may give rise to a hyper-reactive or ‘exhausted’ platelet phenotype, characterised by enhanced phosphorylation of procoagulant proteins such as fibrinogen, altered platelet size and changes in activation markers—all of which correlate with increased thrombotic risk. These findings underscore that platelets serve as central effectors in immune-mediated thrombosis. 21

Although several studies highlight the relevance of platelets in the pathogenesis of tAPS, the most studied antiplatelet therapy, low-dose aspirin, has shown limited effectiveness in preventing recurrent thrombosis in patients with arterial events. However, these findings are based on small sample sizes, retrospective studies and incomplete data regarding outcomes and follow-up. 22 23 Additionally, there is limited knowledge about the efficacy of other antiplatelet agents.

Dipyridamole is an antiplatelet drug that acts as an indirect adenosine A₂A receptor agonist by blocking adenosine reuptake and sustaining extracellular adenosine levels. A proposed regulatory mechanism of NETosis involves adenosine A₂A receptors on the surface of neutrophils, which act as a brake on inflammation. A 2019 mechanistic study using a mouse model of aPL-mediated thrombosis demonstrated that dipyridamole suppressed aPL-induced NETosis via the A₂A receptor and, in turn, mitigated venous thrombosis. 6

Our analysis of the top enriched terms and their associated proteins revealed strikingly divergent functional patterns between tAPS and non-aPL-related thrombosis. In tAPS, upregulated proteins were strongly enriched for MAC, pointing to a central role for complement activation in driving endothelial injury and subsequent thrombus formation. By contrast, the proteomic landscape of non-aPL-related thrombosis was dominated by upregulation of serpins, reflecting enhanced protease inhibition and regulatory processes within the coagulation and fibrinolytic cascades. This pattern suggests a predominance of dysregulated proteolytic activity and compensatory inhibitory mechanisms as central to thrombus biology in the absence of aPL. These findings highlight that thrombosis in APS and non-aPL settings is shaped by fundamentally different underlying biological mechanisms.

The limitations of this work include the relatively small number of available proteomic studies, the lack of standardised protocols for sample collection and proteomic analysis. An additional methodological consideration is the exclusion of proteomic data from non-aPL-related thrombi in the GO enrichment analysis, due to the absence of comparable studies in tAPS. We recognise that this approach may not be fully comprehensive and could fail to capture all molecular differences between thrombotic pathways in tAPS and non-aPL-related thrombosis. Acknowledging the limited availability of studies on well-defined non-aPL-related thrombosis subgroups, our analysis may be influenced by heterogeneity; to reduce variability, we excluded superficial or microvascular thrombosis, as well as thrombosis associated with neoplasms, vaccines or infections.

In conclusion, these results should encourage studies on treatments targeting platelets. The platelet receptors involved in aPL-induced activation could lead to a variety of treatment possibilities, as well as receptors involved in the formation of platelet-leucocyte aggregates.
