# Systemic Lupus Erythematosus With Catastrophic APS and Libman–Sacks Endocarditis in a Patient With Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia: A Case Report

## Abstract

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) can lead to antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) and catastrophic APS (CAPS), causing severe thrombotic events. This case of autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) with stroke and Libman–Sacks endocarditis highlights the need for APS evaluation and shows that prompt anticoagulation and immunosuppressive therapy are crucial in such cases.

## Full Text

## Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by widespread immune dysregulation, leading to inflammation and damage in multiple organ systems. Given its heterogeneity, the diagnosis of SLE relies on a combination of clinical features and laboratory findings, including autoantibody profiles, complement levels, and inflammatory markers [ 1 ]. Among the serious complications associated with SLE is antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), an autoimmune thrombotic disorder characterized by recurrent arterial and venous thrombosis, pregnancy morbidity, and the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL). APS is a complex disorder that can manifest through a wide range of clinical symptoms [ 2 ].

Catastrophic APS (CAPS) is a rare and life‐threatening form of APS, occurring in approximately 1% of APS patients, with a mortality rate of nearly 50%. The diagnostic criteria for this syndrome include the simultaneous or rapidly involving (within less than a week) of three or more organs, systems, or tissues. Additionally, laboratory tests must confirm the presence of aPL, such as lupus anticoagulant (LA), anticardiolipin antibodies, and/or anti‐beta2‐glycoprotein‐I antibodies [ 2 ]. Some cases of CAPS may evolve over a longer period, potentially up to a month, while still meeting the criteria for CAPS because of the rapid and diffuse nature of the thrombotic events and the involvement of multiple organ systems [ 3 , 4 ].

Another hematologic complication frequently observed in SLE is autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA), an immune‐mediated destruction of red blood cells, often confirmed by a positive direct Coombs test. AIHA, and in particular Coombs‐positive AIHA, has been linked to an increased risk of ischemic stroke, further complicating disease prognosis [ 5 ]. In this report, we present a rare and challenging case of SLE complicated by CAPS in the background of AIHA, illustrating the intricate interplay between these autoimmune conditions.

## Case History

A 50‐year‐old male with a history of controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease (CKD) stage G3b, and Coombs‐positive AIHA (a history of diagnosis dating back 2.5 years) was undergoing treatment with biweekly rituximab (600 mg). He received six cycles, with the last dose administered 1 week ago, along with tapering oral prednisolone (1 mg/kg, tapered over several weeks).

He presented with fluctuating and recurrent symptoms of blurred vision, cognitive impairment, memory deficits, and balance disturbances (gait ataxia) starting about 2 weeks ago, suggestive of recurrent ischemic strokes. At the beginning of the patient's admission to the hospital, a thorough and complete neurological examination was done. In the evaluation of cognitive function, the patient was oriented but had some recent memory problems. Also, in the field of language exam the patient had some difficulty in complex‐sentence‐making and word finding in his phrases. In the examination of cranial nerves and visual field, the only positive point was bilateral incomplete incongruous homonymous hemianopia, which was more significant in the right side visual field.

The examination of the equilibrium system showed some degree of tandem gait (truncal) ataxia.

## Investigations and Treatment

Laboratory tests indicated anemia (Hb = 10.4 g/dL), thrombocytopenia (Plt ≈ 138,000/μL), elevated bilirubin (total = 4.34 mg/dL, direct = 0.34 mg/dL), and raised lactate dehydrogenase (LDH = 633 U/L), with a high reticulocyte count (4.9%), suggesting ongoing hemolysis. A hematology consultation was conducted for the patient, and based on the peripheral blood smear (PBS), there was no evidence of active hemolysis reported. Inflammatory markers revealed an elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 63 mm/h and serum ferritin of 405 ng/mL, while C‐reactive protein (CRP) was normal (1.3 mg/L). Both direct and indirect Coombs tests were positive. A follow‐up complete blood count (CBC) 3 days later showed worsening anemia (Hb = 9.6 g/dL, Plt ≈ 138,000/μL, WBC = 4000/μL) (Table 1 ).

According to the patient's creatinine (1.95 mg/dL), the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was approximately 44 mL/min/1.73 m 2 . Urinary tract sonography demonstrated mildly increased cortical echogenicity, prominent corticomedullary differentiation, and kidney sizes at the lower limit of normal (Table 1 ). No evidence in favor of diabetic retinopathy was reported in the ophthalmological consultation.

Brain MRI revealed acute ischemic infarcts in the right posterior temporal and bilateral upper parietal regions (Figures 1 and 2 ), along with a subacute or chronic left occipital infarct (Figure 3 ), showing brain ischemic (thromboembolic) changes at different times (during the recent month, according to the time of the patient's symptom onset). Ultrasound color Doppler of cervical vessels and brain magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) showed no significant stenosis in brain or cervical vessels. Specialized echocardiography and transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) revealed hypodense masses on the mitral valve—6.6 mm on the anterior leaflet and 6.5 mm on the posterior leaflet—causing moderate, highly eccentric, anteriorly directed mitral regurgitation (MR). These findings raised suspicion for vegetation, thrombus, or Libman–Sacks endocarditis. However, repeated blood cultures on enriched media remained negative, and inflammatory markers (CRP = 3.6 mg/L, procalcitonin = 0.1 ng/mL) did not suggest an active infectious process (Table 1 ). The patient's stable general condition further reduced the likelihood of a severe infection.

Given the involvement of multiple major organs (hematologic, cardiac, and central nervous systems), further rheumatologic testing was performed. The results revealed high titers of aPL including anticardiolipin and lupus anticoagulant antibody, decreased complement levels (C3, C4, CH50), and positive anti‐double stranded DNA (anti‐dsDNA) antibody (Table 1 ). The diagnosis of SLE with CAPS was made by a rheumatology consult on the tenth day of hospitalization, when the results of TEE and serologic tests for rheumatologic and autoimmune disorders were ready and serial blood culture was negative for any microbial growth.

The patient was initially managed with a continuous heparin infusion (1000 U/h IV) and broad‐spectrum antibiotics until infectious causes were ruled out. Following establishment of SLE with CAPS, antibiotic therapy was discontinued and treatment with intravenous methylprednisolone pulses (1 g/day for 3 days) was initiated. The treatment was continued with maintenance oral prednisolone (1 mg/kg) and intravenous cyclophosphamide (750 mg, repeated monthly), while anticoagulating with warfarin (target INR: 2.5–3.5).

The patient was discharged in a stable condition with relative improvement in neurological symptoms, including cognitive impairment and ataxia. At early follow‐up visits, residual symptoms included mild ataxia, recent memory impairment, and visual field defects. To improve memory and cognitive function, memantine 10 mg daily was administered as an off‐label treatment [ 6 ]. Symptoms of memory impairment and ataxia improved significantly in the weeks and months following discharge, but incomplete right hemianopia persisted. In the last transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) performed 4 months after discharge, a reduction in the size of the mitral valve vegetation to 2 × 3 mm was reported. Although the patient had retired early from his job, 9 months after discharge he was able to manage his socioeconomic affairs with some help from his family and close relatives.

## Discussion

The onset of APS with hemolytic anemia has been reported in children, and hematologic disorders are known to be the most frequently associated non‐thrombotic manifestation of APS in this age group [ 7 ]. On the other hand, AIHA contributes to hypercoagulability through increased erythrocyte destruction, oxidative stress, and vascular endothelial injury, which can further exacerbate the thrombotic burden in APS patients [ 5 ]. In our case, although there was no evidence of active hemolysis in the PBS (absence of schistocytes), the elevated LDH and bilirubin (primarily total) levels, along with an increased reticulocyte count and worsening anemia in follow‐up CBC, indicated ongoing hemolysis.

The patient had been diagnosed with AIHA approximately two and a half years prior. The chronic and refractory nature of the disease, despite rituximab therapy, suggests a possible underlying SLE. An autoimmune etiology for CKD is also plausible, considering the patient's well‐controlled diabetes, absence of diabetic retinopathy, and kidney sizes at the lower limit of normal. Although a renal biopsy could not be performed because of lack of patient consent, the overall clinical picture supports an autoimmune mechanism as a unifying explanation for both the hematologic and renal manifestations. It is conceivable that earlier and more comprehensive evaluation for autoimmune disorders might have enabled timely intervention and potentially prevented the subsequent severe cardiac and cerebrovascular thromboembolic complications.

This case underscores the intricate interplay between SLE, CAPS, and AIHA, highlighting the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges associated with these overlapping autoimmune conditions. The patient presented with recurrent ischemic strokes and signs of hemolysis, which, in the context of a positive aPL profile and systemic inflammation, strongly suggested CAPS. The presence of mitral valve masses further raised suspicion for Libman–Sacks endocarditis, a well‐documented but often underdiagnosed manifestation of APS and SLE [ 8 ]. CAPS, a rare but life‐threatening form of APS, is characterized by widespread thrombosis leading to multi‐organ failure [ 9 ]. The pathophysiology involves a hypercoagulable state driven by aPL, endothelial dysfunction, complement activation, and a pro‐inflammatory cytokine surge. In this patient, the combination of CAPS and AIHA likely created a vicious cycle of thromboinflammation, increasing the risk of ischemic events.

The patient's echocardiographic findings revealed mitral valve masses suggestive of Libman–Sacks endocarditis, a form of sterile endocarditis commonly associated with SLE and APS. Unlike infective endocarditis, Libman–Sacks endocarditis is nonbacterial and results from immune complex deposition and fibrin formation. Differentiating between the two is critical, as inappropriate antibiotic use can delay necessary immunosuppressive and anticoagulant therapy. In this case, the negative blood cultures and absence of systemic infection markers supported the diagnosis of Libman–Sacks endocarditis over an infectious etiology.

The treatment of CAPS requires an aggressive multimodal approach, including anticoagulation, high‐dose corticosteroids, immunosuppressive agents (such as cyclophosphamide), and, in refractory cases, plasma exchange or intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Early initiation of therapeutic anticoagulation with heparin, followed by warfarin, was crucial in preventing further thrombotic complications. Additionally, the use of high‐dose intravenous methylprednisolone and cyclophosphamide aimed to control the underlying autoimmune activity. The role of rituximab in AIHA management is well established, particularly in refractory cases [ 10 ]. Our patient had previously received multiple cycles of rituximab, suggesting ongoing autoimmune activity despite treatment. This raises questions about optimal long‐term immunosuppression strategies in patients with overlapping SLE, APS, and AIHA.

Despite the high mortality rate associated with CAPS, early recognition and prompt initiation of appropriate therapy can significantly improve patient outcomes. This case highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for APS and CAPS in patients with AIHA and unexplained thrombotic events. It also emphasizes the need for a multidisciplinary approach, involving hematology, rheumatology, and cardiology teams, to ensure optimal management of such complex autoimmune syndromes.

## Conclusion

This case illustrates a rare and severe presentation of SLE complicated by CAPS and AIHA, resulting in recurrent ischemic strokes and Libman–Sacks endocarditis. The coexistence of these conditions underscores the critical need for thorough autoimmune and thrombotic workups in patients with unexplained hemolysis and ischemic events. Early recognition, timely intervention with anticoagulation, immunosuppression, and close monitoring are essential to improving prognosis in such high‐risk cases.

Ultimately, this case reinforces the importance of evaluating APS in patients with AIHA, as well as the need for heightened clinical vigilance in detecting catastrophic thrombotic complications in SLE. A multidisciplinary approach and evidence‐based therapeutic strategies remain pivotal in managing these challenging and life‐threatening autoimmune conditions.
