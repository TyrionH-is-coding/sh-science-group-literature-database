# Abstract

Objectives: To investigate the prevalence, clinical features, histological spectrum and outcomes of intra-renal involvement in patients with primary APS (PAPS), with particular attention to both aPL nephropathy (aPL-N) and non-aPL-N renal lesions.

Methods: This multicentric retrospective case-control study included 258 PAPS patients followed between 1984 and 2023 at two Italian referral centres. Patients with a biopsy-proven intra-renal involvement $(n = 17)$ were compared with those without renal manifestations. Histologic classification followed the updated aPL-N criteria. Clinical, laboratory, therapeutic features, and 12-month renal responses were assessed.

Results: Seventeen of 258 (7%) patients underwent renal biopsy. Two out of 17 (12%) presented with acute kidney injury, 5/17 (29%) with nephrotic syndrome and 5/17 (29%) had isolated urinary abnormalities; the remaining 5 patients presented with different combination of signs/ symptoms. Six (2.3%) were diagnosed with aPL-N, while 11 (4.2%) exhibited alternative glomerular lesions (e.g. membranous nephropathy, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis). Compared with controls, patients with intra-renal involvement had higher frequencies of CAPS (18% vs 0.4%, $P = 0.001$ ), thrombocytopenia (35% vs 10%, $P = 0.006$ ), epilepsy (24% vs 5%, $P = 0.018$ ) and LA positivity (100% vs 62%, $P = 0.002$ ). At 12 months, partial renal response was observed in most cases, with a worse prognosis in those with aPL-N despite anti-thrombotic treatment. All non-aPL-N patients had aPL-related events, including thrombotic, obstetric and non-thrombotic manifestations, with non-aPL-N renal disease preceding these events in 45%.

Conclusion: Intra-renal involvement in PAPS is rare but heterogeneous, encompassing both vascular and glomerular pathologies. These findings support the need for heightened renal surveillance in PAPS, even in the absence of classic thrombotic manifestations, and highlight the importance of histologic assessment to guide tailored therapy. From a nephrologist's perspective, routine screening for aPL in glomerular disease assessment may be relevant, particularly by providing prognostic value for subsequent aPL-related events.

Keywords: primary antiphospholipid syndrome, aPL nephropathy, glomerulonephritis, thrombotic microangiopathy, membranous nephropathy, renal biopsy, antiphospholipid syndrome, CAPS, autoimmune kidney disease.

# Rheumatology key messages

- Renal assessment and, when necessary, biopsy are crucial for aPL-positive patients with persistent urinary abnormalities.   
- aPL nephropathy may require treatment beyond anticoagulation, as part of microvascular APS subset.   
- aPL screening may be relevant in glomerulopathies when vascular lesions or autoimmune features coexist.

# Introduction

The antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a systemic autoimmune disease characterized by episodes of venous and/or arterial thrombosis and/or pregnancy morbidity associated with the persistent positivity of circulating autoantibodies

targeting phospholipid-binding proteins, the so-called anti-phospholipid antibodies (aPL) [1]. A rarer variant of APS (observed in $< 1\%$ of cases of APS), known as catastrophic APS (CAPS), is characterized by systemic thrombotic microangiopathy and it is defined as thrombosis in three or more

<!-- page 2 -->
organs occurring in $< 1$ week and with microvascular involvement [2]. When not associated with other CTDs, APS is defined as primary (PAPS) [3]. At renal level, aPL can potentially affect any part of the vasculature, such as renal arteries and veins, intrarenal arteries and arterioles and glomerular capillaries [4, 5]. In the early 1990s, the first description of intrarenal vascular damage was provided in five PAPS patients [6]. All the patients were hypertensive with a variable degree of proteinuria and kidney dysfunction. Their renal histological features suggested thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), while the typical features of lupus nephritis (LN) were not found. Since then, the term aPL nephropathy (aPL-N) has been coined [7] and, recently, the aPL-N definition was updated by the Renal Pathology Subcommittee, a subgroup of the APS Classification Criteria 2023 Steering Committee [8]. aPL-N includes the presence of acute/early lesions exhibiting features of TMA, with frequent fibrin thrombi in glomeruli and arterioles, and chronic/late lesions that display fibrous intimal hyperplasia (FIH) with luminal obliteration and organized thrombi, with or without recanalization, fibrous arterial or arteriolar occlusion and focal cortical atrophy (FCA). If other causes of renal TMA are excluded, the presence of at least one acute or chronic lesion is defined as aPL-N [8].

In addition to aPL-N lesions, an expanding spectrum of renal lesions (e.g. glomerulonephritis, GN) have been anecdotally reported in PAPS patients. In 2003, Fakhouri and coworkers [9] identified, among kidney biopsies performed in 29 PAPS patients, 9 cases $(31\%)$ with predominant pathological features distinct from vascular aPL-N: 3 membranous nephropathy (MN), 3 minimal change nephropathy (MCD)/focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), 2 cases of C3 nephropathy and 1 pauci-immune crescentic GN. In the following years, other additional case reports described various glomerular conditions in PAPS patients, even without evidence of aPL-related renal vascular lesions [9-18]. These findings are summarized in Table 1.

Nonetheless, it is worth noting that original data regarding intra-renal involvement in PAPS patients are scarce and heterogeneous in existing literature.

The objective of the present study is to evaluate potential clinical and laboratory predictors of intra-renal involvement in PAPS patients, by conducting a case-control study comparing PAPS patients with and without intra-renal

involvement. Secondly, we aimed to clinically and histologically characterize PAPS patients with (i) aPL-N and (ii) nonaPL-N intra-renal involvement.

# Methods

This was an observational retrospective multicentric study considering PAPS patients followed in two Italian Centers (Rheumatology and Clinical Immunology Unit and Nephrology Unit, University Hospital of Brescia and Center of Excellence on Nephrology, Rheumatology and Rare Diseases, University Hospital of Turin) with long-term experience in APS patient management.

# Inclusion criteria and selection of patients

All adult patients $(\geq 18$ years) with PAPS fulfilling the 2006 Sapporo updated and/or 2023 ACR/EULAR classification criteria [19, 20], attending the Units between 1984 and 2023, with at least one follow-up visit and at least 12 months of follow-up were considered for the present study. At the time of renal disease, none of the included patients met the classification criteria for SLE or other CTDs.

# Case-control study

Cases were represented by PAPS patients presenting with renal signs and/or symptoms such as acute kidney injury (AKI), nephritic syndrome, nephrotic syndrome, non-nephrotic proteinuria, haematuria, urinary abnormalities, renal insufficiency and renal arterial hypertension, who underwent renal biopsy with available histological data. Controls included PAPS patients who were not presenting with renal signs and/ or symptoms neither at disease onset nor during the disease course (Fig. 1). Patients exhibiting signs of renal involvement who did not undergo a kidney biopsy were excluded.

# aGAPSS score

To stratify the cardiovascular risk, the adjusted Global Anti-Phospholipid Syndrome Score (aGAPSS), a system based on a quantitative score and including a combination of two classic cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension and dyslipidaemia) and three aPL tests [LA, aCL and anti-beta2glicoprotein I antibodies (aβ2GPI)], was used [21].

# Renal histologic data collection and kidney outcomes assessment

The main histological diagnosis was recorded, retrieving it from the biopsy report done by the expert pathologists at each centre. The aPL-N-related injuries collected were divided into acute and chronic lesions according to the recently published "efforts to better characterize aPL-N" [8]. Additionally, glomerular basement membrane (GBM) reduplication and/or wrinkling was collected, as these lesions are the results of endothelial damage consequent to repeated ischaemic injuries [22]. Kidney outcomes were assessed at 12 months, according to Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines [23]; for TMA the renal response (RR) to therapy was described according to KDIGO guidelines for LN, because no specific consensus exists for assessing the RR specifically in patients with aPL-N [24]. Estimated glomerular filtration rate was assessed in all the patients with the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation.

# Subgroup analysis

Patients with intra-renal involvement were separately analysed according to the main renal histologic findings: (i) aPL-N lesions and (ii) non-aPL-N lesions.

# Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as median and interquartile range and were compared using non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney or Wilcoxon test, as appropriate). Categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages) and were compared using contingency tables; $P$ -value was calculated with $\chi^2$ or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Odds ratios were calculated to assess the risk of the presence of each variable, with Haldane's correction, when needed.

# Ethical statement

Ethics approval was obtained from the local ethics committees (NP 5930), and patients included in the database gave their written informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with Helsinki Declaration principles.

# Results

Among the 333 PAPS patients followed in the two centres between 1984 and 2023, 258 met the inclusion and exclusion criteria for this study (Fig. 1). Their demographic and clinical data are reported in Table 2. Median follow-up of the entire cohort was 13 (7-21) years. Forty-three patients out of 258 $(17\%)$ were lost to follow-up after a median time of 8.5 years [5-19], so for these patients data on possibly evolution toward a definite CTD or death are not available. In 7 out of 215 $(3\%)$ patients with available information, an evolution toward a full-blown CTD was observed after a median of 18 (9-23) years of PAPS duration: 1 patient developed SSc (anti-topoisomerase 1 positive, with limited cutaneous involvement and interstitial lung disease) and 6 patients developed SLE (with renal involvement in 5/6 cases: 2 class V and 3 proliferative LN, class III/IV); 17/215 $(8\%)$ patients died at a median age of 72 (62-78) years after a median disease duration of 14 (11-18) years.

In 10 of these 17 patients $(59\%)$ , renal disease represented the first clinical manifestation that led to medical evaluation, although in 7/17 $(41\%)$ patients other aPL-related events had already occurred [median APS duration of 8 (4-23) years]. # Case-control study

When compared with patients free of intra-renal involvement (Table 2), patients displaying renal disease suffered more frequently of CAPS $18\%$ vs $0.4\%$ , $P: 0.001$ and less commonly experienced thrombotic events involving macrovascular districts $35\%$ vs $69\%$ , $P: 0.004$ ; furthermore, they presented higher rates of thrombocytopenia (proportion of patients with platelets $< 100000 / \mu \mathrm{L}$ ; $35\%$ vs $10\%$ , $P: 0.006$ ) and epilepsy $24\%$ vs $5\%$ , $P: 0.018$ ). In terms of aPL serology, subjects with intra-renal involvement showed LA positivity in all the cases $100\%$ vs $62\%$ , $P: 0.002$ with

only two cases showing single aPL profile; more than half of the cases (9/15, $60\%$ ) displayed a triple aPL profile. # (ii) Patients with non aPL-N lesions

PAPS patients with intra-renal non-aPL-N involvement represented the majority (11/17, $65\%$ ) of the PAPS patients with intra-renal involvement that underwent kidney biopsy. In almost half (5/11, $45\%$ ) of the cases the renal disease preceded other aPL-related manifestations. As renal presentation, they presented with IUAs in 6/11 ( $55\%$ ) cases and nephrotic syndrome in 5/11 ( $45\%$ ) cases. As compared with patients with aPL-N, they presented more frequently normal sCr levels [0.98 (0.6-1.2) vs 1.9 (1.2-2.0) mg/dL, $P: 0.095]$ and higher

24-h-proteinuria levels [3.5 (1.9-3.9) vs 0.5 (0.4-0.5) g/day, P: 0.003] (Supplementary Fig. S1 available at Rheumatology online); no differences regarding aPL profile and ANA, antiENA and complement fraction levels were detected between the two groups (Supplementary Table S1 available at Rheumatology online). The most frequent histologic finding was MN (6/11, $55\%$ )—considered secondary in 5/6 cases, mainly due to C1q deposition detection on immunofluorescence—followed by FSGS in 4/11 cases $(36\%)$ and MCD in 1/11 cases $(9\%)$ . Five of 11 $(45\%)$ patients were treated with immunosuppressive treatment (1 FSGS with rituximab, 1 primary MN with rituximab and cyclosporin A, 1 secondary MN with mycophenolate, 2 secondary MN with cyclophosphamide) and all of them were on anticoagulation $(4/11, 36\%)$ or antiplatelet agents $(7/11, 64\%)$ . The 12-month RR was partial in $5/6$ $(83\%)$ and absent in $1/6$ $(17\%)$ cases.

# Discussion

This is one of the widest studies investigating, in a retrospective way, the prevalence, clinical presentation and course of intra-renal involvement in a longstanding cohort of PAPS patients. We tried to minimize selection bias of patients in our study by enrolling patients from both nephrology and rheumatology/immunology units of two large Italian university hospitals.

<!-- page 7 -->
The latest definition of aPL-N, which was developed to include this aPL-related manifestation into the new ACR/EULAR classification criteria 2023 for APS, refers just to vascular lesions (both acute and/or chronic, both glomerular and/or arteriolar) [20, 25]. However, we decided to include in this study also PAPS patients with other intra-renal/glomerular lesions, if other causes—possibly determining it—were ruled out. Indeed, from the 1990s, various (tens) cases of different glomerular involvement were reported in the presence of aPL positivity (see Table 1) [9-18, 26].

Intra-renal disease was histologically confirmed in $7\%$ $(n = 17)$ of our PAPS patients cohort. This group of patients suffered less frequently of macrovascular thrombotic events and presented higher rates of non-thrombotic manifestations such as thrombocytopenia and seizures, along with higher aGAPSS. One could postulate that they represent a unique APS phenotype, distinct from the "classical" thrombotic and obstetric phenotypes, and more similar to the recently described "systemic" APS phenotype [27]. Regarding laboratory tests, all these patients tested positive for LA, in a higher proportion compared with the control group. However, despite the more "systemic" clinical phenotype above described, there were no differences regarding ANA, anti-ENA, anti-dsDNA positivity and complement fractions levels.

In order to provide a more detailed characterization of PAPS patients with intra-renal involvement, we thought it was important to categorize them based on their renal histology results and, in particular, by differentiating them between those with more prominent vascular injuries (aPL-N) and those exhibiting more histologic "immunologic activity" (non aPL-N). Indeed, in a previous report by Sciascia et al. in 2023, the evaluation of APS patients according to their histologic renal injuries, lead to the definition of three distinct clusters (named "thrombotic microangiopathy", "hyperplastic vasculopathy" and "subendothelial oedema") with different clinical manifestations and different renal prognosis [24].

All the six patients with aPL-N were treated with anticoagulation/antiplatelet agents, half received immunosuppressive/ anti-complement therapies, and the two patients with CAPS were treated as recommended [28] also with IVIG. However, only $40\%$ achieved a 12-month complete RR, and half experienced subsequent aPL-related events. These latest findings align with those of Rousselin et al. who analysed long-term renal outcomes in aPL-N patients, and are confirmed by a recent systematic literature review performed by Hoxha et al. [29, 30]. Rousselin found progression to end-stage renal disease within 6 years in $20\%$ of the cases, despite the young median age and ongoing anticoagulation therapy. Rousselin concluded that anticoagulation alone may not be sufficient, and highlighted as $17\%$ of patients also experienced other vascular aPL-related events. This debate on APS treatment, particularly regarding non-thrombotic APS manifestations (such as heart valve disease and thrombocytopenia, now part of the 2023 ACR/EULAR classification criteria) and microvascular involvement (including aPL-N), has gained particular attention in recent years [31, 32]. These APS subsets may have distinct pathogenesis compared with the more "classic" thrombotic APS, and inflammatory pathways are now being explored as potential therapeutic targets [33]. The potential treatments under investigation include statins, HCQ, rituximab, belimumab, eculizumab and sirolimus, but for now evidence-based recommendations for non-thrombotic and thrombotic microvascular

APS management are still lacking due to their rarity and the paucity of high-quality controlled studies [28, 34].

Among PAPS patients with non-aPL-N intra-renal involvement $(n = 11)$ , all had other aPL-related events (46% thrombotic, 83% obstetric, 73% non-thrombotic), that in 55% of the cases followed the renal disease. Most (64%) were triple-positive for aPL. The most common renal histologic patterns were MN (with histologic features of primary form in one case and of secondary form in the others), followed by FSGS/MCD. Over half (64%) exhibited vascular injuries on renal histology, potentially mediated by aPL, such as GBM reduplication and mild arteriolar hyaline plaques. Our findings align with existing literature, where MN and FSGS are the most commonly reported histologic findings in aPL-positive patients, following the definite and specific aPL-N. Case reports have also described pauci-immune vasculitis, proliferative GN, membranous-proliferative GN and C3 GN in APS [9-18, 26]. Studies on aPL prevalence in chronic primary GN, particularly MN, show a 10% positivity rate [35, 36]. Some have speculated on the relationship between aPL and MN development: Levy et al. transferred lymphocytes from a patient with MN and aPL into a severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) mouse, which developed aPL and renal lesions consistent with human MN, supporting aPL's role in inducing this renal damage [37]. However, at that time, the pathogenic antibodies found in ~70% of primary MN cases (such as anti-PLA2R) had not yet been identified, so these data should be interpreted with caution. Some authors, especially in secondary MN, suggested a continuum between PAPS and SLE, and recommend monitoring these patients for SLE risk, especially when C1q deposits are found on histology [9, 10]. However, it remains unclear whether these nephropathies are simply coincidental with aPL or linked to APS via a common pathogenic pathway.

This research is not without limitations, mainly due to the small sample size and retrospective design. We could not assess long-term renal outcomes, as the evaluation was limited to 12 months, or identify predictors of renal involvement in aPL-positive patients, as many presented with it as the initial manifestation. Additionally, 12 PAPS patients with renal signs/symptoms were excluded from the analysis due to lack of histologic data. A further limitation is the exclusion of 43 patients with missing follow-up data: although included in baseline comparisons, their absence from outcome analysis may introduce bias. Some might have developed renal involvement later, but this remains un-assessable due to the retrospective design, potentially leading to an underestimation of the true prevalence.

An important consideration is that aPL-positive patients have traditionally been regarded as at higher risk of postbiopsy complications, due to intrinsic thrombotic risk, ongoing anticoagulation and potential thrombocytopenia. However, in our cohort, and consistent with our previous experience [38], no major biopsy-related complications were observed, with only one case of a minor, self-limiting perirenal haematoma. Given that the prevalence of intra-renal involvement in APS may currently be underestimated, our findings suggest that renal biopsy, when performed under a monitored protocol, is relatively safe and provides important clinical value to guide treatment decisions.

In conclusion, despite the limitations, this study conveys two key messages. First, it underscores the need for rheumatologists and clinical immunologists to conduct proper renal

<!-- page 8 -->
assessments in aPL-positive patients. If urinary alterations are present, even mild ones, and other causes are ruled out, a renal biopsy should be considered. In our cohort, indeed, renal histology in over half of the PAPS patients with intra-renal involvement did not align with aPL-N.

Second, for nephrologists, it suggests considering testing for aPL when evaluating glomerulopathies, by adding aPL tests to the immunologic panel (ANA, anti-dsDNA, complement fractions, ANCA, RF, PLA2R and cryoglobulins) especially in cases of glomerular disease of uncertain aetiology, and particularly when vascular lesions coexist with glomerular changes. Despite the low prevalence $(10\%)$ reported, and the lack of clear pathogenetic significance of aPL in GN aside from aPL-N, knowing aPL status can be prognostically valuable, particularly regarding the risk of developing other aPL-related manifestations, such as macrovascular thrombosis.
