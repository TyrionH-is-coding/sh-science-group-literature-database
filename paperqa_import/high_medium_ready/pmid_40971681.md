# Elevated pre-eclampsia risk in lupus nephritis and antiphospholipid syndrome: a nationwide Spanish registry analysis

## Abstract

Objective: Evaluate the association of APS and SLE, focusing on LN, with risk of pre-eclampsia (PE) and PE with severity criteria (PESC).

Methods: Analysis of pregnant women admitted between 2016–2022, using data from the Spanish Hospital Discharge Database. Factors associated with PE and PESC, including early-onset PE, severe PE, eclampsia and HELLP syndrome were evaluated.

Results: Among 1 973 249 admissions, 1787 patients had SLE (9.1‰), with prior LN in 13.2%. SLE patients experienced higher PE (5.5% vs 2.1%) and PESC (2.1% vs 0.6%) rates; these rose further in SLE women with LN alone (PE: 10.3%, PESC: 4.2%) or with both LN and APS (PE: 22.7%, PESC: 9.1%) . Adjusted analyses showed PE and PESC were each associated with SLE (OR = 1.78, 95% CI 1.39–2.28; OR = 2.17, 95% CI 1.47–3.22), LN (OR = 1.94, 95% CI 1.18–3.19; OR = 2.45, 95% CI 1.18–5.09) and APS (OR = 1.53, 95% CI 1.28–1.84; OR = 1.91, 95% CI 1.44–2.54). Within SLE patients, baseline hypertension (OR = 4.27, 95% CI 2.45–7.44), LN (OR = 2.06, 95% CI 1.26–3.37) and APS (OR = 1.85, 95% CI 1.03–3.32) were linked to PE, while LN alone predicted PESC (OR = 2.88, 95% CI 1.41–5.91).

Conclusion: The risk of PE and PESC that patients with SLE exhibit is further amplified in those with a history of LN or APS. Recognizing SLE, APS and especially LN is crucial for precise risk stratification and tailored management to improve maternal and fetal outcomes.

## Full Text

## Introduction

Pre-eclampsia (PE) is a hypertensive disorder of pregnancy characterized by new-onset hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg) after 20 weeks of gestation in a previously normotensive woman, accompanied by proteinuria or signs of maternal end-organ dysfunction [ 1–3 ]. It is a major cause of maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality, affecting 2–5% of all pregnancies [ 1–5 ]. While most cases of PE are mild and improve after delivery, 5–20% progress to severe forms [ 5 ]. PE accounts for 46 000 maternal deaths and half a million fetal and neonatal deaths annually [ 4 , 5 ].

The pathogenesis of PE is initiated by defective placentation early in gestation. Inadequate physiological conversion of the spiral arteries causes relative placental hypoxia and ischaemia; the stressed placenta, in turn, releases soluble fms-like tyrosine-kinase-1 (sFlt-1) and other anti-angiogenic and pro-inflammatory mediators into the maternal circulation, provoking systemic endothelial dysfunction, hypertension and, ultimately, multi-organ injury [ 3 , 5 , 6 ]. While factors like nulliparity, advanced maternal age, obesity and chronic hypertension are known contributors, autoimmune conditions represent a significant, distinct category of risk [ 1 , 4 , 7–10 ]. Chronic complement activation, NETosis and sustained type-I interferon signalling in these disorders augment oxidative stress and drive further sFlt-1 /vascular endothelial growth factor imbalance, thereby lowering the threshold for clinical PE [ 11 , 12 ]. Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) are prominent examples of autoimmune diseases strongly associated with PE [ 13 ].

In SLE, the risk is markedly elevated, with reported PE rates between 9% and 30% [ 9 , 14 , 15 ]. However, SLE is notably heterogeneous; the specific pathogenic contributions and resulting PE risk vary depending on the clinical phenotype and disease activity [ 12 , 16 ]. The risk of PE development in patients with SLE is related to clinical, immunological and serological activity, to certain manifestations such as renal disease or damage accrual from previous flares or drug toxicity [ 4 , 13 , 14 , 17 ]. Therefore, women with quiescent cutaneous-only SLE carry a far lower risk than those with patent active disease or prior lupus nephritis (LN) [ 9 , 10 , 14 ]. Mechanistically, chronic immune-complex deposition, complement activation and lupus-related endothelial injury promote oxidative stress, increase vascular permeability and disrupt angiogenic pathways, thereby impairing early trophoblast invasion and spiral-artery remodeling [ 6 , 18–21 ].

APS is defined by the presence of pathogenic antiphospholipid antibodies leading to clinical manifestations from micro and macrovascular disease, including venous and arterial thrombosis and obstetric morbidity [ 22 ]. In addition to miscarriage, pre-eclampsia, especially severe or early-onset forms, is of particular importance in APS. This significance is reflected in the weight it carries within the disease classification criteria [ 22 ]. Antibody-mediated endothelial activation and excessive thrombin generation create a hypercoagulable, pro-inflammatory milieu that amplifies placental ischaemia and oxidative stress [ 19 , 23 ]. Trophoblast development abnormalities lead to complications in the first trimester, while in the third trimester, placental dysfunction is primarily responsible for pregnancy complications, including PE and other placental insufficiency related phenomena [ 18 , 19 , 24 , 25 ]. Without treatment, only one in four affected women delivers a healthy neonate, a proportion that rises to 70% with current antithrombotic therapy and an 85% live birthrate [ 9 , 24 ]. These outcomes are more evident when SLE and APS coexist [ 24 ]. Furthermore, APS is characterized by considerable heterogeneity, stemming from the diversity in serological and clinical profiles as well as the co-occurrence of other autoimmune, prothrombotic and/or cardiovascular factors in affected individuals [ 26 , 27 ].

In line with the impact of these autoimmune conditions, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of national hospital admissions in Spain from 2016 to 2022 to examine all pregnancy-related hospitalizations. Our study evaluated the impact of SLE, LN and SLE with APS on PE, as well as on cases of PE with severity criteria (PESC). Our findings aim to enhance clinical understanding and underscore the importance of pre-conceptional management, individualized risk stratification and tailored obstetric monitoring for high-risk populations.

## Methods

We conducted a nationwide descriptive analysis using data from the Spanish National Hospital Discharge Database (SNHDD) to assess the association of SLE, LN and SLE with APS on the risk of developing PE and PESC among hospitalized pregnant patients in Spain from 2016 to 2022. The SNHDD is a publicly accessible registry maintained by the Spanish government, providing comprehensive demographic, epidemiological and clinical data, coded using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10). Previous studies have validated the robustness, reliability and high utility of the SNHDD in estimating the burden of various clinical conditions at a national level [ 7 , 28 ].

## Study population and variables definition

We identified all pregnancy-related hospital admissions recorded in the Spanish National Hospital Discharge Database (SNHDD) from 2016 to 2022, using ICD-10 codes for obstetric conditions (O00-O99). Epidemiological data, including age, ethnic group, length of hospital stay, ICU admission status and in-hospital mortality were available in the database. Medical conditions, such as baseline hypertension (HBP), diabetes mellitus (DM), dyslipidaemia (DL), obesity, chronic kidney disease (CKD), SLE, LN and SLE with APS were identified using ICD-10 codes. Obstetric comorbidities and characteristics of the current pregnancy, including previous adverse obstetric outcomes (PRAOO), use of assisted reproductive techniques (ART), twin pregnancy and primiparity were also recorded. Data on the total number of deliveries, neonatal status (live or deceased), mode of delivery (cesarean section or instrumental delivery) and the prevalence of fetal loss, intrauterine growth restriction-low gestational weight (IUGR-LGW), fetal distress, abruptio placentae, preterm birth and stillbirth during pregnancy were collected. Maternal outcomes, such as stroke, were also extracted from diagnoses and procedures performed during hospitalization.

According to ICD-10 codes and clinical guidelines, PE was defined as new high blood pressure (≥140/90 mmHg) developing after 20 weeks of gestation in a woman who previously had normal blood pressure, accompanied by either protein in the urine or signs of organ damage. As a combined outcome, PESC included early-onset PE, severe PE, eclampsia, or HELLP syndrome, as coded in the SNHDD. These were defined as follows: early-onset PE, diagnosed before 34 weeks’ gestation; severe PE, characterized by severe hypertension (≥160/110 mmHg), thrombocytopenia, impaired liver or renal function, pulmonary oedema, unresponsive headache, or visual disturbances; eclampsia, defined by new-onset seizures without other identifiable causes; or HELLP syndrome, indicated by elevated LDH (≥600 IU/l), elevated liver enzymes (AST and ALT >2 times the upper normal limit) and platelet counts below 100 000 × 10^9/l [ 29 ].

## Statistical analysis

The study assessed epidemiological and demographic characteristics, medical and obstetric comorbidities, and the prevalence of PE and PESC in the Spanish general population and in women with SLE. To enable a more granular analysis of the prevalence of PE, PESC, their risk factors and related outcomes among SLE patients, these patients were stratified into five groups: all SLE patients; SLE patients without LN and APS (SLE-nNL-nAPS); SLE patients without LN but with APS (SLE-nNL-APS); SLE patients with LN but without APS (SLE-NL-nAPS); and SLE patients presenting with both conditions (SLE-NL-APS).

For the descriptive analysis, categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, while numerical variables were presented as means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges (IQr). The differences between groups were assessed using the Pearson χ 2 test for categorical variables and the Student’s t test for continuous variables.

Subsequent multivariable binary logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with the development of PE and PE with severity criteria (PESC) in both the overall obstetric population and in patients with SLE. Covariates were prespecified from the ASPRE high-risk model and clinical plausibility: advanced maternal age (>35 years), Black-African, Hispanic and Polynesian ethnicity, previous adverse obstetric outcomes (PRAOO), assisted reproductive techniques (ART), twin pregnancy, baseline hypertension (HBP), diabetes mellitus (DM) and obesity for adjustment [ 30 ]. Effect sizes were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0. Statistical significance was set at P -values <0.05.

## Ethics

The study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Puerta de Hierro University Hospital Ethics Committee (PI 101/25). The data were provided after all potential patient’s identifiers had been deleted, and all data were given anonymously. According to the Spanish law, informed consent was not required for the study.

## Results

### Baseline characteristics of the study population

Between 2016 and 2022, a total of 1 973 249 pregnancy-related hospital admissions were recorded in Spain. Of these, 1787 pregnancies (9.1‰) were in women diagnosed with SLE. In the group with SLE, 13.2% were previously diagnosed with LN and 9.3% with APS ( Table 1 ). Accordingly, the SLE population was distributed as follows: 1408 SLE nNL-nAPS (78.8%), 144 SLE-nLN-APS (8.1%), 213 SLE-LN-nAPS (11.9%) and 22 SLE-LN-APS (1.2%).

Compared with the general population of pregnant admitted women, those with SLE were more frequently over 35 years of age (39.2% vs 30%, P < 0.001) and primiparous (5.3% vs 3.9%, P = 0.003) and had higher rates of PRAOO (4.1% vs 2.2%, P < 0.001), ART (2.6% vs 1.5%, P < 0.001) and twin pregnancies (4.0% vs 2.4%, P < 0.001). Regarding cardiovascular and autoimmune comorbidities, HBP (5.8% vs 1%, P < 0.001), DL (0.4% vs 0.2%, P < 0.001), CKD (1.2% vs <0.1%, P < 0.001) and APS (9.3% vs 0.1%, P < 0.001) were more prevalent in SLE. Conversely, DM was less frequent (4.3% vs 6.3%, P < 0.001), while obesity rates were similar (3.8% vs 3.9%, P = 0.895). Finally, SLE patients had longer admission length (4.1 vs 3.1 mean days, P < 0.001) and a higher rate of ICU admission (1% vs 0.6%, P < 0.042). No deaths were identified in the SLE population and 126 in the non-SLE group (0% vs <0.1%, P = 1).

When SLE patients were further characterized according to prior LN or APS, the SLE-LN-nAPS and SLE-LN-APS groups exhibited higher prevalences of HBP (16.9% and 18.2%, respectively), CKD (5.2% and 13.6%, respectively) and of ICU admission (2.3% and 13.6%) compared with the general SLE population ( P < 0.05 for all).

### Pre-eclampsia rate, severity and outcomes during admission

In the overall cohort, 42 050 pregnancies (2.1%) developed PE, with 12 421 of them (29.5%) meeting severity criteria (PESC), including early-onset PE (7.4%), severe PE (73.1%), eclampsia (7.4%) and HELLP syndrome (16.8%) ( Table 2 ). Pregnant women with SLE had significantly higher rates of PE (5.5% vs 2.1%) and PESC (2.1% vs 0.6%), including early-onset PE (0.5% vs <0.1%), severe PE (1.3% vs 0.5%) and HELLP (0.6% vs 0.1%), ( P < 0.001 for all) ( Fig. 1 ).

However, significant differences in PE and PESC prevalence were observed across the four SLE subgroups, as illustrated in Table 2 . Compared with the other SLE subgroups, individuals without APS and LN showed a lower risk of PE and PESC (4.4% and 1.6%, respectively), while a markedly higher prevalence of both PE and PESC was evident in the SLE-LN-nAPS group (PE 10.3%, PESC 4.2%, respectively) and the SLE-APS-LN group (PE 22.7%, PESC 9.1%, respectively), ( P < 0.05 for all comparison with the remaining SLE patients).

Overall, 16.3% developed IUGR-LGW, 23.8% fetal distress and 1.6% abruptio placentae, without differences between SLE patients and the general Spanish population. Nevertheless, the rates of alive newborn, death newborn, cesarean section and instrumental delivery were similar. Regarding maternal outcomes, 3.310 (7.9%) were admitted to the ICU, without differences between patients with or without SLE. Fifty-four women (0.1%) suffered from a stroke and 16 (0.1%) died, all of these complications happening among women without SLE.

## Discussion

Building on evidence from previous smaller cohort studies, this nationwide cohort analysis confirms a higher prevalence of PE and PESC in SLE patients compared with the general obstetric population [ 11 , 31 , 32 ]. However, such an increased risk is largely attributable to LN and APS. Renal involvement in SLE confers a high likelihood of developing PE, particularly in the more severe forms. Both conditions potentiate each other, as the combination of LN and APS results in a 4-fold higher risk of PE compared with lupus women without any of them. Consequently, LN and APS should be a fundamental factor in pre-conceptional planning, PE risk stratification, pregnancy monitoring and prognostic assessment of PE in SLE patients. These findings underscore that strategies for PE risk assessment, prevention and management should not be uniform across all patients with SLE.

The heterogeneity of SLE means that PE risks vary considerably. Furthermore, this risk is likely compounded by baseline CKD and HBP from previous or current LN activity. As our findings indicate, these comorbidities are considerably more prevalent in this group of patients and are recognized to have a substantial impact on the development of PE [ 4 , 5 , 29 ]. Collectively, all these factors drive the elevated prevalence of PE and PESC in pregnant women with SLE and LN. Other studies have already proven that renal involvement in SLE, often manifesting as persistent hypertension and proteinuria, can increase the likelihood of developing PE by two to ten times [ 11 , 31 ]. Our study offers additional insights. Firstly, it identified only HBP, APS and LN as risk factors for PE in nearly 1800 pregnant patients with SLE, in whom ethnicity and other epidemiological or risk factors were not sustained as independent risk factors. Secondly, and of critical clinical importance, our analysis identifies LN as the sole independent factor associated with PE with severe features in patients with SLE, a finding that quantifies the severe risk previously suggested in the literature and reinforces the need for heightened surveillance in this specific subgroup [ 11 ]. This association likely reflects shared pathophysiological pathways, including defective placentation, chronic complement activation, immune complex deposition, increased type I interferon signalling and NETosis, which exacerbate placental oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction and an imbalance of angiogenic factors like sFlt-1 and PlGF [ 3 , 5 , 6 , 11 , 13 , 18 ].

Previous studies have shown that active lupus is a risk factor for PE [ 32 ]. Although our study could not assess the degree and chronology of disease activity, the adjusted association of SLE itself with PE and PESC makes it likely that active SLE or high disease burden play a role even in the absence of LN or APS.

As said, APS represents a major risk factor for PE, acting synergistically with SLE. Between 29 and 46% of SLE patients harbour antiphospholipid antibodies, with about 15% developing clinical APS; the other way round, around 36% of APS patients have coexisting SLE [ 16 , 31 ]. APS fosters a hypercoagulable state via antibody-mediated endothelial activation, platelet aggregation and thrombin generation, directly contributing to placental thrombosis and PE risk [ 14 , 30 , 33 ]. The inherent hypercoagulability associated with APS compounds the prothrombotic state often observed in PE [ 18 , 33 ]. Consequently, 20–50% of pregnant women with APS would develop PE, often with earlier onset and greater severity [ 18 , 20 ].

In addition, our work suggests a potential association linking LN and APS with IUGR-LGW, preterm birth and ICU admission. However, statistical confirmation was not achieved, likely due to the prevalence of these outcomes in the SLE patient group and sample size limitations for these specific analyses, despite the nationwide design of the study. Given the substantial contribution of both LN and APS to PE and its severity in SLE pregnancies, a risk-adapted therapeutic approach is essential to optimize maternal and fetal health [ 34–36 ]. Translating this heterogeneity into risk-stratified prevention is key, and current EULAR and FIGO guidance converge on an individualized strategy [ 2 , 34 ].

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, the data were obtained from a nationwide hospital discharge database, which precluded direct verification of the diagnoses by the authors and relied on secondary data, introducing the potential for inaccuracies in diagnostic coding. Nevertheless, we considered and analysed categorical variables and diagnoses based on robust clinical criteria that are difficult to misclassify, such as PE, eclampsia, HELLP, IUGR or preterm birth, thereby attenuating potential bias. Secondly, our analysis was limited to hospital admissions, potentially restricting the generalizability of our findings by excluding cases of PE managed in outpatient settings and introducing a selection bias toward more severe presentations. However, given that PE, and more so PESC, frequently requires hospitalization and monitored treatment, this concern may be mitigated. More important, we lacked detailed clinical data of patients, such as disease activity, serological markers or specific therapeutic regimens. The absence of differentiation between various SLE phenotypes and APS subtypes limits a more granular understanding of the risk profiles within these populations. Moreover, antiphospholipid antibodies carriers without APS were not captured in this study; therefore, it is possible that part of the increased risk of PE seen in patients with SLE without LN or APS would be caused by the presence of these antibodies. In addition, we lacked data on whether mothers received any treatment, as well as the specific therapies administered and their timing, including the use of low-dose aspirin either before conception or after a positive PE screening. It is likely that a substantial number of women with known risk factors such as APS had received therapy, which would have underestimated the risk of PE in this group. Despite these limitations, the large nationwide sample size, the inclusion of a well-defined obstetric population, and the robustness of the statistical analyses reinforce the validity of our findings, which are consistent with prior studies.

From a therapeutic and practical standpoint, our findings underscore the need for risk-stratified management throughout pregnancy in women with SLE, in accordance with EULAR recommendations [ 37 ]. First, pre-conceptional counseling should assure disease remission for at least 6–12 months, longer in women with LN, with adjustment to pregnancy-compatible therapies, stressing the continuation of hydroxychloroquine, evaluate APS history or high-risk antiphospholipid profiles and the history of adverse pregnancy outcomes, alongside general risk factors such as HBP. Second, during and even after pregnancy, baseline renal assessment, close monitoring of maternal disease activity and fetal or newborn well-being, tight monitoring of blood pressure and proteinuria, from a multidisciplinary approach, are essential to improve maternal and fetal outcomes [ 34 , 37 , 38 ].

Given the significant risks we have quantified, these findings highlight the importance of risk-stratified preventive therapy. However, as the SNHDD does not capture medication use during hospitalization or in outpatient care, we could not account for exposure to low-dose aspirin or heparin. Low-dose acetylsalicylic acid (LDASA) reduces the incidence of pre-eclampsia, yet real-world uptake remains inconsistent: only 25% of SLE pregnancies in a multinational inception cohort received LDASA, despite three-quarters of these pregnancies meeting at least one PE risk factor. Similarly, the SPROUT survey showed that only 63% of European rheumatologists routinely prescribe LDASA for all SLE pregnancies, with doses ranging from 75 to 162 mg daily [ 39 , 40 ]. The timing of initiation is also debated; many experts now recommend starting LDASA preconceptionally when LN or high-risk antiphospholipid antibodies are present. In obstetric APS, EULAR guidelines endorse a combination of LDASA and prophylactic low-molecular-weight heparin to mitigate the prothrombotic milieu [ 41 ]. Nevertheless, European registry studies indicate that 60–80% of women with SLE/LN already receive LDASA, although adherence may still be suboptimal, as highlighted by the PREPARE educational trial [ 42 , 43 ]. Evidence regarding heparin use and optimal timing is even more limited and conflicting. In any case, our findings suggest that not all women with SLE should be considered at high risk for PE, and therefore not all are candidates for early initiation of LDASA. Based on current evidence, our data support offering LDASA from preconception or ≤12 weeks’ gestation to women with LN, persistent high-risk antiphospholipid antibodies or a history of early-onset/severe PE. This recommendation aligns with evidence showing that LN doubles the risk of PE and that LDASA is a low-cost, safe intervention when appropriately targeted [ 44 , 45 ]. In contrast, for patients without LN, APS, high-risk antiphospholipid antibodies or a history of early-onset severe PE, first-trimester screening such as the ASPRE algorithm should guide decisions regarding LDASA initiation.

In conclusion, our nationwide analysis confirms that pregnant women with SLE face a substantially higher risk of PE and PESC, with both LN and APS emerging as strong, independent risk factors for these complications. These findings underscore the critical contributions of renal involvement and prothrombotic mechanisms to adverse obstetric outcomes. Therefore, recognizing the predictive roles of SLE, APS and especially LN is essential for implementing meticulous risk stratification and tailored, vigilant management strategies to improve maternal and fetal health in this high-risk population.
