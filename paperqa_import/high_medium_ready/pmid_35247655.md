# ABSTRACT

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an autoimmune disorder characterized by the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL) (lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin antibodies and anti-beta2glycoprotein I (anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ ) antibodies) and a plethora of macro- and micro-vascular manifestations, affecting predominantly young adults. Cardiovascular events are the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in APS. APL-mediated thromboinflammation and atherothrombosis are emerging pathogenetic mechanisms of cardiovascular disease (CVD) in APS, involving endothelial cell and monocyte activation, cytokines and adhesion molecules expression, complement and neutrophils activation, neutrophil extracellular traps formation, platelet cell activation and aggregation, and subsequent thrombin generation, in parallel with an oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL)- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ complex induced macrophage differentiation to foam cells. High risk aPL profile, especially the presence of lupus anticoagulant and triple aPL positivity (all three aPL subtypes), co-existence with Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE), as well as traditional risk factors such as smoking, hypertension, hyperlipemia and obesity are associated with both subclinical atherosclerosis and cardiovascular events in APS. Increased awareness of CVD risk by the physicians and patients, regular assessment and strict control of traditional risk factors, and lifestyle modifications are recommended. Use of low-dose aspirin should be considered for cardiovascular prevention in asymptomatic aPL carriers or SLE patients with high-risk aPL profile. The role of older agents such as hydroxychloroquine and statins or new potential targeted treatments against immuno- and athero-thrombosis has been demonstrated by experimental and some clinical studies and needs to be further evaluated by randomized controlled studies. This review summarizes the available evidence about the pathogenetic mechanisms and prevalence of cardiovascular events and subclinical atherosclerosis, the interrelationship between traditional and disease-related CVD risk factors, and the cardiovascular risk assessment and management in APS.

# 1. Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) in systemic autoimmune disorders has been associated with an interconnection between underlying autoimmune and inflammatory mechanisms and traditional CVD risk factors leading to a variety of cardiovascular events including ischemic stroke, coronary heart disease, myocardial disease, diastolic and systolic heart dysfunction, arrhythmias and heart failure [1,2]. Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a rare autoimmune disorder characterized by recurrent vascular events from large, medium and small vessels, often occurring despite an adequate anticoagulation treatment, and the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL), namely lupus

anticoagulant, anticardiolipin antibodies, and anti-beta2glycoprotein I (anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ ) antibodies [3,4]. Accumulating evidence supports an inflammatory, in addition to classic thrombotic, pathophysiology in APS shedding also light on pathogenesis and management of CVD, a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in APS.

This review will present the current evidence about the aPL-mediated inflammation, thrombosis, and atherogenesis interplay, the traditional and disease-related CVD factors, and the CVD risk assessment and management in APS.

# 2. Pathogenesis of CVD in APS

APL-mediated vascular endothelial dysfunction has a major role in APS pathogenesis but also in APS-associated coronary, cerebral, and peripheral vessel atherosclerosis [4-9]. Major aspects of the aPL-induced endothelial dysfunction include the endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) inhibition and impaired nitric oxide (NO) production and release, mediated by apolipoprotein E receptor 2 (apoER2), and protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A) [10-12] (Fig. 1). Binding of aPL to their major target, the phospholipid-binding protein $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ , induces endothelial cell activation via p38 mitogen activated protein kinase (p38MAPK) and nuclear factor $\kappa \mathrm{B}$ (NFkB), endothelial cell production of microparticles (MP) and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1, adhesion of monocytes to the endothelium, toll-like receptors (TLR) expression, and release of proinflammatory cytokines e.g IL- $1\beta$ , IL-6, IL-8, and anti-TNF, chemokines, and adhesion molecules including E-selectin, VCAM-1, ICAM-1 and tissue factor release leading to vascular inflammation and endothelial cell proliferation and intimal hyperplasia [4-12]. APL can also activate monocytes and the classic complement pathway which in turn induces tissue factor expression. Generation of C5a further contributes to vascular inflammation by promoting monocytes and endothelial cells activation, platelets aggregation, and neutrophils activation [13-15]. Neutrophil activation is an important player of the aPL-mediated thrombo-inflammation and atherothrombosis inducing tissue factor expression, excessive release of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) and release of their components (DNA complex, histones, microbialicidal peptides, cytokines, tissue factor) leading to thrombus formation [16-18]. Resistance to fibrinolysis has also been shown due to plasmin and tissue plasminogen activator inactivation [19]. Platelets have also a major role in the prothrombotic state in APS. Binding of aPL to domain I

of $\beta 2\mathrm{GP1}$ receptors on platelets induces platelet activation and aggregation [5-9,20]. Bidirectional relationship between platelets and neutrophils increases ROS release and NETs production [18,21]. Tissue factor expressed by NETs also activates platelets, while cytokines released from NETs participate in the activation of B cells and a new production of autoantibodies [18,21]. In addition, as it was shown in other autoimmune diseases, plasmacytoid dendritic cells produce type interferon (IFN-I) in APS which may regulate neutrophils, and in turn, NETs have been suggested to drive IFN-I production [22-24]. Further in the atherogenesis process, circulating aPL, especially those targeting $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ , cross-react with oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL), and the oxLDL- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ complex induces the differentiation of macrophages to foam cells and the development of atherosclerotic lesions, while activated cells express tissue factor, cytokines, chemokines, growth factors and metalloproteases, intensifying atherothrombosis [5-12]. The role of $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ in atherosclerosis development has been further tested. Recent studies showed that $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ may drive Th1 inflammatory process in atherosclerotic plaques from patients with primary APS (PAPS) [25], while in Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)-associated APS (SLE/APS), $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ can induce the expression of Interleukin-17/Interleukin-21 and Interferon- $\gamma$ in plaque-derived T-cell clones [26].

Evidence of epigenetic alterations and recent transcriptomic and protein profile studies helped to identify genes, miRNAs, and altered pathways in monocytes and plasma of patients with APS towards a better characterization of APS atherothrombotic risk [27-29].

# 3. Prevalence of CVD in APS

# 3.1. Cardiovascular events

In a European multicenter registry of 1000 APS patients (53% PAPS, 36.2% SLE/APS, 82% females, mean age: $42 \pm 14$ years), ischemic stroke was reported in 19.8% of patients, representing the most frequent arterial manifestation and the second most common vascular manifestation after deep vein thrombosis [4]. In the same cohort, myocardial infarction (MI) was reported in 5.5% of included patients while 1.9% of patients developed MI and 2.4% unstable angina during a 10-year follow-up. The prevalence of coronary artery disease was higher in patients with Systemic Lupus Erythematosus-associated APS (SLE/APS) than in those with primary APS (PAPS) (3.8% vs 1.2%, respectively) that can be explained by the high burden of CVD in SLE. The increased risk of premature atherosclerosis and cardiovascular events has been well recognized in patients with SLE vs the general population, however, their pathogenetic mechanisms remain poorly elucidated [1]. An interrelationship between the traditional risk factors although fail to fully account for accelerated atherosclerosis in SLE [30], and disease-related factors such as immune dysregulation involving pro-inflammatory cytokines, neutrophils, and the type I interferon pathway leading to endothelial dysfunction, and the atherogenic effects of glucocorticoids, contributes to vascular injury [1,31,32]. In a US population-based study using nationwide data, age-adjusted rates for acute MI and ischemic stroke steadily increased between 1996 and 2012 in both women and men with SLE in comparison with the general population, where decreased rates were observed over time [33]. In a systematic review and meta-analysis of all published survival studies in SLE between 1950 and 2016, CVD was responsible for 11.3% and 10.6% of deaths, respectively [34]. A meta-analysis of 27 articles reporting on MI in APS showed that 45% of patients were women and they were younger (mean age: $41.10 \pm 13.61$ years) than patients with MI in the general population and in the majority of cases, coronary arteries were described as normal [35].

Silent myocardial ischemic disease in APS has also been demonstrated by a few small imaging studies using scintigraphy or positron emission tomography (PET) [36]. One controlled cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMRI) study showed a significantly higher prevalence of late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) in 27 APS patients vs 81 healthy controls, a finding consistent with subclinical coronary microvascular dysfunction [37]. In a recent case-control study using for the first-time in APS adenosine stress CMRI, LGE was detected in 16 $(36\%)$ of 44 asymptomatic APS patients ( $64\%$ women, mean age: 44 years) but in none of the 44 age and sex-matched healthy controls $\mathrm{(p < 0.001)}$ although only 2 of 16 patients had abnormal coronary angiography [38]. A significantly lower myocardial perfusion reserve index (MPRI) was detected in patients with APS vs controls (1.5 (0.9-1.9) vs. 2.7 (2.2-3.2), $\mathsf{p} < 0.001$ ), and during a 12-month follow-up, $7 \%$ of patients developed coronary artery disease (mainly MI), notably, those with the lowest MPRI values.

Myocardial dysfunction has also been described in patients with APS [39]. In an earlier Doppler echocardiography study, a gradation of diastolic function impairment manifested by isovolumic relaxation time and prolonged deceleration time was observed across the groups of patients with PAPS, SLE/APS, SLE with positive aPL but without APS, and SLE without aPL [40]. In a 10-yr follow-up study of the same cohort, left ventricular dysfunction progressed similarly among patients with PAPS, SLE/APS, SLE with positive aPL but without APS, and SLE without aPL. Right ventricular diastolic dysfunction progressed mainly in the SLE-APS group [41]. Microangiopathy of the intramyocardial arterioles due to aPL-mediated endothelial perturbation has been suggested as a potential pathogenetic mechanism of ventricular dysfunction in APS [39-41]. Multiple small-vessel microthrombi have been detected in autopsy studies of aPL-positive patients with myocardial involvement [42,43].

Cardiovascular mortality: In the European cohort of 1000 APS patients, MI was the second most common cause of death after bacterial infections accounted for $19\%$ of deaths in a 5-year follow-up period, followed by $13\%$ of stroke-related deaths. The 10-year survival was $91\%$ in the entire APS group, without significant differences between PAPS and SLE/APS patients [4].

# 3.2. Subclinical atherosclerosis

Subclinical atherosclerosis is recognized as an early indicator of atherosclerotic burden in the general population and its timely recognition may prevent or delay the progression to CVD events. Table 1 summarizes the main findings and methodological characteristics of vascular ultrasound studies examining subclinical atherosclerosis in APS [44-57]. In a meta-analysis of 20 vascular ultrasound case-control studies), patients with APS had higher mean carotid intima-media thickness (IMT), a 3.8-fold higher frequency of carotid plaques (OR 3.87; $95\%$ CI 1.61, 9.31), and 4-times lower flow-mediated dilation compared to controls [58]. However, in the majority of these studies, the prevalence of subclinical atherosclerosis was compared only with healthy controls. In an earlier study, we compared, for the first time in the literature, the prevalence of carotid and femoral plaques and abnormal IMT in patients with APS vs those with SLE and rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and we observed a 4.3-fold higher risk of plaques in APS or SLE vs RA and healthy individuals [45]. Given that diabetes mellitus (DM) is considered a cardiovascular risk equivalent, we compared in a recent study the prevalence of subclinical atherosclerosis in APS vs age-and sex-matched DM patients and healthy subjects. Carotid and/or femoral plaques were detected in one third of both PAPS and DM patients versus $9\%$ of controls. After adjusting for age, sex, and all traditional CVD risk factors, both patients with PAPS and SLE/APS had a nearly 2.5-fold risk of atherosclerotic plaques in carotid and/or femoral arteries compared to controls, and similar to DM patients [54]. In a multivariate analysis, atherosclerotic plaques were associated with IgG anti- $\beta 2$ GPI antibodies in patients with SLE/APS and with the number of traditional CVD risk factors in both PAPS (relative risk $(\mathrm{RR} = 2.75,\mathrm{p}<0.001)$ and SLE/APS $(\mathrm{RR} = 1.84,\mathrm{p} < 0.001)$ , supporting their role in APS atherosclerosis. Interestingly, absence of premature atherosclerosis was detected in patients without traditional coronary artery disease risk factors in a case-control study of 27 female patients with PAPS and 27 age, body mass index (BMI) and sex-matched healthy controls [60].

Several studies have compared the risk for accelerated atherosclerosis in patients with PAPS vs SLE/APS, reporting either no significant difference in atherosclerotic plaque presence and/or IMT values between the two groups [45,49,53,54] or a higher prevalence of plaques in SLE/APS vs PAPS (37.5% vs 8%, $p = 0.003$ ) [47]. In a recent 3-year follow-up study of atherosclerotic plaque progression, the risk of new plaque development was higher in SLE/APS vs PAPS (OR = 7.75, $p = 0.038$ ) [59], in accordance with previous studies demonstrating a higher risk for atherosclerotic plaques in patients with SLE/APS vs PAPS [47], and the impact of SLE/APS in SLE atherosclerosis [60].

Additional vascular surrogates that have been evaluated in patients with APS include the pulse wave velocity and the augmentation index, both representing indicators of arterial stiffness; the flow mediation dilation, an index of endothelial function; and the ankle brachial index (the ratio of the systolic blood pressure at the ankle to that measured at the brachial artery), a noninvasive test for peripheral artery disease [61-72] (Table 2).

# 4.1. Traditional CVD risk factors

Hypertension is one of the most common traditional risk factors in APS, reported in $20 - 35\%$ of patients [73-77]. It is more frequently observed in SLE/APS than PAPS patients, and often in the context of co-existent lupus nephritis, renal artery thrombosis, renal thrombotic microangiopathy, and chronic APS nephropathy lesions [77,78]. It is mostly associated with arterial compared to venous thrombotic events. Hyperlipidemia is present in $20 - 25\%$ of patients in several APS registries and has been demonstrated as independent risk factor for CVD manifestations [4,73-77].

The prevalence of smoking ranges between $30\%$ and $40\%$ in APS patients [4,74-76], and is one of the most important predictors of CVD events, especially of cerebrovascular events. It has been also shown that SLE patients with positive aPL are more likely to be smokers than those without aPL [79,80]. From a large set of commonly occurring autoantibodies in SLE, only aPL were positively associated with a history of smoking, and more specifically the positive LA test and the IgG anticardiolipin antibodies (aCL) and anti- $\beta 2$ GPI antibodies [80]. The combination of smoking with aPL, and especially the coexistence of ever regular smoking with LA or triple aPL positivity, was strongly correlated with arterial vascular events, especially ischemic stroke.

Metabolic syndrome has been reported in $17 - 38\%$ of APS patients in different studies using various sets of diagnostic criteria [73,74,81]. In a recent study from the Antiphospholipid Syndrome Alliance for Clinical Trials and International Networking (APS ACTION) Registry database, DM was present in around $5\%$ of aPL positive patients with and without SLE, and obesity was observed in one fourth of asymptomatic aPL carriers and in one third of SLE patients with positive aPL [82]. The clinical significance of traditional CVD risk factors in APS has also been shown in another analysis of the APS ACTION registry data where CVD risk factors were present in $73\%$ of recurrent vascular events compared to $44\%$ of the initial vascular events [83].

# 4.2. Disease-related CVD risk factors

Disease-related CVD risk factors include the aPL profile and the disease-related medications. In a large population study of women younger than 50 years admitted with first ischemic stroke or myocardial infarction (MI), LA was tested positive in $17\%$ of patients with stroke and $3\%$ of those with MI, while women with positive LA had higher risk for stroke and MI vs those without (OR 43 for stroke, OR 5.3 for MI) [84]. In another multicenter study from 17 Swedish hospitals including 800 patients with a first MI, a statistically significant association was observed between MI and the presence of positive IgG anticardiolipin and/or anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ antibodies (OR 7.8 [95% CI, 4.0–15.3]; $p < 0.001$ ) [85]. In a systematic literature review of 43 studies, the overall frequency of aPL among young patients with CVD events was $17\%$ for any CVD event and $12\%$ for stroke, while the presence of aPL was associated with a 5-fold increased risk of CVD manifestations [86]. In a 3-year follow-up carotid and femoral artery ultrasound study of 101 patients with SLE and 85 age/sex-matched patients with RA and 85 healthy controls, a 3-fold higher risk for plaque progression was detected in SLE vs controls (OR 2.81, CI 1.04, 7.64). Multivariate determinants of plaque progression in SLE were the presence of aPL (OR 2.00, $p = 0.043$ ) and the traditional CVD risk factors as expressed by the SCORE (OR 2.87, $p = 0.019$ ) [87]. In another subclinical atherosclerosis study including asymptomatic aPL carriers and APS patients, the prevalence of carotid IMT and plaques was significantly higher in patients with triple or double aPL positivity and in anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ positive vs anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ negative subjects [88]. Medications in PAPS or SLE-APS patients can be proatherogenic such as glucocorticoids, especially their daily and cumulative dose, or atheroprotective such as aspirin and hydroxychloroquine (HCQ). In EULAR recommendations for the management of APS, low dose aspirin is recommended in asymptomatic aPL carriers or SLE patients with high risk aPL profile [90]. SLE studies providing stratified data for the aPL positive patients showed a protective effect of HCQ against arterial thrombosis, especially stroke and MI [91]. Observational studies [92] and one randomized controlled trial in PAPS [93] showed a reduced incidence of arterial event recurrences in patients treated with HCQ plus standard treatment vs standard treatment alone, and a reduction in aPL titres at longitudinal measures.

# 5. Assessment of CVD risk

CVD risk in APS can be assessed by the clinical prediction tools and vascular surrogate markers used in the general population. Generic risk prediction models such as the Framingham score, the heart SCORE or other clinical scores (American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA, Reynolds Risk Score) usually underestimate CVD risk in systemic rheumatic disorders like RA and SLE [94,95]. The performance of their modified versions such as the modified Framingham and the modified heart SCORE (mSCORE) multiplied by 2.0 and 1.5, respectively, have been examined in SLE [95,96] and other rheumatic disorders but not in APS populations. Given the current evidence limitations, the recently published "EULAR recommendations for the management of CVD risk in Rheumatic and Musculoskeletal Disorders (RMDs) including SLE and APS" [97] did not endorse the use of any specific CVD risk prediction tool, but instead suggested a rigorous assessment of traditional and disease-related risk factors. Disease-specific prediction tools incorporating disease-specific characteristics in addition to traditional risk factors are warranted, however, previous efforts in other autoimmune diseases have proven insufficient. The adjusted Global APS SCORE (aGAPSS) including a combination of the aPL profile and two traditional cardiovascular risk factors, namely hyperlipidaemia and arterial hypertension, was developed as a scoring system to stratify patients with aPL according to their risk for thrombotic manifestations of APS [98]. A modified version $(\mathrm{aGAPSS}_{\mathrm{CVD}})$ was recently designed, adding also smoking, obesity and DM, with a higher detection rate for CVD events compared to aGAPSS [99], but further validation in external cohorts is needed.

Identification of circulating or imaging biomarkers of CVD in APS is also needed. Recently, growth differentiation factor-15 (GDF-15), a proinflammatory cytokine was associated with 2.4-fold higher risk for

atherosclerotic plaques suggesting its potential role in CVD risk stratification in APS [100]. Vascular surrogate markers of CVD risk used in the general population might also be used in APS, including the presence of carotid and femoral atherosclerotic plaques, IMT, pulse wave velocity, augmentation index, flow-mediated dilatation, and the coronary artery calcium score, however, their implementation in routine care needs to be further tested [101]. According to the European Society of Cardiology guidelines, the presence of atherosclerotic plaques should be considered as a risk modifier in individuals at low or moderate risk [102]. Previous studies in other rheumatic diseases of high CVD risk showed a CVD risk re-classification after incorporating vascular ultrasound findings in CVD risk estimates using generic clinical scores [94, 95,103].

# 6. Management of CVD risk

In the "EULAR recommendations for the management of APS in adults", screening for and strict control of CVD risk factors is recommended for all aPL positive individuals and particularly for those with a high-risk aPL profile, including smoking cessation, management of hypertension, hyperlipidaemia and diabetes, and regular physical activity [90]. Recently, CVD risk modification was successfully attempted via a 3-year counseling program in SLE patients (some aPL positive) [104]. Prospective studies are needed to examine these approaches in APS. The effect of implementation of similar CVD risk prevention strategies to those followed in other high CVD risk populations such as DM [102], should also be tested. Recently, we found a comparable or higher prevalence of CVD risk factors between APS and age- and sex-matched patients with RA and DM [105] but low CVD risk factor target attainment in APS according to the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines [106].

Due to lack of evidence from clinical studies on the use of specific antihypertensives or the effect of lipid-lowering agents on the prevention of cardiovascular events, the EULAR task force for the management of CVD risk in RMDs recommended the use of the general population guidelines for hypertension and hyperlipidemia treatment in APS [97]. In patients with SLE or asymptomatic aPL carriers with high-risk aPL profile, prophylactic treatment with low-dose aspirin was recommended for cardiovascular prevention [80,97].

The pleotropic action of HCQ, including its effect on glucose and lipid levels, antibody and cytokine production, oxidative stress, complement inhibition, TLR 7 and 9 signaling inhibition, and its role in the prevention of arterial thrombosis [93,107-109], supports its potential use for CVD risk reduction in APS but this needs to be evaluated in randomized controlled studies. A multicenter trial is currently examining the role of HCQ in secondary prevention of thrombotic events in PAPS [110]. Interestingly, a pilot trial (OXI) examined the

anti-inflammatory effect of HCQ use after acute MI in the general population and showed a significant reduction in interleukin-6 levels [111].

In vitro and experimental studies have also shown that statin use can inhibit the aPL-induced endothelial cell activation, upregulation of tissue factor, expression of cellular adhesion and migration molecules and of nuclear factor kappa beta (NF- $\kappa \beta$ ), and the thrombogenic effects of aPL in aPL-injected mice [112]. In addition, two prospective clinical studies showed that treatment with fluvastatin $20\mathrm{mg}$ for 1 month was associated with inhibition of monocyte activation markers, tissue factor and PAR1 and PAR2 expression, while fluvastatin $40\mathrm{mg / d}$ for 3 months reduced the levels in half of pro-inflammatory and pro-thrombotic biomarkers tested [113,114]. A recent prospective study showed a lower risk of recurrent thrombosis with statin use (HR 0.28; $95\%$ CI, 0.12-0.65; $\mathfrak{p} = 0.003$ ) during a $48.5\pm 34.9$ months follow-up [115]. The 16th international congress task force report on APS treatment trends stated that statins may be beneficial in arterial thrombosis prevention in APS, however, according to current evidence, their use cannot be recommended in the absence of hyperlipidaemia [116].

Evidence also supports the effect of coenzyme Q10 on oxidative stress, monocyte and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) activation, and tissue factor expression [117]. Emerging targeted treatments in APS may alter the thromboinflammation and atherothrombosis process, including B-cell depletion or B-lymphocyte stimulator (BLyS) inhibition based on the results from a few experimental [118] and clinical studies [119] and probably, independently of their effect on aPL titres [120,121], complement inhibition, tissue factor inhibition, or anti-interferon treatment [122-125]. Recent experimental studies also showed that adenosine A2A receptor agonism can ameliorate aPL-mediated NETosis and endothelial cell activation and mitigates thrombosis [126,127].

# 7. Future directions and conclusion

Patients with APS, either at its primary form or in the context of SLE, are at increased CVD risk mediated by thrombo-inflammatory and atherothrombotic mechanisms and an interrelationship between disease-related and traditional risk factors. However, there are still several CVD risk prediction goals to be addressed such as improvements in awareness of CVD risk by both physicians and patients, regular assessment and early management of modifiable CVD risk factors, patient education and counseling about lifestyle changes, and the future development of validated disease-specific CVD risk screening tools [128]. The cardiovascular effect of older agents such as HCQ and statins and the efficacy of new potential immunoregulatory treatments in APS needs also to be examined in large prospective studies.
