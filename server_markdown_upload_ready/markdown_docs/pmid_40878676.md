# INTRODUCTION

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an acquired autoimmune disease with a prevalence of roughly 1 in 2000 that is best known for causing thrombosis and late-term pregnancy loss. $^{1,2}$ While APS is classically associated with acute macrovascular thrombotic events such as stroke, deep vein thrombosis, and pulmonary embolism, a significant proportion of APS patients will also experience chronic organ damage resulting from vessel-occlusive neointima formation in smaller blood vessels. This underrecognized (and likely underdiagnosed) picture is sometimes referred to as APS vasculopathy. $^{3-5}$ Though “APS vasculopathy” lacks a universally accepted definition, the term is most commonly used to describe immune-mediated vascular injury of the APS microvasculature leading to endothelial dysfunction, neointima formation, fibrosis, and vessel wall remodeling. It may occur with or without local thrombosis and commonly occurs in patients who also have a history of the macrovascular thrombotic events for which APS is best known. In a multinational cohort comprising over 800 patients with positive antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL), more than half presented with some of these other manifestations of APS, $^{6}$ including livedoid vasculopathy of the skin, nephropathic changes in the kidneys, myocardial ischemia with normal coronary arteries, and white matter lesions in the brain,

<!-- page 3 -->
all of which are likely attributable to a smoldering and progressive vasculopathy. $^{6}$ Currently, lifelong anticoagulation is the primary treatment for APS, providing some protection against large-vessel events. However, its efficacy in preventing small-vessel vasculopathy and long-term organ deterioration remains limited.

APS vasculopathy is thought to be driven by the abnormal proliferation of vessel-resident cells, such as microvascular endothelial cells (MVECs) and vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), leading to neointima formation and eventually the occlusion of blood vessels. $^{7,8}$ Chronic kidney damage is the best-described complication of APS vasculopathy, appreciated in both patients with primary APS and APS associated with systemic lupus erythematosus. Histopathological examination of the kidneys often demonstrates not only acute fibrin thrombi in arterioles or glomeruli (e.g., thrombotic microangiopathy), but also chronic changes of the arteries and arterioles such as fibrous and fibrocellular occlusions and fibrous intimal hyperplasia (e.g., neointima formation). $^{9}$ Beyond the kidneys, autopsies have also demonstrated vaso-occlusive lesions in small vessels of organs such as the brain, heart, liver, and adrenal glands that are reminiscent of the aforementioned changes in the kidneys, $^{10-12}$ highlighting that APS vasculopathy is likely underdiagnosed in routine clinical practice.

A significant knowledge gap remains regarding the molecular pathways involved in the development of the proliferative lesions characteristic of APS vasculopathy. Roughly 10 years ago, a study linking clinical observations with in vitro experimentation suggested a vital role for the mTORC pathway in the pathogenesis of APS vasculopathy. $^{13}$ Specifically, it was observed that the mTORC pathway was activated in proliferating MVECs and adjacent VSMCs. Supporting these findings, pharmacological inhibition of the mTORC pathway using sirolimus showed promise in preventing neointima formation in a retrospective cohort of APS patients following kidney transplant. $^{13}$ Mouse models were not utilized in this work, nor were any unbiased approaches to pathway detection employed.

Among the organs affected by APS vasculopathy, the skin's "accessibility" presents a unique advantage for research purposes. In particular, livedo racemosa, the most frequently observed skin manifestation of APS, serves as a potentially valuable window for understanding APS small-vessel vasculopathy. Livedo racemosa presents as violaceous, net-like mottling of the skin that is typically nonuniform, not reversible, and characterized by "broken" rather than closed circles. In contrast, reversibility and closed circles are features of the milder livedo reticularis, which can also be appreciated in APS, albeit with less specificity for the diagnosis. Both forms of livedo are thought to reflect superficial venous network congestion (and possibly deoxygenation) secondary to sluggish or occluded blood flow in the deeper tissues. In severe cases, this pathophysiology can progress to tissue damage and ulcerations, heralding livedoid vasculopathy. Notably, the presence of livedo racemosa associates with various internal manifestations of APS, most notably cerebrovascular disease.[14,15]

Here, we began by using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to analyze dermal MVECs of skin samples obtained from well-characterized APS patients with livedo racemosa. We found evidence for activation of YAP1-mediated signaling in APS vessels, and then used in vitro experiments to demonstrate that the well-established YAP1 target

<!-- page 4 -->
CCN2 plays a crucial role in facilitating communication between MVECs and VSMCs. In vivo, CCN2 inhibition with an anti-CCN2 monoclonal antibody significantly reduced neointima thickening and cell proliferation in a novel mouse model of APS IgG-accelerated neointima formation. These findings offer new insights into the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying APS vasculopathy, while providing several potential therapeutic targets for patients.

# BRIEF METHODS

# Human participants and sample collection.

Three patients with primary APS and active livedo racemosa were recruited for skin biopsy at the University of Michigan (Table S1). Furthermore, 95 patients from a United States-based APS cohort (Table S2) and 81 patients from an independent China-based APS cohort (Table S3) were recruited for biomarker studies. In all tables, race/ethnicity was self-identified. APS patients met the clinical and laboratory requirements of APS, as defined by the updated Sapporo classification criteria. $^{1}$ All aspects of the study were approved by the relevant institutional review committees, and participants provided informed consent prior to any study activities.

# Single-cell RNA sequencing.

Skin biopsies were incubated overnight in $0.4\%$ Dispase (Life Technologies) in Hank's balanced saline solution (Gibco) at $4^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ . The epidermis and dermis were separated. The epidermis was digested in $0.25\%$ trypsin-EDTA (Gibco) with deoxyribonuclease I (10 U/ml; Thermo Scientific) for 1 hour at $37^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ , quenched with fetal bovine serum (Atlanta Biologicals), and strained through a $70 - \mu \mathrm{m}$ mesh. The dermis was minced, digested in $0.2\%$ collagenase II (Life Technologies) and $0.2\%$ collagenase V (Sigma) in plain medium for 1.5 hours at $37^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ , and strained through a $70 - \mu \mathrm{m}$ mesh. Epidermal and dermal cells were combined in a 1:1 ratio, and libraries were constructed by the University of Michigan Advanced Genomics Core on the 10x Chromium system with chemistry v3. Libraries were then sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer to generate 150-base-pair paired-end reads. Data processing, $^{16}$ quality control, $^{16}$ cell type annotation, $^{16}$ determination of differentially expressed genes, ligand-receptor interactions, $^{17}$ and other aspects of the analysis are all described in the supplemental materials. The single-cell RNA-sequencing data generated in this study have been deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession number GSE290946.

# Cell culture and reagents.

Human dermal MVECs (CC-2543, Lonza) were grown in culture medium (CC-3202, Lonza) without hydrocortisone in $2\%$ gelatin-coated plates and used between passages 3 and 6. All treatments were done in stimulation medium (culture medium without FBS and hydrocortisone). For serum stimulation, serum from APS patients and healthy volunteers was diluted in stimulation medium $(20\%)$ and incubated with MVECs for 48 hours; in studies that quantified proteins (e.g., CCN1 and CCN2) in the culture medium, cell-free wells were also included in order to subtract for any protein that may have been present in the serum itself. For IgG stimulation, MVECs were preincubated with purified $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ (5

<!-- page 5 -->
$\mu \mathrm{g} / \mathrm{ml}$ ) in stimulation medium for 1 hour before adding human IgG (isolated from healthy volunteers or APS patients, $100~\mu \mathrm{g} / \mathrm{ml}$ ) or anti- $\beta 2$ GPI IgG ( $10~\mu \mathrm{g} / \mathrm{ml}$ ) for 48 hours. An anti-CCN2 monoclonal antibody, with a sequence identical to pamrevlumab, was used in some in vitro studies (Selleck A2042, purity $99\%$ , and affinity confirmed). The control IgG1 was from Abcam (ab155632).

# Mouse model.

All procedures followed were in accordance with institutional guidelines. Femoral artery endothelial injury and activation were performed on male C57BL6/J mice at least 10 weeks of age and 25 grams of weight, as previously described.[19,20] Following anesthesia, an arteriotomy was created with microscopic scissors at the branching point of the deep femoral and superficial femoral arteries. A 0.015" guidewire (S-SF-15-15, Cook Medical) was then inserted and advanced $5 - 10\mathrm{mm}$ toward the iliac artery, remaining in place for 3 minutes. Upon guidewire removal, blood flow was reconstituted after careful suturing of the vessel wall with 11-0 nylon. Starting from the first postoperative day, mice were administered either control IgG or APS IgG $(500\mu \mathrm{g}$ each) by intraperitoneal injection every 3 days. Some APS IgG-treated mice also received an anti-CCN2 monoclonal antibody $(10\mathrm{mg / kg})$ every 3 days. After 2 weeks, mice were euthanized and immediately perfused with $4 \%$ paraformaldehyde (PFA) via the left ventricle for 10 minutes, and the injured left femoral arteries were harvested for analysis.

# Statistics.

The statistical methods for analyzing the relevant scRNA-seq data are detailed in the supplemental materials. Otherwise, normally distributed data were presented as the mean (error bars indicate standard error of the mean) and analyzed using a t-test, with Welch's correction applied if the groups had unequal variances. For skewed data, differences in values between the two groups were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Associations were assessed by Spearman's correlation coefficients. Grouped data were compared by two-way ANOVA, followed by Sidak post hoc analysis. For each analysis, the sample size (n) for each experimental group is indicated in the figure legend and/or data table. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software. A p-value of $< 0.05$ was considered statistically significant.

# RESULTS

# Single-cell RNA sequencing identifies dermal endothelial cells in livedoid skin biopsies.

To gain insights into the cellular composition and transcriptional changes associated with APS and livedo racemosa, we performed scRNA-seq analysis on research skin biopsies obtained from the upper thighs of three primary APS patients with both livedo racemosa

<!-- page 6 -->
and a history of known microvascular complications such as APS nephropathy, extensive white matter lesions, and catastrophic APS (Table S1). We also included skin samples from four age-matched healthy individuals collected from the same anatomical site for comparison. The scRNA-seq analysis included a total of 14,683 individual cells, with an average expression of 2,743 genes and 12,498 transcripts per cell. Visualization of the results using a uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) plot revealed 24 potential cell clusters (Fig. 1A). Further annotation of these clusters identified 10 major cell types, encompassing cells from both APS and healthy control skin biopsies (Fig. 1B-D). Cell composition analysis demonstrated similar proportions of cells, including endothelial cells, in the APS skin samples compared with healthy controls (Fig. S1A-B). Given the likely role of MVECs in the pathogenesis of APS vasculopathy, we conducted a comprehensive investigation of EC subtypes within our dataset. EC subclustering identified six distinct cell clusters comprising a total of 1,240 ECs (Fig. 1E). Further annotation of the clusters allowed us to categorize these ECs into four main subtypes: arterial ECs (AECs), venous ECs (VECs), capillary ECs (CapECs), and lymphatic ECs (LECs). These subtypes encompassed ECs from both APS and healthy control samples (Fig. 1F-H and Fig. S2). Finally, we explored differentially expressed genes in total APS endothelial cells relative to controls, with the top-20 upregulated genes highlighted in Fig. 1I. We used gene set enrichment analysis (based on all differentially expressed genes) to identify potentially enriched biological processes in APS endothelial cells (Fig 1J). Among the top enriched processes were interferon response and complement, both of which have been previously implicated in APS. $^{1,2}$ There was also evidence of increased adipogenesis, mediated by differentially expressed genes like $FABP4$ , $CD36$ , and $FABP5$ . With potential relevance to neointima formation, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition and hypoxia also emerged as notable processes likely to be enhanced in APS endothelial cells relative to controls.

# APS dermal MVECs demonstrate increased expression of CCN1 and CCN2.

$CCN1$ (cellular communication network factor 1, also known as CYR61) and $CCN2$ (cellular communication network factor 2, also known as connective tissue growth factor/CTGF) emerged as the most notable upregulated genes associated with the cell proliferation that is expected in APS vasculopathy (Fig. 2A). With a key role in biological processes discussed above like mesenchymal transition and hypoxia, CCN1 and CCN2 are secreted proteins known to play critical roles in various cellular functions including cell proliferation, differentiation, adhesion, migration, and angiogenesis,[21] suggesting their potential involvement in the progression of vasculopathy. Violin and feature plots were generated to further characterize the distribution of $CCN1$ and $CCN2$ expression in both control and APS MVECs (Fig. 2B-C). We also used violin plots to assess the expression of $CCN1$ and $CCN2$ across different MVEC subtypes. We found that both genes demonstrated higher expression in APS versus control across all four MVEC subtypes (Fig. S3A-B). To further validate our findings, we performed immunofluorescence staining of CCN1 and CCN2 in dermal skin vessels. We found increased CCN1 and CCN2 expression in both APS dermal MVECs and APS dermal VSMCs relative to controls (Fig. 2D-F). Of note, $CCN1$ and $CCN2$ were also upregulated at the transcriptional level in APS VSMCs compared with controls based on our scRNA-seq dataset (Fig. S4A-B).

# Increased CCN1 and CCN2 in APS plasma.

To investigate the potential for systemic distribution of CCN1 and CCN2, we examined their levels in the plasma of 95 patients from a United States-based APS cohort compared with 40 healthy controls (Table S2). We found a significant increase in both CCN1 and CCN2 in APS patients (Fig. 3A-B). Notably, we observed a strong correlation between the plasma levels of CCN1 and CCN2 (Fig. 3C), suggesting potential coregulation. Of the 95 individuals included in the APS cohort, 33 $(35\%)$ were noted to have a history of some type of "livedo" (Table S2), though the case report forms did not distinguish between livedo reticularis and the more specific (and severe) livedo racemosa that had anchored our skin biopsies. The case report forms also failed to note whether the livedo was active at the time of blood sampling. With that context, CCN1 and CCN2 did not differ between historically livedo-positive and livedo-negative participants. We then conducted a correlation analysis to explore the possible relationship between CCN1/2 levels and objective kidney functional parameters in APS patients. The results demonstrated significant positive correlations between CCN2 and serum creatinine, as well as the urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (Fig. 3D-E), suggesting the potential involvement of CCN2 in APS-related kidney damage. To validate our findings, we evaluated CCN2 levels in an independent China-based APS cohort $(n = 81)$ (Table S3) where we found elevated CCN2 levels in APS plasma compared with healthy controls $(n = 42)$ (Fig. 3F). We also asked whether a history of non-APS kidney dysfunction might promote increased CCN2 in circulation and found no difference between individuals with and without an eGFR $< 60$ (Fig. S5).

# Upregulation of CCN1 and CCN2 in response to APS patient-derived serum and IgG.

We next conducted in vitro experiments using healthy dermal MVECs. We found that the expression of both $CCN1$ and $CCN2$ genes was upregulated when the dermal MVECs were cultured with APS patient-derived serum, total IgG, or affinity-purified anti-beta-2 glycoprotein I (anti- $\beta$ 2GPI) antibodies, all of which were isolated from APS patients with known microvascular complications (Fig. 3G-I). To determine whether anti- $\beta$ 2GPI activity is instrumental in the upregulation of CCN1 and CCN2, we depleted anti- $\beta$ 2GPI antibodies from total APS IgG, which eliminated all detectable anti- $\beta$ 2GPI activity (data not shown) and thereby prevented CCN1 and CCN2 upregulation by IgG-treated MVECs (Fig. S6A). Furthermore, pilot experiments demonstrated that APS IgG failed to trigger significant upregulation of $CCN1/CCN2$ unless the culture medium was supplemented with $\beta$ 2GPI protein (Fig. S6B), as was done in all subsequent experiments. Notably, similar $CCN1$ and $CCN2$ expression increases were appreciated when human aortic endothelial cells (HAECs) were exposed to APS IgG (Fig. S7). As expected from past studies, treating MVECs with APS IgG also upregulated the expression of classic adhesion molecules (Fig. S8A), without triggering increased apoptosis (Fig. S8B). Moreover, MVECs exposed to the APS-derived specimens exhibited increased release of CCN1 and CCN2 into the culture supernatants (Fig. 3J-L), with CCN2 consistently more abundant than CCN1. Collectively, these findings indicate that CCN1 and CCN2 not only have the potential to be expressed locally but can also be found at elevated levels in the circulation.

# YAP1 mediates APS antibody-induced CCN1 and CCN2 expression in MVECs.

To gain further insights into the underlying molecular mechanisms regulating CCN1 and CCN2 expression, we focused on the yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1) pathway. YAP1, the classic regulator of CCN1 and CCN2 gene expression, is a transcriptional co-activator that plays a fundamental role in cellular growth and proliferation.[22-24] Activation of YAP1 occurs through its translocation from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, facilitated by decreased phosphorylation. This translocation enables YAP1 to guide transcription factors to specific DNA sequences and initiate the transcription of target genes, including CCN1 and CCN2.[25] To investigate the translocation of YAP1 in response to APS IgG stimulation, dermal MVECs were stimulated and subjected to immunofluorescence staining. We identified significant translocation of YAP1 into the nucleus upon APS IgG stimulation (Fig. 4A-B). Western blot analysis further supported these findings by revealing a decrease in YAP1 phosphorylation at serine 127 (S127) (Fig. 4C-D). To determine the potential functional role of YAP1 in the regulation of CCN1 and CCN2 expression, we performed siRNA-mediated YAP1 knockdown experiments in APS IgG-stimulated MVECs. Knockdown of YAP1 resulted in a significant reduction in CCN1 and CCN2, whether measured as mRNA or secreted protein (Fig. 4E-J). Regarding the surface receptor responsible for activating the YAP1-CCN1/CCN2 axis, we knocked down two potential coreceptors for $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI} / \alpha \mathrm{nt} - \beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ complexes on the endothelial cell surface, LRP8/ApoER2 and TLR4 (Fig. S9). Knockdown of TLR4, and to a lesser extent $LRP8$ , abolished increased CCN1 and CCN2 expression in response to APS IgG (Fig. 4K-N). Of note, none of YAP1, LRP8, or TLR4 was upregulated at the transcriptional level in our scRNA-seq dataset. Together, these findings provide evidence for the critical role of YAP1 in the upregulation of CCN1 and CCN2 in APS IgG-stimulated MVECs, highlighting its potential as a regulator of APS vasculopathy.

# MVEC-derived CCN2 promotes the proliferation and migration of VSMCs.

We next characterized the potential for communication within APS skin by analyzing potential ligand-receptor pairs (Database of Interacting Proteins) within our scRNA-seq dataset. By focusing on upregulated ligands in APS MVECs, we found the potential for strong communication between MVECs, particularly through ligand CCN2, and both VSMCs and fibroblasts (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, compared with CCN1, CCN2 had higher expression and distribution in dermal MVECs (Fig. 2B-C). We therefore asked whether a CCN2-mediated interaction between MVECs and VSMCs might play an important role in APS vasculopathy. We calculated interaction scores for each ligand-receptor pair between MVECs and VSMCs. We then depicted the top 5 receptors on VSMCs, including LRP1 and EGFR, that might interact with MVEC-derived CCN2 (Fig. 5B). To investigate the potential functional consequences of the interaction between CCN2 and VSMCs, we established an in vitro experimental system (Fig. 5C). Conditioned medium from APS IgG-stimulated dermal MVECs was added to VSMCs in the presence of either an anti-CCN2 monoclonal antibody or isotype control IgG1. VSMCs exposed to the APS IgG-stimulated MVEC medium demonstrated increased proliferation, while blocking CCN2 with an anti-CCN2 monoclonal antibody partially reversed this effect (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, we used a scratch assay, which quantifies the migratory behavior of VSMCs, to demonstrate that VSMCs exhibited increased migration when exposed to APS IgG-stimulated MVEC medium. Again,

<!-- page 9 -->
when CCN2 was blocked with an anti-CCN2 monoclonal antibody, the migratory behavior of VSMCs was reversed (Fig. 5E and Fig. S10), indicating the direct involvement of CCN2 in promoting VSMC migration. Guided by the aforementioned receptor-ligand informatics analysis, we knocked down $LRP1$ and $EGFR$ in VSMCs (Fig. S11) and then exposed them to conditioned medium from APS IgG-stimulated dermal MVECs as above. Knockdown of $EGFR$ , but not $LRP1$ , led to significant decreases in VSMC proliferation and migration (Fig. 5F-G and Fig. S12). Finally, we also found a significant increase in MVEC proliferation in the presence of APS IgG, which was counteracted by an anti-CCN2 monoclonal antibody (Fig. S13). Taken together, these findings underscore the potential therapeutic value of an anti-CCN2 monoclonal antibody in inhibiting the proliferation of both MVECs and VSMCs induced by APS IgG.

# Activation of YAP1 and upregulation of CCN2 in the vessels of patients with APS nephropathy.

To investigate APS vasculopathy in internal organs, we focused on the kidneys, which are at risk for chronic APS nephropathy. Immunofluorescence staining for Ki67, a cell proliferation marker, was significantly increased in both MVECs and VSMCs of APS nephropathy patients (Fig. S14A-C). Ki67 was similarly increased in skin vessels (Fig. S15A-C). We next examined the expression patterns of CCN2 in the vessels of APS nephropathy biopsies. Immunofluorescence staining revealed a pronounced elevation in the expression of CCN2 in the APS vessels, where they colocalized with markers of both MVECs and VSMCs (Fig. 6A-B). Furthermore, our investigation of YAP1 expression patterns in MVECs demonstrated increased YAP1 nuclear translocation in APS vessels compared with controls (Fig. 6C-D). These findings underscore the potentially critical association between vascular CCN2 expression, along with YAP1 nuclear translocation, in proliferative APS nephropathy lesions.

# CCN2 inhibition protects against APS IgG-accelerated neointima formation in mice.

Though acute arterial, venous, and microvascular APS thrombosis have all been routinely modeled in mice and other animals $^{26}$ , we were unable to identify a chronic APS model suitable for studying CCN2 and its related proliferative pathways. We therefore developed a new strategy adapted from a common approach used to study neointima formation in mice. $^{19,20}$ After introducing a guidewire into the femoral artery to trigger endothelial injury and activation, mice were administered either control IgG or APS IgG every 3 days over the course of 2 weeks (Fig. 7A); some mice also received an anti-CCN2 monoclonal antibody every 3 days over the course of the 2-week treatment period. Compared with the injured vessels of mice receiving control IgG, the injured vessels of mice receiving APS IgG demonstrated significant enlargement of the intima at 2 weeks (Fig. 7B), whether quantified as average cross-sectional thickness or cross-sectional area (Fig. 7C, Fig. S16); spontaneous thrombosis was not appreciated in any of the examined vessels. Furthermore, the neointima primarily consisted of a significantly expanded smooth muscle actin-positive cell population, likely VSMCs, rather than endothelial cells or collagen (Fig. S17). Compared with APS IgG-treated mice receiving vehicle, APS IgG-treated mice receiving the anti-CCN2 monoclonal antibody showed a significant reduction in neointima formation (Fig. 7B-C and Fig. S16), resembling the control IgG-treated mice. Among all groups treated with

<!-- page 10 -->
control IgG, APS IgG, and APS IgG plus the anti-CCN2 monoclonal antibody, there were no differences in mouse body weight (Fig. S18). Taken together, these data help support a causative role for CCN2 in promoting APS-associated neointima formation.

# DISCUSSION

Our study employed an unbiased approach to understanding the underlying pathophysiology of APS vasculopathy, a clinical manifestation of APS that requires improved therapies in the clinic. Using scRNA-seq, we initially focused on dermal MVECs from patients with both livedo racemosa and a history of other microvascular complications. We identified high expression of CCN1 and CCN2 along with activation of their upstream regulator YAP1, likely driven by $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI} / \alpha$ anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ -mediated TLR4 activation; however, a role for LRP8/ApoER2 was not definitively ruled out and deserves further attention in subsequent studies. Furthermore, we validated increased expression of CCN1 and CCN2 at the protein level in APS skin, plasma, and kidneys. In addition, in vitro experiments demonstrated that CCN2 produced by dermal MVECs could promote the proliferation and migration of VSMCs in an EGFR-dependent fashion. Notably, blocking CCN2 abolished APS IgG-accelerated neointima formation in vivo. These findings provide insights into the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying APS vasculopathy and identify TLR4, YAP1, CCN2, EGFR, and possibly LRP8/ApoER2, as potential therapeutic targets.

In our study, we found higher levels of CCN1 and CCN2 in the plasma of APS patients compared with controls and observed a significantly positive correlation between CCN2 levels and kidney function. CCN1 and CCN2 have been extensively studied in other diseases where they have been linked to processes such as inflammation, fibrosis, and angiogenesis.[27-29] Furthermore, elevated plasma levels of CCN1 and CCN2 have been observed in conditions such as cancer, cardiovascular diseases, and autoimmune disorders,[29] suggesting their potential as diagnostic and prognostic markers. Our findings further emphasize the importance of investigating the role of CCN1 and CCN2 in APS-related vasculopathy and their potential utility as biomarkers. Key future directions on this front should include understanding why these proteins are increased in the blood and vessels of some, but not all, individuals with APS, which could potentially be related to the particulars of a patient's autoantibody profile or other concomitant genetic or acquired risk factors that we have yet to pinpoint. Furthermore, while APS vasculopathy is an important driver of APS-associated morbidity, especially organ dysfunction, and therefore merits significant study in its own right, a fascinating question is whether the axis described here might also have relevance to other manifestations of APS, including macrovascular thrombosis (either arterial or venous), cardiac valve dysfunction, and obstetric morbidity. Regarding thrombosis specifically, there is little evidence in the literature presently to suggest a connection to CCN1 or CCN2, but, at the same time, it isn't clear whether the question has ever been asked in a comprehensive manner. Finally, given that our study was anchored to skin biopsies of patients with striking, durable livedo racemosa, another interesting question is whether the YAP1-CCN2 axis might be bolstered in patients with livedo racemosa and/or livedoid vasculopathy who do not have an underlying diagnosis of APS; genetic studies of such individuals might be particularly enlightening.

<!-- page 11 -->
YAP1 has been extensively studied for its involvement in tissue homeostasis, regeneration, and various cancer-related processes. It regulates critical cellular functions, including proliferation, survival, invasiveness, metastasis, and therapy resistance.[23] In the context of vascular disorders, YAP1 has been implicated in angiogenesis in conditions like diabetic vasculopathy, ischemic heart disease, and dilated cardiomyopathy.[30,31] The proliferative properties of YAP1 have also been associated, in at least a few studies, with neointima formation.[32,33] As such, targeting YAP1 could be a potential therapeutic approach to modulate the proliferative signature observed in APS vasculopathy. It should also be noted that, in APS endothelial cells, there was evidence of a number of other enriched biological processes that have previously been linked to neointima formation, including interferon signaling,[34] complement activation,[35] and fatty acid metabolism.[36] Such pathways, which are likely to include additional relevant therapeutic targets, deserve attention in follow-up studies of APS vasculopathy.

Furthermore, the data presented here suggest that TLR4, previously implicated in the activation of APS endothelial cells, $^{37}$ is the more important $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}/$ anti- $\beta 2\mathrm{GPI}$ coreceptor for driving $CCN1$ and $CCN2$ expression. Of course, these data do not rule out a complementary role for another important APS-relevant coreceptor, ApoER2/LRP8, $^{38}$ in patients or in vivo models. As such, we would advocate for continuing to investigate both TLR4 and ApoER2 in studies like this one. We should also acknowledge that although circulating CCN1 and CCN2 were measured in a relatively large number of patients, the IgG samples selected for mechanistic studies were from individuals with strong autoantibody profiles and relatively severe manifestations of APS. Future studies should investigate whether IgG fractions from milder forms of APS activate the MVEC signaling pathways observed in this study.

We found that CCN2 exhibited higher levels in dermal MVECs and plasma samples than CCN1. This led us to prioritize CCN2 for subsequent in vitro experiments. Additionally, the existence of monoclonal antibodies—including investigational drugs such as pamrevlumab that are specifically designed to target CCN2 in patients—likely positions CCN2, at least in the short term, as the more advantageous candidate for therapeutic interventions. CCN2 plays a critical role in fibrosis, tissue repair, and angiogenesis, making it relevant to various fibrotic disorders. Although pamrevlumab failed to meet the primary endpoints for efficacy in the recent phase III clinical trials for patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis,[39] its safety and good tolerability were consistent in these trials. Given the significant involvement of CCN2 in VSMC proliferation and migration, as revealed by our study and the work of others,[40] anti-CCN2 monoclonal antibodies may hold potential as a novel and targeted therapeutic approach for individuals with APS vasculopathy.

CCN2 exerts its biological functions by interacting with an array of cell surface receptors. Our analysis of VSMCs identified several potential targets for CCN2, including low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1), epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), Erb-B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 4 (ERBB4), integrin $\alpha5$ (ITGA5), and integrin $\beta2$ (ITGB2). Among these receptors, integrins have long been known to play a crucial role as primary receptors for CCN2. $^{41}$ Additionally, CCN2 can also interact with other cell surface receptors such as LRP1, which facilitates the internalization and clearance of CCN2 from the

<!-- page 12 -->
extracellular space, $^{42,43}$ as well as EGFR and ERBB4, which belong to the EGF receptor family and promote cell division and proliferation once activated. $^{44}$ Based on the results of our in vitro knockdown studies, EGFR should be prioritized for additional characterization in APS patients and in vivo models.

While there are existing animal models that focus on thrombosis associated with APS,[26,45] the lack of a dedicated APS-related vasculopathy animal model highlights a gap in current research. Here, we developed a model that appears to mimic intima hyperplasia upon APS IgG administration. We also confirmed that inhibition of CCN2 with a monoclonal antibody is an effective approach for protecting against neointima formation in this new model, albeit with the caveat that its use was limited to mice treated with APS IgG. Therefore, it remains unclear whether reduced intima-media thickness in the treated mice is due to a general or an aPL-specific reduction in VSMC proliferation. Future work using similar models can continue to dissect cell-cell communication in vivo, as well as the potential role of YAP1 as a therapeutic target. In particular, lineage-specific knockout mice will be needed to prove that YAP1 and CCN2 of endothelial cell origin are indeed the primordial orchestrators of the observed vascular phenotypes.

In conclusion, our study sheds light on the molecular mechanisms underlying APS vasculopathy and identifies potential plasma biomarkers, CCN1 and CCN2, that may aid in the diagnosis and management of this condition. Furthermore, YAP1- and CCN2-mediated signaling may offer new opportunities for targeted therapeutic approaches. Our study lays the groundwork for future research aimed at enhancing the understanding and treatment of APS-related vascular complications, particularly those that are unresponsive to anticoagulation.
