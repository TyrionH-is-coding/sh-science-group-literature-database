## Abstract

Background: The diagnosis of antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) uses standard antibodies (lupus anticoagulant (LAC), anticardiolipin antibodies (aCL) IgG/IgM, β2-glycoprotein I antibodies (β2GPI) IgG/IgM), which results in some patients with ‘seronegative APS’ being overlooked. The diagnostic value of an extended antibody profile, including antiphosphatidylserine/prothrombin complex (aPS/PT) and aCL/β2GPI IgA, requires clarification.

Objective: This study aimed to define antibody heterogeneity in APS and determine the diagnostic and risk-stratification value of novel antibodies.

Methods: We retrospectively enrolled 2994 patients with clinical features suggestive of APS who underwent testing for criteria antibodies (LAC, aCL IgG/IgM and β 2 GPI IgG/IgM) and extended markers (aPS/PT IgG/IgM and aCL/β 2 GPI IgA). Principal component analysis (PCA) explored antibody reactivity patterns. Multivariate logistic regression identified independent diagnostic predictors, and receiver operating characteristic curve analysis evaluated diagnostic performance. Associations between antibodies and clinical parameters defined clinical phenotypes.

Results: PCA revealed three dimensions explaining 73.56% of variance: principal component (PC)1 (IgG/LAC axis; 41.42%), PC2 (specific IgM axis; 18.12%) and PC3 (specific IgA axis; 14.02%). aPS/PT-IgM was a strong independent predictor of clinical diagnosis (adjusted OR 1.147, 95% CI 1.129 to 1.165, p<0.001). The area under the curve for diagnosing triple-negative APS was 0.868 for aPS/PT-IgM. Standard criteria antibodies lost independent significance in the full model. Phenotypic analysis identified three subtypes: a ‘classical type’ (PC1-High) with prolonged coagulation times and complement consumption; an ‘inflammatory type’ (PC3-High) with elevated systemic inflammation markers without complement consumption; and a ‘restricted type’ (PC2-High) associated with anaemia.

Conclusion: Distinct antiphospholipid antibody heterogeneity exists, categorisable into ‘classical’, ‘inflammatory’ and ‘restricted’ subtypes. This study identifies aPS/PT-IgM as a strong independent serological marker associated with clinical status. Incorporating aPS/PT-IgM into routine testing could significantly reduce seronegative APS misdiagnosis.

## Introduction

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a systemic autoimmune disorder characterised by the persistent presence of circulating antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL), which increases the risk of arterial or venous thrombosis and obstetric complications such as recurrent miscarriage and fetal death. 1 The current diagnostic criteria for APS rely on a combination of clinical manifestations and laboratory tests, including the detection of lupus anticoagulant (LAC), anticardiolipin antibodies (aCL) and anti-β2-glycoprotein I antibodies (β 2 GPI), typically of the IgG or IgM isotypes, confirmed on at least two occasions. 2 4

However, in clinical practice, a subset of patients presents with typical clinical features of APS (eg, unexplained thrombosis or recurrent pregnancy loss) yet tests negative for the standard aPL profile. These patients are often classified as having ‘seronegative APS’ (SN-APS). 5 6 This diagnostic dilemma has prompted the exploration of non-criteria aPL antibodies to improve diagnostic sensitivity, particularly in patients with SN-APS. 7 9 Among these non-criteria antibodies, antibodies targeting the antiphosphatidylserine/prothrombin complex (aPS/PT) and the IgA isotypes of aCL and anti-β 2 GPI are considered promising potential biomarkers. 10 11

aPS/PT antibodies have garnered significant attention in recent years. Several studies suggest a strong association between aPS/PT antibodies and clinical events in APS, highlighting their potential diagnostic value. 12 14 Similarly, the clinical relevance of aCL-IgA and anti-β 2 GPI-IgA is increasingly recognised, especially in certain ethnic populations, despite not being included in the current classification criteria. 3 Nevertheless, the landscape of these non-criteria antibodies is complex. Their individual prevalence, interrelationships with each other and with standard antibodies, and their relative independent contributions to APS diagnosis remain incompletely elucidated. Most existing literature focuses on these markers in isolation, lacking a comprehensive analysis that integrates the full antibody profile—including both standard and novel antibodies—to provide a holistic view of the APS antibody repertoire.

Therefore, the primary objective of this large-scale cohort study is to conduct a comprehensive evaluation of both standard and extended aPL profiles. We aim to determine the positivity rates and intercorrelations of aPS/PT and IgA antibodies in a clinical cohort. Furthermore, using dimensionality reduction techniques, we seek to uncover the underlying structure of the antibody landscape, identify which antibodies serve as independent predictors of clinical status, and quantify their potential value in narrowing the diagnostic gap in SN-APS.

## Materials and methods

### Study population and design

This study systematically analysed the medical records of 2994 patients who visited Peking University Third Hospital between August 2024 and September 2025 and had complete aPL profiles, using a retrospective cohort analysis.

#### Inclusion criteria

Patients were included if they met the following criteria. (1) Physicians had ordered a comprehensive aPL profile for clinical indications suggestive of APS, such as unexplained thrombosis, adverse obstetric history or evaluation of systemic autoimmune diseases. (2) Complete and analysable results for the entire aPL profile were available. (3) Age was 18 years or older. (4) For patients not on long-term anticoagulation, at least one confirmatory aPL test was performed approximately 12 weeks after the initial positive test to establish antibody persistence, where clinically feasible and data were available.

#### Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded based on the following criteria. (1) Insufficient aPL data: absence of key results from the aPL profile or inability to confirm antibody persistence due to lack of follow-up testing.(2) Anticoagulant interference: patients receiving therapeutic anticoagulation (eg, warfarin, direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs)) or therapeutic-dose low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) at the time of blood draw for LAC testing. (3) Confounding medical conditions: presence of end-stage liver disease (Child-Pugh class C) or end-stage renal disease, as these conditions can cause significant coagulopathy and immune dysregulation. (4) Active immunomodulatory therapy: ongoing treatment with high-dose corticosteroids (≥20 mg prednisone equivalent per day), rituximab or other B-cell depleting agents within 3 months prior to aPL testing, due to their potential to suppress aPL titres.

#### Definition of triple-negative APS

For the purpose of this study, patients were classified as having ‘triple-negative APS’ if they fulfilled all the following criteria.

##### Clinical criteria

The presence of at least one of the following objective clinical events, as defined by the Sydney classification criteria. 15

##### Thrombosis

Objectively confirmed arterial, venous or small-vessel thrombosis in any tissue or organ, verified by imaging, Doppler studies or histopathology.

##### Morbidity in pregnancy

- One or more unexplained deaths of a morphologically normal foetus at or beyond the 10th week of gestation.

- One or more premature births of a morphologically normal neonate before the 34th week of gestation due to severe pre-eclampsia or placental insufficiency.

- Three or more unexplained consecutive spontaneous abortions before the 10th week of gestation, with exclusion of anatomical, hormonal and parental chromosomal causes.

### Serological criteria

Persistent negativity for all three standard criteria aPL, based on testing performed at the time of or following the clinical event.

- LAC: negative result.

- aCL and anti-β 2 GPI: negative for both IgG and IgM isotypes.

Ideally, negativity should be confirmed on two or more occasions at least 12 weeks apart. Acknowledging the constraints of the retrospective design, we included all patients who tested negative on initial testing and on any subsequent available testing, and we addressed the inability to confirm persistence in all cases as a limitation in the Discussion.

### Laboratory methods

#### Sample collection and preanalytical processing

Blood samples for LAC testing were collected in 3.2% (0.109 M) sodium citrate vacuum tubes (Becton Dickinson). After collection, tubes were inverted gently 3–5 times to ensure complete mixing with the anticoagulant. LAC testing was performed on fresh plasma within 4 hours of collection. Blood samples for aPL immunoassays were collected in serum separator tubes (Becton Dickinson), allowed to clot at room temperature for 30–60 min, and then centrifuged at 1500× g for 10 min. The resulting serum was aliquoted and stored at −80°C until batch analysis.

#### aPL testing

LAC testing was performed on the ACL TOP 700 fully automated coagulometer (Werfen) using the manufacturer’s reagents and protocols. The assay comprised a screening and a confirmation step for both the dilute Russell’s viper venom time (dRVVT; HemosIL dRVVT Screen & Confirm) and the silica clotting time (SCT; HemosIL Silica Clotting Time). The results were expressed as normalised ratios (patient clotting time/mean normal clotting time). An LAC result was considered positive if the normalised ratio for either assay was above the established cut-off and the screening-to-confirmation ratio demonstrated significant phospholipid dependence, in accordance with the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis guidelines.

Solid-phase assays for the quantitative determination of IgG/IgM/IgA isotypes of aCL, anti-β 2 GPI and IgG/IgM isotypes of aPS/PT antibodies were performed using chemiluminescence immunoassays (QUANTA Flash series, Inova Diagnostics) on the BIO-FLASH instrument (Werfen). All procedures were strictly followed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

#### Cut-off values and definition of positivity

The cut-off values for antibody positivity were defined based on the manufacturer’s recommendations, which are derived from the 99th percentile of a healthy reference population. The specific positivity thresholds for each assay were as follows: aCL IgG/IgM/IgA: ≥20 chemiluminescent units (CU); anti-β 2 GPI IgG/IgM/IgA: ≥20 CU; aPS/PT IgG/IgM: ≥20 CU. The result was considered positive if it met or exceeded the specified cut-off value.

### Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (V.4.3.0) and SPSS Statistics (V.26.0). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and are presented as median (IQR) or mean±SD, as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (percentages). All tests were two-sided, and a p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the baseline characteristics and antibody positivity rates of the study population. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was employed to calculate correlation coefficients (r s ) for assessing the relationships between antibody levels and coagulation parameters. To explore the underlying structure of the antibody profile, principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on all laboratory antibody variables. Components with eigenvalues >1 were retained, and varimax rotation was applied to facilitate interpretation.

Group comparisons (eg, among clusters identified by PCA) for continuous variables were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal-Wallis H test, depending on data distribution, with post hoc pairwise comparisons conducted using the least significant difference (LSD) test. Categorical variables were compared using the χ 2 test. Agreement between different antibody test results was evaluated using Cohen’s Kappa (κ) statistic. The diagnostic performance of various markers was assessed by generating receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calculating the area under the curve (AUC) with its 95% CI. The optimal cut-off value was determined by maximising the Youden’s index, and the corresponding sensitivity and specificity were reported. Finally, to identify independent predictors of clinical diagnosis, all significant laboratory variables were included in a multivariate logistic regression model. Results are expressed as adjusted ORs (aOR) with 95% CIs. The model’s discriminative ability and calibration were evaluated.

## Results

### Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 2994 participants were included in this study. The baseline characteristics are summarised as follows: the cohort was predominantly female (2927 (97.76%)), with 67 (2.24%) male participants. The mean age of the study population was 35.36±7.70 years (mean±SD).

#### Distribution of clinical diagnoses

The clinical diagnoses of the participants were categorised into four major groups, with the specific distribution detailed in table 1 . Autoimmune diseases and autoimmune-related laboratory/clinical manifestations constituted the most common category, affecting 804 patients (26.85%), primarily comprising APS (201 (6.71%)), positive antinuclear antibodies (232 (7.75%)) and positive autoantibodies (92 (3.07%)). Disorders related to thrombosis and coagulation abnormalities were present in 85 patients (2.84%), with coagulation dysfunction (68 (2.27%)) being the main condition. Diagnoses associated with pregnancy outcomes and status formed the largest group, encompassing 1700 patients (56.78%). Within this group, adverse obstetric history (952 (31.80%)) and embryonic arrest (316 (10.55%)) were the predominant presentations. Other diagnoses accounted for 405 cases (13.53%), including arthritis (65 (2.17%)).

#### Distribution of antiphospholipid-related antibodies

The detection results for various autoantibodies are presented in online supplemental table 1 . The overall positivity rates for aCL (IgG/IgM) and anti-β 2 GPI (IgG/IgM) were 10.49% (314/2994) and 4.98% (149/2994), respectively. The positivity rate for LAC was 5.01% (150/2994). Among the standard antibody isotypes, aCL-IgG (8.35%, 250/2994) was the most frequently detected, followed by aCL-IgM (2.67%, 80/2994), anti-β 2 GPI-IgG (3.54%, 106/2994) and anti-β 2 GPI-IgM (1.30%, 39/2994).

Our analysis revealed notably higher positivity rates for non-criteria antibodies. Specifically, aPS/PT-IgM was the most frequently detected antibody in the entire cohort, with a positivity rate of 26.25% (786/2994)—approximately five times higher than the rates of the standard criteria antibodies. In contrast, the positivity rate for aPS/PT-IgG was 2.37% (71/2994).

Of particular clinical significance were the positivity rates of the IgA isotypes. The rates for aCL-IgA (2.17%, 65/2994) and anti-β 2 GPI-IgA (1.30%, 39/2994) were comparable to those of their respective IgM counterparts (aCL-IgM: 2.67%; anti-β 2 GPI-IgM: 1.30%). This finding suggests that IgA antibodies may define a patient subgroup of similar size that has been previously under-recognised.

### Intercorrelations among antiphospholipid-related antibodies

Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess the relationships between the quantitative levels of all tested antibodies and functional coagulation assays. The results revealed a complex and highly significant network of associations (most correlations p<0.01, figure 1 ).

**Fig:** Figure 1 Correlation analysis among antiphospholipid-related antibodies. aCL, anticardiolipin antibody; aPS/PT, antiphosphatidylserine/prothrombin complex; dRVVT, dilute Russell’s viper venom time; ndRVVT, normalised ratios for dRVVT; nSCT, normalised ratio for SCT; SCT, silica clotting time; β 2 GPI, β2-glycoprotein I antibodies

A very strong correlation was observed between aCL-IgG and anti-β 2 GPI-IgG (r s =0.957, p<0.001). These two standard antibodies, measured on QUANTA Flash, where cardiolipin is complexed with β 2 GPI, target a shared β 2 GPI-dependent epitope, which explains their very high correlation in our cohort.

LAC assays showed strong positive correlations with IgG isotype antibodies. The normalised ratios for the SCT and dRVVT tests (nSCT-R and ndRVVT-R) suggested moderate to strong correlations with aCL-IgG (r s =0.417 and 0.538, respectively) and anti-β 2 GPI-IgG (r s =0.431 and 0.549, respectively). The most pronounced correlations were observed between aPS/PT-IgG and the LAC assays (r s =0.642 and 0.615, respectively; all p<0.001). This pattern suggests that higher titres of these IgG antibodies are strongly associated with more pronounced prolongation of in vitro phospholipid-dependent coagulation tests.

The IgA isotypes formed a distinct correlation cluster. aCL-IgA and anti-β 2 GPI-IgA exhibited a very strong correlation with each other (r s =0.925, p<0.001) but only low to moderate correlations with their IgG and IgM counterparts. This provides strong statistical evidence that IgA antibodies represent a unique and independent immunological pathway.

aPS/PT-IgM suggested a distinct correlation pattern. It showed the strongest correlation with the SCT screening time (r s =0.470, p<0.001) among the functional assay parameters, while its correlation with aPS/PT-IgG was only moderate (r s =0.327). This suggests that the IgM class of aPS/PT antibodies may interact with the coagulation cascade through a mechanism distinct from that of its IgG isotype.

### Diagnostic agreement among antiphospholipid-related antibodies

The agreement between different aPL markers was evaluated using Cohen’s Kappa statistic ( table 2 ). Agreement strength was interpreted as follows: <0.20, slight; 0.21–0.40, fair; 0.41–0.60, moderate; 0.61–0.80, substantial; and 0.81–1.00, almost perfect.

**Table Wrap:** Table 2 Analysis of diagnostic agreement among antiphospholipid-related antibodies

All pairwise comparisons showed statistically significant agreement (all p<0.01). However, the strength of agreement varied substantially. A moderate level of agreement was observed between the two core standard solid-phase assays. The agreement between aCL and anti-β 2 GPI (any isotype) was κ=0.419 (95% CI 0.361 to 0.476), indicating that while these assays are related, they are not interchangeable and capture overlapping yet distinct patient populations.

Critically, the agreement between the standard solid-phase assays (aCL, anti-β 2 GPI) and the functional LAC assay was markedly low, with Kappa values of κ=0.083 and κ=0.123, respectively. This signifies that a substantial proportion of patients testing positive by one methodological pathway (solid-phase immunoassay vs functional coagulation test) were negative by the other, justifying their complementary use in the current diagnostic criteria.

The novel antibodies exhibited slight agreement with the established criteria. The agreement of aPS/PT with aCL (κ=0.150), anti-β 2 GPI (κ=0.142) and LAC (κ=0.175) all fell within the ‘slight’ range. Similarly, IgA isotypes showed minimal agreement with LAC (κ=0.051) and aPS/PT (κ=0.053). This lack of agreement is not indicative of poor assay performance but rather provides strong evidence that these novel markers identify unique serological profiles missed by conventional testing.

### PCA reveals distinct dimensions of the aPL profile

To comprehensively assess the contributions of each aPL assay and reduce data dimensionality, we performed PCA on 10 laboratory parameters. Four principal components (PCs) with eigenvalues greater than one were extracted, collectively explaining 84.62% of the total variance (PC1: 41%, PC2: 18.09%, PC3: 14.34% and PC4: 11.19%). The loadings of each variable on the four components are detailed in online supplemental table 2 .

Based on the component loadings, we interpreted the clinical significance of each PC. The first PC (PC1), explaining the largest proportion of variance (41%), was identified as a ‘Global IgG-driven Prothrombotic Axis’. This component was predominantly driven by IgG isotype antibodies (aCL-IgG: 0.380; anti-β 2 GPI-IgG: 0.388 and aPS/PT-IgG: 0.385) and the functional LAC tests (nSCT-R: 0.373 and ndRVVT-R: 0.393). This strong association confirms the intrinsic link between IgG antibodies and phospholipid-dependent coagulation disturbances, representing the core pathophysiological mechanism of APS.

The second component (PC2), explaining 18.09% of the variance, represented a distinct ‘IgM-Specific Axis’. It was exclusively and strongly loaded by aCL-IgM (0.680) and anti-β 2 GPI-IgM (0.675), with negligible contributions from other antibodies. This indicates that the IgM antibody response constitutes a biologically and immunologically distinct pathway, independent of IgG-mediated processes.

The third component (PC3), explaining 14.34% of the variance, defined a unique ‘IgA-Specific Axis’. It was characterised almost entirely by high loadings from aCL-IgA (0.611) and anti-β 2 GPI-IgA (0.654). The segregation of IgA antibodies into an independent component provides strong statistical evidence that they constitute a third distinct dimension of the APS immune response, warranting independent clinical consideration.

A fourth component (PC4) was identified but not retained for subsequent clinical analysis. Although it explained an additional 11.19% of variance, it was characterised primarily by aCL-IgG and anti-β 2 GPI-IgG, representing a subset of the IgG response already captured by the more comprehensive PC1, which additionally included aPS/PT-IgG and LAC activity. Therefore, PC4 did not appear to represent a sufficiently distinct biological or clinical dimension to warrant a separate subtype.

We calculated the composite score coefficients and their corresponding weights for each antibody ( figure 2 ). Among all markers, aCL-IgA (14.26%), anti-β 2 GPI-IgA (13.82%), and anti-β 2 GPI-IgM (12.78%) carried the highest weights in constituting the composite score, highlighting their relative importance within the overall antibody profile.

Based on the scree plot’s elbow criterion and the clearer biological interpretability of the first three PCs (PC1: IgG/LAC axis; PC2: IgM axis and PC3: IgA axis), we retained the first three components for subsequent subtype and clinical correlation analyses, with a cumulative variance explanation of 73.56%. To validate the clinical relevance of these statistical constructs, we compared the distribution of clinical diagnoses across subgroups stratified by their dominant PC ( table 3 ).

Among cases dominated by PC1 (n=384), the prevalence of autoimmune diseases was significantly higher (49.2%, 189/384), particularly APS (17.7%, 68/384) and systemic lupus erythematosus (11.5%, 44/384). In contrast, the PC3-dominated group (n=1946), which constituted the majority of the cohort, was overwhelmingly associated with pregnancy outcomes and status abnormalities (63.3%, 1232/1946), such as adverse obstetric history (35%, 681/1946) and embryonic arrest (12.3%, 239/1946).

This analysis suggests that the latent antibody patterns uncovered by PCA are not mere statistical artefacts but are meaningfully associated with distinct clinical presentations. While these correlations are observational and do not establish causality, the distinct laboratory profiles suggest that these subgroups may represent different pathophysiological pathways.

### Association between PCA-defined subgroups and haematological parameters

To investigate the systemic haematological manifestations associated with distinct aPL profiles, we compared routine complete blood count parameters across the three subgroups defined by the dominant PCs (PC1-High, PC2-High and PC3-High) using one-way ANOVA with LSD post hoc testing. Only patients with complete haematological parameter data were included in this statistical analysis.

Significant differences were observed across multiple haematological indicators ( online supplemental table 4 ). Most notably, the PC3-High group (characterised by a dominant IgA antibody profile) exhibited consistent elevations in several parameters. Specifically, this group showed significantly higher white blood cell count (p<0.001), mean corpuscular haemoglobin (p=0.026), mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration (p<0.001), absolute neutrophil count (p<0.001) and systemic immune-inflammation index (p=0.039) compared with both the PC1-High and PC2-High groups.

Conversely, the PC2-High group (characterised by a dominant IgM antibody profile) had significantly lower levels of haemoglobin (p=0.002) and haematocrit (p=0.020) than the other two groups. Regarding thrombotic risk markers, both the PC2-High and PC3-High groups suggested significantly higher platelet counts (p=0.003) and plateletcrit (p=0.016) compared with the PC1-High group (characterised by dominant IgG antibody/LAC abnormalities).

### Association between PCA-defined subgroups and coagulation parameters

To further investigate the functional consequences of the distinct antibody profiles, we analysed standard coagulation parameters across the three PC-based subgroups. We observed significant and highly consistent differences ( online supplemental table 5 ). Only patients with complete coagulation parameter data were included in this analysis.

The most consistent and significant differences were associated with the PC1-High group (IgG/LAC-dominant phenotype). This group exhibited significantly prolonged prothrombin time (PT) (p<0.001), international normalised ratio (INR) (p<0.001), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) (p<0.001) and thrombin time (p<0.001) compared with both the PC2-High and PC3-High groups. In contrast, the PC3-High group (IgA-dominant phenotype) displayed a distinct profile, characterised by a significantly higher plasma fibrinogen level than the other two groups (p=0.006).

The observed prolongation of PT/INR specifically in the PC1-High (‘classical’) subgroup, in the absence of vitamin K antagonist use, warrants specific discussion. This phenomenon is most parsimoniously explained as an in vitro interference in the PT assay. High-titre IgG aPL (particularly those with specificity for prothrombin-containing complexes) is known to disrupt the assembly of the prothrombinase complex on phospholipid surfaces in a manner that can be detected not only in APTT-based LAC assays but occasionally in PT assays as well. Thus, this finding likely represents a laboratory epiphenomenon of the autoantibody profile in this subgroup rather than an indicator of a true hypocoagulable state in vivo. Clinicians should be aware of this potential interference when interpreting coagulation panels in such patients.

### Association between PCA-defined subgroups and levels of immunoglobulins, complement and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR)

To investigate the overall immune status underlying different antibody subtypes, we further analysed the differences in immunoglobulin and complement levels among the three subgroups. The results revealed highly significant differences and a clear pattern ( online supplemental table 6 ). Only patients with complete data for immunoglobulins, complement and ESR were included in this analysis.

The distribution of immunoglobulin levels was highly consistent with the antibody profile defining each group: the PC1-High group (IgG/LAC-dominant) showed a significantly higher level of IgG than the PC3-High group (p=0.003). The PC2-High group (IgM-dominant) had a significantly higher IgM level than both the PC1-High and PC3-High groups (p<0.001), which aligns perfectly with its core definition. The PC1-High group also exhibited a significantly higher IgA level than the other two groups (p<0.001), suggesting broad B-cell activation and hypergammaglobulinaemia in this subgroup.

Complement levels displayed a distinct pattern: the PC3-High group (IgA-dominant) had significantly higher levels of both complement C3 and C4 than the PC1-High and PC2-High groups (p<0.001).

Furthermore, significant differences were observed in the inflammatory marker ESR among the subtypes (p=0.002). Post hoc analysis indicated that the ESR in the PC1-High group was significantly higher than that in both the PC2-High and PC3-High groups.

### Multivariable logistic regression identifies aPS/PT-IgM and anti-β 2 GPI-IgA as independent predictors

To identify factors that could specifically distinguish patients with triple-negative APS (as defined in the Definition of triple-negative APS section, n=277) from other conditions (including non-APS with negative aPL), we performed a multivariate logistic regression analysis ( table 4 ). After adjusting for potential confounders, anti-β 2 GPI IgA and aPS/PT IgM emerged as strong independent predictors specifically for SN-APS. In contrast, standard diagnostic antibodies (aCL IgG, aCL IgM, anti-β 2 GPI IgG and anti-β 2 GPI IgM) did not show independent significance in the full model (all p>0.05), consistent with the seronegative status of this patient population. Functional LAC testing also did not reach significance. For each unit increase in aPS/PT IgM titre, the odds of SN-APS increased by 14.7% (aOR 1.147, 95% CI 1.129 to 1.165, p<0.001). Similarly, each unit increase in anti-β 2 GPI IgA was associated with a 23.5% increase in the odds of SN-APS (aOR 1.235, 95% CI 1.058 to 1.441, p=0.007). In contrast, aCL IgA was significantly associated with a decreased likelihood of being diagnosed with SN-APS (aOR 0.914, 95% CI 0.842 to 0.992, p=0.032).

**Table Wrap:** Table 4 Multivariate logistic regression analysis

ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the ability of aPL to discriminate patients with triple-negative APS from the combined control group (non-APS with negative aPL). As shown in figure 3 and online supplemental table 3 , aPS/PT-IgM suggested superior diagnostic performance, with an AUC of 0.868 (p<0.001). At the optimal cut-off value of 31.19 U/mL, it achieved a sensitivity of 96.2% and a specificity of 76.9%. In contrast, the diagnostic performance of traditional standard antibodies was substantially lower (AUC range: 0.415–0.752).

## Discussion

This comprehensive analysis of a large cohort delineates significant heterogeneity within the aPL profile and challenges the current diagnostic paradigm for APS. Our integrated findings consistently demonstrate that an extended antibody profile, particularly aPS/PT-IgM and anti-β 2 GPI-IgA, captures independent dimensions of disease autoimmunity not identified by standard criteria. 9 16

The most salient finding is the prominent role of aPS/PT-IgM as a powerful independent predictor. It exhibited the largest effect size in our multivariate model (aOR 1.147, p<0.001) and superior diagnostic accuracy (AUC=0.868). Critically, its predictive strength was consistent across clinical categories, supporting its broad relevance beyond the obstetric context enriched in our cohort. Biologically, as aPS/PT is a direct target in the coagulation cascade and IgM is a potent complement activator, aPS/PT-IgM likely represents a key pathogenic driver, not merely an associative marker. 12

Concurrently, anti-β 2 GPI-IgA emerged as another significant independent predictor. Its segregation into a unique PC suggests a distinct immunological pathway, justifying its targeted assessment in seronegative cases.

Through PCA, we defined three major serological dimensions, each linked to a distinct clinical-laboratory phenotype: the ‘classical’ subtype (PC1-High), driven by IgG antibodies and LAC activity, characterised by prolonged coagulation times and evidence of complement consumption, aligning with the classic APS pathophysiology. The ‘inflammatory’ subtype (PC3-High) is associated with IgA antibodies and marked by elevated systemic inflammation (eg, fibrinogen and leucocytes) and paradoxically high complement levels, suggesting a prothrombotic state fuelled by innate immune activation. The ‘restricted’ subtype (PC2-High), defined by a confined IgM response and associated with haematological features like anaemia, indicates a potentially distinct mechanistic pathway.

Our findings must be interpreted considering several limitations. First, the retrospective design limited our ability to confirm antibody persistence in all cases and to fully account for potential confounders like immunomodulatory therapy. Second, the use of a clinical diagnosis reference standard, while reflecting real-world practice, necessitates that our results be viewed as demonstrating a strong association meriting prospective validation in rigorously classified cohorts. Third, the generalisability of our subtyping framework requires external validation. Our cohort was predominantly female (97.76%) and of Chinese ethnicity, with a high proportion of obstetric morbidity. The small male subgroup (n=67) precluded meaningful sex-stratified analysis. Future prospective studies involving multiethnic cohorts with balanced sex distribution and a broader spectrum of clinical manifestations (including more thrombotic events) are essential to confirm the universality of these subtypes and the diagnostic value of aPS/PT-IgM across the entire APS disease spectrum. Finally, the exclusion of patients on therapeutic anticoagulation may have introduced a selection bias, and the clinical translation of the subtle laboratory differences between subtypes requires longitudinal investigation.

Despite these limitations, our study advocates for a refined diagnostic approach. We propose a stepped strategy: integrating aPS/PT-IgM testing as a first-line adjunct for clinically suspected but seronegative patients, followed by anti-β 2 GPI-IgA assessment. This could significantly reduce the diagnostic gap in SN-APS. Moreover, the identification of pathophysiologically distinct subtypes lays a crucial foundation for future research into stratified, mechanism-targeted therapies, advancing the field towards precision medicine in APS.

## Conclusion

This study establishes significant immunological heterogeneity in APS, defining three distinct serological and clinical subtypes: a classical type (IgG/LAC-driven), an inflammatory type (innate immunity-driven, associated with IgA) and a restricted type (IgM-confined). We identify aPS/PT-IgM as a powerful independent diagnostic predictor. These findings highlight the insufficiency of the current limited antibody panel and demonstrate that transitioning to a multiplex model incorporating aPS/PT-IgM is critical to narrowing the diagnostic gap in SN-APS and paving the way for precision management.
