## Abstract

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an autoimmune disorder characterized by recurrent life-threatening blood clots and pregnancy complications in individuals with antiphospholipid antibodies. Among these antibodies, those targeting the plasma glycoprotein β 2 -glycoprotein I (β 2 GPI) hold particular clinical significance. Despite extensive research, controversies persist regarding the structure of β 2 GPI, which has substantial implications for understanding autoantibody reactivity and APS development. This article critically examines recent advancements in the structural biology of β 2 GPI and its relevance to the recognition of antiphospholipid antibodies. Additionally, it introduces a new structure-based theory to explain how autoantibodies interact with β 2 GPI and the functional consequence of this interaction. Finally, it identifies potential areas for future research that could enhance approaches to diagnosing and treating APS.

## Introduction

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a systemic autoimmune disorder characterized by recurrent thromboembolic events and pregnancy morbidity in patients with antiphospholipid antibodies. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 In 1990, the phospholipid-binding protein β 2 -glycoprotein I (β 2 GPI), also known as apolipoprotein H, was identified as a key target of antiphospholipid antibodies. 5 , 6 , 7 In 2006, immunoglobulin G and immunoglobulin M targeting β 2 GPI were included in the classification criteria, becoming a pivotal part of APS diagnosis. 8 , 9 More recently, antibodies targeting a specific domain of β 2 GPI, domain I (DI), have been found to strongly correlate with thrombotic recurrence 10 , 11 and to be a procoagulant when tested in animals. 12 , 13 , 14 In 2010, it was suggested that these anti-DI antibodies, especially those recognizing the immunodominant epitope G40-R43, 10 , 15 , 16 bind to β 2 GPI only when β 2 GPI is bound to anionic surfaces such as anionic phospholipids but not in solution because of drastic conformational changes caused by phospholipids binding, shifting the shape of β 2 GPI from circular to open. 17 The pathogenesis of APS was thus explained by assuming that β 2 GPI almost always circulates in the blood in a circular form that is not recognized by autoantibodies, but becomes available to autoantibodies upon lipid binding as it becomes open. 1 , 2 , 17 , 18 The generation of pathogenic autoantibodies against DI was proposed to arise from a disturbance in this circular form. 1 , 2 , 17 , 18 Any mechanism, such as naturally occurring mutations, posttranslational modifications, and binding to pathogens, might contribute to APS pathogenicity by altering this equilibrium. 1 , 2 , 17 , 18 However, in 2020, this model was challenged by showing that β 2 GPI adopts an open form in solution, not circular. 19 In 2023, a new model was proposed based on structural, biophysical, and biochemical data suggesting that what drives autoantibody recognition is not the opening of β 2 GPI molecule but rather the binding of the elongated form to anionic surfaces such as anionic phospholipids, as this increases the local density of the protein, which, in accordance with the law of mass action, can bind autoantibodies more abundantly. 20 This review provides a historical perspective of the evolving structural biology β 2 GPI and discusses future directions of APS research.

## 1960-1990: discovery of β 2 GPI and definition of its chemical composition

β 2 GPI was discovered in 1961, 21 and its complete amino acid sequence was reported for the first time in 1984. 22 The form circulating in plasma contains 326 amino acids ( Figure 1 A). These 326 amino acids are organized into 5 domains, DI, DII, DIII, DIV, and DV, connected by 4 short linkers ( Figure 1 B). DI through DIV are canonical complement control protein (CCP) domains, also known as Sushi domains. They consist of ∼60 amino acids and, typically, contain 2 disulfide bonds. The C-terminal DV, however, is larger, containing 82 amino acids and 3 disulfide bonds. Due to its larger size and the additional disulfide bond, DV is often referred to as a CCP-like domain.

This accounts for a theoretical molecular weight of 36 254.60 Da when all cysteine residues are reduced and 36 232.60 Da when 11 disulfide bonds are formed. (B) The 326 amino acids are organized into 5 domains, connected end-to-end by 4 short linkers: TPRV for DI/DII, APII for DII/DIII, REVK for DIII/DIV, and KAS for DIV/DV. Established N-glycosylation at positions 143, 164, 174, and 234 (depicted as triangles) accounts for ∼15 kDa, giving rise to an ∼50-kDa protein.

In addition to 326 amino acids, β 2 GPI undergoes extensive posttranslational modification. It contains 4 established N-linked glycosylation sites (N143, N164, N174, and N234; Figure 1 B) and potentially a few more, including N-linked (N88) and O-linked (T14 and T130) sites. 23 Glycosylation adds ∼15 kDa to the protein mass (∼36 kDa) and causes β 2 GPI to run as an ∼50-kDa smeared band during gel electrophoresis analysis. 20 , 24 The width of the band generally reflects the glycosidic heterogeneity within a protein preparation. Other posttranslational modifications include nitrosylation of tyrosine residues 25 and reduction and nitrosylation of cysteine residues. 26 Though others are likely to exist. Unlike glycosylation, these modifications are dynamic and usually occur extracellularly due to exposure to different redox environments and interaction with thiol-isomerase enzymes. 24 , 27

## 1999: the J-elongated form

In 1999, Bouma et al and Schwarzenbacher et al simultaneously reported 2 similar (root mean square deviation = 0.448 Å) structures of β 2 GPI, which was purified from human plasma. The structures were solved by X-ray crystallography at 2.70 Å (1QUB.pdb) 28 and 2.87 Å (1C1Z.pdb) 29 resolution, respectively. For the first time, the crystal structures revealed the arrangement of the 5 domains and the spatial relationships between them. In the structures, β 2 GPI adopted an elongated shape with the potential for flexibility between the domains ( Figure 2 A). The first 2 domains were arranged almost vertically, with some bending between DII and DIII. However, a very significant bending occurred between DIII and DIV, giving rise to a characteristic J-elongated form ( Figure 2 B). Four branched glycosylations were found in the electron density at residues N143, N164, and N174 in DIII and N234 in DIV. Notably, both papers emphasized the positive charge of DI and DV ( Figure 2 C). In contrast, a large patch of negative charge was noted in DIV, which some studies suggest may be involved in protein-protein interactions. 30 , 31 , 32 All 22 cysteine residues were engaged in 11 disulfide bonds. No unpaired cysteine residues were found. Together with strong biochemical data, 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 the 2 crystal structures supported a model in which DV anchors to phospholipids projecting the other 4 domains toward the vascular lumen, being available for antiphospholipid antibodies binding.

This structural work marked a significant milestone for the APS field. It provided insight into the protein's structural architecture and revealed potential interactions with both membranes and antiphospholipid antibodies. However, some limitations left lingering questions. In the 1QUB.pdb structure, there was a gap in the phospholipid-binding loop between residues 312 and 318. This gap can be explained by the loop’s flexibility. However, it is also possible that the loop was cleaved by a protease such as plasmin. In fact, plasmin cleavage is known to occur in vivo at position 317 to 318 and results in a β 2 GPI variant bearing different functional properties. 33 , 37 Moreover, residue 311 was a threonine (T) instead of the conserved serine (S). Unlike 1QUB.pdb, in the 1C1Z.pdb structure, the loop was modeled based on the electron density, and residue 311 was a serine. However, residue 247 was a leucine (L) instead of a valine (V). The V247L variant of β 2 GPI increases the risk of developing anti-β 2 GPI antibodies. 38 Therefore, both 1QUB.pdb and 1C1Z.pdb may not accurately represent the structure of the most common circulating form of β 2 GPI. In addition, crystals formed at high salt concentrations, and β 2 GPI was purified using perchloric acid as a purification reagent. These are harsh oxidizing conditions that may have disrupted the native conformation in the human plasma due to chemical modifications of glycans and aminoacidic residues, which are difficult to assign at the resolution at which the structures were solved. Therefore, these ambiguities, combined with the observation that autoantibodies targeting DI prefer phospholipid-bound β 2 GPI over soluble β 2 GPI, fueled extensive speculation about the nature of the J-elongated form, opening the door to alternative theories to explain the modest autoantibody reactivity toward soluble β 2 GPI.

## 2002: the S-twisted conformation

In 2002, mindful of the limitations of X-ray studies, Hammel et al conducted studies on human and bovine β 2 GPI using small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS). 39 Unlike X-ray crystallography, this technique enables studying proteins' structure in solution under conditions more closely resembling physiological pH and salt concentrations. However, unlike crystallography, this method provides low-resolution information regarding a protein shape and, unfortunately, cannot provide detailed structural information.

In their studies, Hammel et al measured a SAXS profile characteristic of an elongated protein and confirmed that β 2 GPI particles are well-behaved, indicative of β 2 GPI existing almost exclusively as a monomer in solution. The radius of gyration was 4.3 nm, and the maximal particle length was 13.6 nm, which is close to the 14.3 nm obtained from the X-ray crystal structures ( Figure 3 A). In fact, the J-elongated form in which the 4 N-glycosylations are modeled, provided a good fit to the experimental SAXS data, especially given the uncertainty associated with modeling the glycans. Nonetheless, guided by their ab initio deconvolution of the buffer-subtracted SAXS profiles, Hammel at al proposed that a dramatic rotation of 130° rotation between DII and DIII was needed to slighly improve the fit ( Figure 3 B). This rotation, however, resulted in an S-twisted conformation, which was significantly different from the J-elongated form observed via X-ray crystallography. Importantly, to satisfy the low-resolution models reconstructed from the SAXS data, Hammel et al had to force the N-glycosylations linked to residue 143 in DIII to adopt an unusual fully stretched configuration, which extended to DI, potentially covering epitopes recognized by pathogenic autoantibodies (ie, G40-R43).

A model was proposed based on these experiments to explain why anti-DI antibodies prefer phospholipid-bound β 2 GPI over soluble β 2 GPI. The model suggested that β 2 GPI adopts an S-twisted form in solution, making epitopes in DI cryptic, but becomes J-shaped when bound to phospholipids. There was, however, no biochemical demonstration that the N-glycosylation at position 143 blocks autoantibody binding to soluble β 2 GPI. Moreover, there were no structural analyses of β 2 GPI bound to anionic surfaces, which left questions about the robustness of these structural interpretations.

## 2010: the O-circular conformation

Despite these limitations and due to the lack of additional structural data at that time, the S to J model was adopted by the community and dominated the field for 8 years until a new twist emerged in the story. In 2010, Ağar et al reported that monomeric β 2 GPI does not have an S- or J-shape but instead takes on an O-circular conformation 17 ( Figure 4 A). Using negative stain electron microscopy (EM), they proposed that the O-circular form is characterized by an intramolecular interaction between DI and DV ( Figure 4 B). They further suggested contacts between specific residues in DI (K19, R39, and R43) and DV (K305 and K317) based on mass spectrometry analyses. Finally, they showed that the O-closed form could be converted to a J-elongated open form by changing salt and pH conditions or incubating the O-form with the mouse monoclonal anti-DI antibody 3B7. This led to the conclusion that (1) β 2 GPI takes on a closed form in blood; (2) the closed form is dynamic, in equilibrium with the J-elongated open form; and (3) anti-DI antibodies specifically recognize the open form. A new model was, therefore, proposed to explain how pathogenic anti-DI antibodies interact with β 2 GPI. β 2 GPI circulates in the blood in a circular form, with hidden epitopes in DI becoming available upon binding to negatively charged phospholipids due to the transition to the J-elongated form. Because EM studies did not capture the S-twisted form, it was also concluded that this is an intermediate state between O and J shapes.

A few years later, in 2018, Buchholz et al reached similar conclusions using atomic force microscopy (AFM) 40 instead of EM. They described β 2 GPI purified from plasma as mostly adopting a closed conformation ( Figure 4 C). In addition, they showed that chemical acetylation of lysine residues using acetic acid N-hydroxysuccinimide ester disrupted the O-circular form in favor of the J-elongated form, concluding that lysine residues control the conformational dynamics of β 2 GPI. A follow-up study by Buchholz et al analyzed differences between oxidized and reduced β 2 GPI by AFM, 42 , concluding that enzymatic reduction of β 2 GPI by thioredoxin results in a slightly higher proportion of open conformation. In between these 2 studies, in 2020, Szabo et al also reported EM images of closed and open forms obtained with β 2 GPI purified from human plasma 41 ( Figure 4 D). Together, these findings solidified the concept of a monomeric O-circular conformation as the dominant species in the blood that is incapable of reacting with anti-DI antibodies.

Despite its conceptual elegance, this model had some important caveats. First, the O-circular form was obtained with β 2 GPI bound to surfaces, not in solution. Second, immobilizing particles into surfaces needed for EM and AFM imaging can result in orientation bias and even surface-induced aggregation/oligomerization phenomena. As a result, the structure of β 2 GPI might appear as if it were circular, even if it is not. This might occur, for example, if the protein adopts a preferred orientation that makes it more likely to be viewed from a particular orientation. Alternatively, the O-circular form may arise from 2 or more β 2 GPI molecules coming together, forming a circular multimeric structure. Given the dimensions of the particles reported in the EM and AFM studies are much larger than what is predicted by a monomeric O-circular form, this latter interpretation seems the most likely. This interpretation would also account for (1) both DI and DV are positively charged, thus expecting to undergo electrostatic repulsion; (2) the interdomain linkers are relatively short, limiting the degree of bending between 2 adjacent domains; and (3) Ağar et al reported difficulty in capturing the interaction between β 2 GPI and anionic phospholipid vesicles due to agglutination, 17 perhaps hinting at a mechanism for surface-mediated oligomerization. Therefore, while EM and AFM studies documented the potential for an O-closed form, it is unlikely that this form exists in solution as a monomer. Instead, these experiments may have captured oligomerization/aggregation events induced by surfaces. The conformation of β 2 GPI in human plasma, therefore, remained mysterious.

## 2020-2024: revitalization of the J-elongated form and implications for APS

Over the past 5 years, studies from our laboratory have extensively examined the solution structure of β 2 GPI using both traditional and modern approaches ( Figure 5 ). 19 , 20 , 24 By combining structural (X-ray crystallography, SAXS, and single-particle EM), biophysical (single-molecule spectroscopy, surface plasmon resonance, and microfluidic diffusional sizing), and mutational studies, it was concluded that (1) the monomeric form of β 2 GPI adopts a J-elongated conformation in human plasma that is primed for phospholipid and autoantibody recognition; (2) the elongated form does not undergo circularization even when subjected to extreme pH and ionic strength conditions, indicating that open and closed forms are not in equilibrium, and this transition may require more drastic events; (3) the J-elongated form is flexible and capable of domain bending/rotation supporting the presence of structural intermediates, that, however, interconvert very rapidly, in the sub-millisecond timescale; (4) anti-DI antibodies recognizing the pathogenic epitope G40-R43 bind weakly to β 2 GPI to despite its elongated conformation. Moreover, their binding affinity is not affected by the presence of glycosylation nor deletion of the linker connecting DII with DIII, indicating that potential structural intermediates, like the S-twisted form, do not alter autoantibody recognition of epitope G40-R43, although it may affect the recognition of other epitopes. Finally, (5) loss of phospholipid binding is enough to prevent the complexation of β 2 GPI with anti-DI antibodies on lipid membranes.

A new mechanistic view emerges from these findings ( Figure 6 A). Soluble β 2 GPI adopts a flexible, elongated form rather than a circular one. Binding to phospholipids via DV does not forcefully open its structure or relocate the glycosylations, but rather increases the protein’s local concentration, which stimulates autoantibody recognition and downstream effects.

**Fig:** Figure 6. Proposed new model of autoantibody recognition in APS based on antigenic density and spatial organization of the immune complex. (A) Soluble β 2 GPI adopts a flexible, elongated form. Autoantibodies can bind to the soluble form but with low affinity (μM). When β 2 GPI binds to phospholipids via DV, the protein’s local concentration increases, stimulating autoantibody recognition (nM affinity) due to antibody bivalency/avidity. Multiple β 2 GPI-antibodies complexes form a phospholipid-bound cluster. Low affinity is expected for a monomeric β 2 GPI bound to phospholipids; however, this event may not be frequent, because several β 2 GPI monomers are likely to be found in the proximity of the antibody. (B) Antibodies binding to different epitopes of the same domain, such as DI, result in a different spatial organization of the complex. For example, the arrangement of DV in the antibody with epitope 1 is further apart but closer together in the antibody targeting epitope 2. This could lead to potentially engaging different receptors or complement recruitment.

Two key aspects emerge from this new model. First, in addition to DI, DV takes centerstage, thus becoming a major driver of APS pathology. Second, DIV surfaces as a potential new key player, fulfilling both structural and functional roles. Specifically, while DV is necessary for the interaction of β 2 GPI with phospholipids, 24 , 33 , 35 , 36 cell receptors, 43 , 44 , 45 and anionic surfaces, 24 , 46 DIV may enhance pathogenic autoantibody functions by optimally orienting phospholipid-bound β 2 GPI and facilitating critical interactions with soluble and phospholipid-bound proteins. A handful of studies have suggested that DIV could be involved in unique protein-protein interactions, 30 , 31 , 32 particularly with the platelet receptor glycoprotein Ib-IX-V. 31 However, these findings remain preliminary, underscoring the need for targeted structural investigations to confirm these interactions.

Another important consequence of this model is that it offers a clear framework for future mechanistic investigations into the “two-hit” hypothesis in APS. 47 , 48 This hypothesis posits that antiphospholipid antibodies represent the first hit, which is essential but insufficient to instigate vascular events. A secondary event (such as trauma, infection, inflammation, or pregnancy) acts as the second hit, crucial for tipping the hemostatic balance toward a proinflammatory and procoagulant state. According to this new model, in healthy individuals without autoantibodies, secondary events are expected to enhance the binding of monomeric β 2 GPI to anionic surfaces such as cell membranes displaying suitable negatively charged phospholipids, but also extracellular DNA, potentially offering a protective mechanism by reducing coagulation activation, curbing inflammation, and promoting the clearance of damaged cells, debris, and toxins. 49 , 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 , 56 Conversely, the presence of autoantibodies could lead to stable surface-bound dimers or multimers of β 2 GPI, transforming its role from protective to pathogenic by increasing cell signaling and complement activation. 1 , 2 , 57 , 58

A compelling clinical scenario that seems to exemplify such a gain-of-function pathogenic switch driven by autoantibodies is pregnancy. Over the years, research has suggested a crucial role of β 2 GPI in enhancing embryonic fitness. β 2 GPI is not only expressed by fetal trophoblasts 59 but also shows a significant affinity for these cells. This is evidenced by studies in which fluorescently labeled β 2 GPI, when injected into rodents, accumulates extensively on trophoblasts as well as on uterine and decidual endothelial cells at the implantation sites cells. 60 In addition, while individuals and animals lacking β 2 GPI do not exhibit a distinct phenotype, the observed percentage of β 2 GPI null homozygous offspring significantly deviates from expected Mendelian ratios. 61 Thus, while monomeric β 2 GPI is likely beneficial for pregnancy, the multimerization of β 2 GPI induced by autoantibodies poses a risk, potentially leading to fetal loss.

## 2024: future

So, where do we go from here? Despite significant advancements, several critical questions persist.

The unique affinity of β 2 GPI for trophoblasts, as well as uterine and decidual endothelial cells, suggests the presence of specific cellular determinants that attract β 2 GPI. Although these determinants remain unidentified, uncovering them is crucial for understanding the unknown mechanisms that govern the cellular localization of β 2 GPI.

How changes in glycan composition in β 2 GPI and, particularly, attenuated sialylation documented in APS patients, 62 contribute to APS pathogenesis remains unknown. Although glycosylations do not block access to the epitope G40-R43 in DI, they may be functionally relevant if autoantibodies bind to epitopes closer to DIII, such as DII and DIV, in which the glycosylations cluster. Moreover, glycosylations may help retain β 2 GPI in the J-elongated form by alleviating electrostatic repulsion between DI and DV. This leads to the testable prediction that deglycosylated β 2 GPI or alternative glycosylated forms of β 2 GPI lacking negatively charged sialic acid are even more elongated than normally glycosylated β 2 GPI, which might have functional consequences on how β 2 GPI packs into membranes and, in turn, how it is recognized by autoantibodies and how it signals via receptor cross-linking. Understanding these changes could provide valuable insights for devising strategies to block the pathogenic effects of autoantibodies.

The heterogeneity of autoantibodies and their correlation with clinical manifestations in APS necessitates further research. It is particularly important to understand why APS patients who exhibit comparable autoantibody profiles can have markedly different clinical outcomes. For instance, while the presence of anti-DI antibodies signals an increased risk of thrombosis, 11 these antibodies do not reliably distinguish between arterial or venous thromboses nor between obstetric and nonobstetric APS cases. In this context, recent findings from our laboratory indicate that thrombotic APS patients harbor at least 2 distinct populations of anti-DI antibodies, each recognizing unique epitopes located on opposite sides of the R39 residue in the DI. 63 We hypothesize that the engagement of different epitopes within the same domain may alter the spatial arrangement of the resulting immune complex ( Figure 6 B). This alteration could lead to varied functional outcomes by differentially interacting with platelet receptors, 31 , 43 , 64 , 65 endothelial cell receptors, 66 , 67 neutrophils, 68 , 69 and complement. 57 , 58 Although these cellular components cooperate in the pathogenesis of APS, they play different roles in arterial and vascular environments. Therefore, we anticipate functional differences among the various anti-DI antibody populations, which may further elucidate the diverse clinical manifestations of APS.

The clinical significance of redox modifications needs to be better understood and requires further exploration. Redox modifications of redox-sensitive amino acids have been detected in APS patients, who are known to experience increased levels of oxidative stress. 70 , 71 Such modifications might alter the reactivity of monomeric β 2 GPI toward anionic surfaces, 72 changing the partition between soluble vs surface-bound β 2 GPI. This could affect autoantibody pathogenic potential and phospholipid-dependent physiological functions of β 2 GPI. Examples of this altered activity have recently emerged in the literature. 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 For example, our research has highlighted that disruption of the disulfide bond between C288 and C326 in DV results in impaired phospholipid-binding capabilities due to structural modifications, which, in turn, prevents antiphospholipid antibody-dependent β 2 GPI accumulation on lipid membranes. 24 Detailed atomic characterization of DV interactions with model membranes revealed that the loop from residues 311 to 318 penetrates the lipid bilayer, with the integrity of the C288-C326 disulfide bond being crucial for the loop's conformation. The linearization of this loop, a consequence of the disulfide bond's rupture, is hypothesized to contribute to the diminished binding affinity. Moreover, recent studies led by Krilis et al 25 have demonstrated that nitration of tyrosine residues DIV (Y218) and DV (Y275 and Y309), observed at elevated levels in certain APS patient subsets, significantly inhibits β 2 GPI's interaction with lipid peroxidation products, thus reducing its antithrombotic activity. Identifying the mechanisms by which redox modifications influence β 2 GPI's structure and function, as well as developing reliable methods for detecting levels of β 2 GPI redox species in APS patients, represent critical research avenues with profound clinical implications.

Another important area of investigation is identifying factors and mechanisms that increase the propensity of β 2 GPI to form multimeric structures, either as soluble oligomers or insoluble protein fibrils. Interestingly, 1 biochemical study reported that β 2 GPI could form fibrils when misfolded 73 and that this misfolded species can be immunogenic. 73 We recently reported that rupture of the disulfide bond C32-C60 in DI results in a protein with reduced thermodynamic stability, resulting in easier unfolding compared to the wild type. 24 Furthermore, recent experiments using hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry have indicated that although minor local conformational changes in the 21 to 27 segment of β 2 GPI occur after the initial contact with anionic membranes, more significant global changes take place over extended periods, particularly when β 2 GPI is bound to cardiolipin, 74 hinting at surface-dependent and time-dependent conformational changes. In this context, clarifying the origin of the O-circular form revealed by EM and AFM will be extremely valuable as it could lead to new insights into these oligomerization/aggregation mechanisms or reveal new mechanisms leading to circularization. In fact, it is possible that monomeric β 2 GPI could become circular, yet this circularization would require the rupture of several disulfide bonds or noncanonical pairing of disulfide bonds, which could be mediated by thiol isomerases. Indeed, β 2 GPI is a substrate of thiol isomerases, 26 , 27 , 75 most notably protein disulfide isomerase. 75 However, it remains unclear how thiol isomerases interact with β 2 GPI.

Finally, structural studies are urgently needed to better understand how full-length β 2 GPI interacts with autoantibodies, different types of membranes, and other partners at the atomic level, for which there is no experimental data. In fact, since 1999, our published structures in 2020 using plasma purified (6V06.pdb) and recombinantly made β 2 GPI proteins (6V08.pdb and 6V09.pdb) remain the only major advancement in terms of structural biology of β 2 GPI. More recently, Klenotic and Yu deposited 4 structures in the Protein Data Bank (6XSD.pdb, 6XST.pdb, 7JIK.pdb and 7KG4.pdb) ( Table 1 ). While 3 of them replicate the results of the 1999 and 2020 structures, one (7JIK.pdb) features a change in the sequence 39 RGGMR 43 in DI to 39 SEGVG, 43 which is intriguing in the context of autoantibody recognition. However, a manuscript detailing this finding is yet to be published.

## Conclusions

Over the past 3 decades, substantial advancements in structural and biophysical methods have made the study of β 2 GPI much more tractable. In recent years, X-ray crystallography, SAXS, single-particle EM, surface plasmon resonance, single-molecule spectroscopy, and microfluidic diffusional sizing have been used to assemble a constellation of clinical and experimental findings into a working theory to explain how antiphospholid antibodies bind β 2 GPI. This system portrays 1 of many instances in which structural dynamics play a critical role in the pathogenesis of a devastating disease, underscoring the importance of developing and applying orthogonal techniques to solve complex biological questions. Continuing this work is crucial because it has the potential to significantly expedite the development of diagnostic tests that can identify specific subpopulations of pathogenic autoantibodies and facilitate the creation of new therapeutics to counter their harmful effects. This structure-based approach aims to overcome existing limitations stemming from the broad suppression of the coagulation cascade and the immune system.
