## Query: What are the current diagnostic criteria for Antiphospholipid Syndrome (APS)?

Question: What are the current diagnostic criteria for Antiphospholipid Syndrome (APS)?

The current classification criteria for Antiphospholipid Syndrome (APS) require the presence of IgG or IgM isotypes of anticardiolipin (aCL) antibodies, anti-β2 glycoprotein I (anti-β2GPI) antibodies, and/or lupus anticoagulant (LA) to satisfy the laboratory disease definition (pmid_37933818 lines 0-43). Specifically, "IgG/M isotypes of anti-β2 GPI, anti-CL, and/or lupus anticoagulant (LA) positivity, comprise the laboratory criteria for classification of the antiphospholipid syndrome (APS)" (pmid_33957795 lines 43-55). Non-criteria aPL, such as antibodies to phosphatidylserine–prothrombin complex (aPS/PT), are not part of the Sydney criteria but are associated with thrombosis and considered for risk stratification (pmid_33957795 lines 43-55).

## Query: What is the recommended first-line anticoagulation therapy for APS patients with thrombosis?

Question: What is the recommended first-line anticoagulation therapy for APS patients with thrombosis?

For APS patients with thrombosis, vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) such as warfarin are the recommended first-line anticoagulation therapy (pmid_35867933 lines 41-53). Standard-intensity oral anticoagulation targeting an INR of 2.0–3.0 is advised for venous thromboembolism (pmid_33614687 lines 111-113). Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) remain controversial, particularly in high-risk triple-positive patients, due to an increased risk of recurrent arterial thrombosis and stroke (pmid_33614687 lines 111-113; pmid_35867933 lines 41-53). Current guidelines suggest DOACs only as an alternative after shared decision-making, primarily for patients unsuitable for VKAs (pmid_40879863 lines 37-57).

## Query: How does complement activation contribute to pregnancy loss in APS?

Question: How does complement activation contribute to pregnancy loss in APS?

Complement activation in antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) contributes to pregnancy loss through placental insufficiency and abnormal fetal brain development. In APS, complement deposition occurs in the villous tree, the vascular bed for feto-maternal exchange, and is associated with placental insufficiency resulting in intrauterine growth restriction (pmid_25245499 lines 169-177). This activation leads to increased oxidative stress, decreased levels of proangiogenic factors VEGF and PlGF, and reduced placental and fetal weight (pmid_25245499 lines 157-165). Additionally, complement-activating aPL antibodies cross the placenta and fetal blood-brain barrier, causing C3 deposition in fetal brains linked to abnormal cortical development (pmid_25245499 lines 157-165). These placental and fetal abnormalities are precursors to pregnancy loss (pmid_25245499 lines 157-165).

## Query: What are the differences between primary and secondary APS in terms of clinical presentation?

Question: What are the differences between primary and secondary APS in terms of clinical presentation?

According to the available context, there is no difference in clinical presentation between primary and secondary antiphospholipid syndrome (APS). Both forms are "characterised by thrombosis, thrombocytopenia and recurrent foetal loss" (pmid_17223652 lines 41-57). The distinction lies solely in the presence of an underlying disease: primary APS has no evidence of any other underlying disease, while secondary APS is mainly associated with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) (pmid_17223652 lines 41-57). No further comparative clinical data are provided.

## Query: What is the role of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) in APS management?

Question: What is the role of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) in APS management?

Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have a limited and conditional role in antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) management. In high-risk patients, particularly those with triple-positive APS or arterial thrombosis, DOACs—especially rivaroxaban—are contraindicated due to significantly higher recurrent thrombosis rates compared to vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) (pmid_30405613 lines 35-47; pmid_36299971 lines 41-53). International guidelines generally contraindicate DOACs in these populations (pmid_34414220 lines 117-125). However, for venous non-triple APS, some guidelines (EULAR, BSH, ISTH) allow DOAC use after shared decision-making in specific situations, such as poor VKA control (TiTR <60%) or patient preference against INR monitoring (pmid_34414220 lines 117-125). Data on apixaban, dabigatran, and edoxaban remain limited (pmid_34414220 lines 51-63).

## Query: What are the main treatment strategies for APS?

Question: What are the main treatment strategies for APS?

The cornerstone of APS treatment is anticoagulation with vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) for thrombotic APS, while obstetric APS is managed with low-dose aspirin (LDA) and heparin (pmid_31168416 lines 39-57). Biologics are reserved for refractory or catastrophic APS (CAPS). Rituximab effectively controls noncriteria manifestations without decreasing aPL titers (pmid_37275894 lines 163-173). Daratumumab is recommended for patients unresponsive to anticoagulation and standard immunosuppression, potentially requiring long-term belimumab maintenance (pmid_37275894 lines 143-163). Anti-TNF-α therapy (adalimumab or certolizumab) is used in refractory obstetric APS, achieving "positive obstetric results... in 70% of patients" (pmid_37275894 lines 143-163).

## Query: What are the major clinical manifestations of APS?

Question: What are the major clinical manifestations of APS?

The major clinical manifestations of antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) are recurrent arterial or venous thrombosis and pregnancy morbidity (pmid_31168416 lines 39-57). Thrombosis and pregnancy loss are the core clinical events associated with the condition (pmid_33348782 lines 131-143). Diagnosis requires at least one clinical criterion (such as thrombosis or obstetric complications) combined with persistent antiphospholipid antibodies (pmid_35317658 lines 55-69). Management goals focus on preventing first or recurrent thrombotic or obstetric complications, including recurrent pregnancy losses (pmid_31168416 lines 39-57).
